TL;DR: Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told *Face the Nation* moderator Margaret Brennan that the Trump administration has been in touch with Nvidia's temporary leadership and legal team but is still awaiting answers on potential illicit chip sales to China, following a 10-day deadline issued a week prior. Bessent also defended the resilience of U.S. banks amid recent tech outages and outlined the administration's push to onshore semiconductor manufacturing and tighten AI chip export controls.

Banking Resilience & Market Volatility

  • Bank Tech Outages: Brennan noted that recent tech outages at major banks caused thousands of customers to panic and withdraw cash. Bessent reassured Americans that banks are the "most resilient companies in the U.S. economy" and have been at the forefront of cybersecurity for decades.
    • He compared recent glitches—such as the one at JP Morgan—to a vaccine, explaining that security patches may cause temporary disruptions ("your arm may hurt") while increasing long-term immunity.
  • Drivers of the Market Sell-Off: Addressing recent stock market anxiety and AI-related sell-offs, Bessent attributed the downturn to a combination of factors:
    • Political Shifts: He stated the sell-off began as Democrats moved ahead in the polls and accelerated after election day, arguing that the Democratic position on slowing down the AI build-out spooked investors.
    • Monetary Policy: He noted that "very hawkish remarks from the Federal Reserve" and a substantial tightening in financial conditions over the past few months have led many to worry the Fed is making a policy mistake.

U.S. AI Policy, Data Centers & On-Shoring

  • Offshore Data Center Ban: In a clip from a 90-minute Friday press conference, President Donald Trump stated: "We're not allowing data centers to go into other countries cuz China will take all of them... And we're going to put tremendous penalties on companies that try and do it."
    • Bessent said he had not yet spoken directly with Trump about the policy, but explained the president wants to prevent offshore technology transfers.
    • Clarifying the scope, Bessent confirmed the policy targets American companies rather than reversing U.S. encouragement of data centers in Gulf allies like the United Arab Emirates.
  • Semiconductor On-Shoring: Bessent highlighted a strategic vulnerability in the U.S. chip industry, noting that 97% of high-end chips are currently made in Taiwan.
    • He pointed to large fabs being built by TSMC in Arizona as part of the administration's effort to bring manufacturing back to the U.S.
  • Rebuilding Public Trust: Responding to a *Wall Street Journal* editorial board argument that restricting offshore data centers could give Chinese competitors an edge, Bessent argued the AI industry has done a "terrible job of explaining themselves to the American people" and must rebuild trust with local communities to enable a larger domestic build-out.

Nvidia Investigation & Leadership Fallout

  • Awaiting Answers: Brennan noted that multiple congressional committees, federal prosecutors, and regulators are probing Nvidia's role in sending advanced AI chips to China, and reminded Bessent that he gave Nvidia a 10-day deadline seven days prior to explain a potential "culture of non-compliance."
    • Bessent confirmed the administration has been in touch with Nvidia's temporary management, lead directors, and lawyers, but has not yet received the answers it needs.
    • He emphasized that while Nvidia's CFO is now in charge and the company remains "fantastically profitable" with near-unlimited global demand, it "behooves Nvidia to move quickly" to clarify whether there was a "promiscuous attitude" toward compliance.
  • Tracing "Handshake Deals": Addressing reports that Nvidia's deals were often based on handshakes without a paper trail, Bessent stated that for deals of that size, "there's no such thing as an untraceable handshake deal," pointing to bills of lading and money movement.
  • Jensen Huang's Status: When Brennan asked about President Trump's comment that former CEO Jensen Huang should get his job back—contrasting with Bessent's earlier call for an outsider—Bessent noted that "Jensen is on the outside now." He added that the administration needs to see "where the buck stops" and whether non-compliance was driven by lower-level actors or went to the top.
  • Alice Huang & Mega Speed: Brennan raised reports linking Alice Huang—who ran Singapore-based Mega Speed—to Chinese security. Bessent called the focus on one person's relationship "kind of a canard" compared to the broader question of Nvidia's compliance culture, assuming Huang did not know she was an alleged spy.

China Chip Exports & National Security Controls

  • Mega Speed Scrutiny: Brennan cited Republican Intelligence Committee Chair Sen. Tom Cotton's frustration that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick allegedly ignored repeated letters about Mega Speed. Bessent said investigators will determine if Mega Speed was a pass-through front company, noting that Nvidia removed Mega Speed from its vendor/sales list in August 2025.
  • Remote Access Rule: While Treasury and other departments have pushed to blacklist Mega Speed, Bessent highlighted a "remote access rule" enacted the previous week. Brennan noted the rule tightens licensing for countries including Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil to prevent restricted entities from accessing chips via leased capacity.
  • Commerce Department Approvals: Brennan cited documents released by the Republican chair of the House Foreign Affairs Committee showing the Commerce Department approved the sale of more than 800,000 Nvidia and AMD AI chips to China, including to two companies linked to the Chinese military.
    • Bessent defended the approvals by stating President Trump has drawn a "very hard line at the H200s" (the chip performance threshold) and noted that one of the approved companies, Alibaba, is a general social media and online platform that merely does business with the Chinese military rather than being the military itself.

TL;DR

Jeff Yass, co-founder of Susquehanna International Group and vice chairman of the Cato Institute, argues that Social Security is an unsustainable "Ponzi scheme" set to run dry in 2032, and proposes privatizing it by routing payroll taxes into individual, low-cost index fund accounts ("Trump accounts"). Under Yass's plan, workers receive stock market returns with a government-backed guarantee—funded by a potential tax on the top 2%—ensuring they never receive less than traditional Treasury bill returns.

The Current Social Security Crisis

  • No Actual Trust Fund: Yass explains that despite political rhetoric—such as Al Gore's "lockbox" or Franklin D. Roosevelt's original framing—the Social Security trust fund has never existed as a vaulted savings account.
    • Weeks after Social Security launched, Roosevelt's Treasury Secretary admitted the funds were spent immediately in exchange for a political promise.
    • In the 1960 or 1962 Supreme Court case *Fleming v. Nestor*, the court ruled that taxpayers have no legal ownership of Social Security money, only a promise that Congress can alter or revoke at any time.
  • The 2032 Cliff: Yass notes that Social Security faces an annual shortfall of $400 billion to $500 billion and will run dry in 2032.
    • If Congress takes no action, benefits will automatically drop by 22%, leaving recipients with only 78% of their promised payouts, with further declines over time.
  • Political Gridlock: Asked by host Amir why Washington has ignored these dates for decades, Yass states that the only traditional fixes—cutting benefits or raising taxes—are wildly unpopular.
    • Yass warns that the most politically plausible fix in 2032—eliminating the current $183,000 income cap on Social Security taxes—would raise about $250 billion a year (covering just over half the shortfall) while constituting the "biggest tax increase in American history" and turning Social Security into a welfare program.

The Privatization Plan ("Trump Accounts")

  • Account Structure: Yass proposes redirecting the 12.2% to 12.4% payroll tax (paid by individuals and employers) into private accounts with the worker's name on them, utilizing the "Trump account" infrastructure already developed with Brad Gerstner.
    • Funds would be invested in zero-cost or low-cost index funds.
    • At retirement (around age 65), the worker receives the money—which Yass favors distributing as a lump sum—and any remaining funds pass to their heirs upon death.
  • Overcoming Past Objections: Yass notes that Bill Clinton nearly passed a 2% private account carve-out in 1998 before dropping it amid the Monica Lewinsky scandal and opposition from figures like Chuck Schumer, and George Bush's later attempt failed for similar reasons: high Wall Street fees and market volatility.
    • Eliminating Fees: While mutual funds charged around 100 basis points in 1998, modern index funds cost practically zero.
    • Eliminating Downside Risk: Yass points out that in U.S. history, no 30-to-40-year investment in the stock market—even starting on the worst day in 1929—has ever underperformed Treasury bills. To guarantee this, any shortfall below Treasury bill returns would be covered by an extra tax on the top 2% of earners, giving workers a "free roll" on the stock market.
  • Wealth Creation: Yass contrasts a typical 67-year-old retiring today with roughly $300,000 in Social Security benefits against what they would have had—well over $1 million—if this plan had been enacted 45 years ago.

Transition Costs and Opt-Out Savings

  • Honoring Current Promises: When Amir asks who pays for current retirees during a transition, Yass states their benefits would be paid out of general Treasury funds without a 22% cut.
  • Reducing the $4–$5 Trillion Liability: While honoring the existing system carries a $4 trillion to $5 trillion obligation, Yass cites studies showing that if 40-year-olds (who have already paid in $100,000 to $200,000) are given the option to forfeit their past benefits in exchange for putting all future contributions into the stock market, enough would opt out to save the Treasury $1 trillion to $2 trillion.

Political and Philosophical Impact

  • Making Every Laborer a Capitalist: Referencing Yass's 2019 writing, Amir asks how this changes the country. Yass argues it ends the 200-year ideological battle between Marxism/collectivism and free-market capitalism.
    • A Walmart worker making $60,000 to $80,000 a year would eventually generate as much income from their investments as from their labor, turning them into a hybrid "laborer-capitalist" aligned with economic growth.
  • Partisan Divide: Yass anticipates a "war of misinformation," predicting that because Donald Trump supports the idea, the political left will oppose it. He argues only Marxists would oppose workers owning the means of production. Amir agrees that very few people could oppose the plan in good faith and expresses hope that listeners will support it to make the U.S. more prosperous over the next decade.

TL;DR

Zipline has reached a $21.5 billion valuation following a $1.5 billion funding round (bringing total funding to over $3 billion) as it scales autonomous drone delivery across the US. Anchored by massive new partnerships with Uber Eats (targeting 1 million deliveries per day) and Walmart (expanding to 300 Supercenters), CEO Keller Rinaudo Cliffton explains how Zipline is tearing up its operating plan to scale 3x to 5x faster over the next two years while navigating the hard realities of hardware manufacturing.

Explosive Growth, Valuation, and Landmark Partnerships

  • Valuation & Funding: Host Molly O'Shea noted that Zipline has tripled or quadrupled its valuation since last year to $21.5 billion, raising a $1.5 billion round and surpassing $3 billion in total funding.
  • Uber Eats Partnership: Announced the day of the interview, Zipline is partnering with Uber Eats to scale to 1 million deliveries per day—roughly 25% of Uber Eats' total US deliveries.
    • The contract will take Zipline to almost every US metro, putting ~100 million homes within 10-minute instant delivery of 1.2 million merchants.
  • Walmart National Expansion: Announced the prior week, Zipline is expanding its multi-year Walmart partnership from 22 Supercenters to 300 Supercenters in phase one, with further scaling based on performance.
    • Customers gain autonomous delivery access to 90,000 of the 100,000 SKUs in a Supercenter within minutes.
  • Rewriting the Operating Plan: Zipline originally built its multi-year operating plan to reach 1 million deliveries per day; it now has 3+ strategic partners each trying to buy more than 1 million deliveries per day.
    • The company tore up its 3-year plan to figure out how to scale 3x to 5x faster over the next two years.

Scaling Operations and "Approximating Teleportation"

  • Rapid Metro & Factory Expansion: Zipline is growing 10% to 15% week-over-week, and its factory has quadrupled in size.
    • Metro Rollout: Zipline is at massive scale in Dallas (operating ~25 charging sites that continue to grow week-over-week), launched Houston a month ago, and just launched Austin.
  • Delivery Speed & Precision: Average delivery time once food is prepared is 2 to 5 minutes.
    • Approximating Teleportation: Beyond speed, the system is quiet and innocuous, delivering to any GPS coordinate—including backyards, Little League practices, mountain peaks, and a 7th-floor college dorm pool party.
  • Fleet Command: Formerly called the Remote Operating Command Center (ROCC), Fleet Command displays live streams of charging sites and a sky map of active Zips.
    • Visited by figures like Secretary Pete Buttigieg, it proves to skeptics that autonomous delivery is operating at scale—though Cliffton noted humanity is still only seeing the first 0.01% of this infrastructure layer.
    • O'Shea and Cliffton discussed making Fleet Command available as a public live cam on Zipline's website.

Global Competition: US vs. Chinese Tech Stacks

  • A Decade Outside the US: Founded in 2013, Zipline spent its first 10 years operating in obscurity outside the US (starting in Rwanda) because flying beyond visual line of sight autonomously was illegal in the US.
    • This forced Zipline to log hundreds of millions of commercial autonomous miles and build reliable hardware, maintenance, and fleet command before scaling domestically two years ago.
  • Trade, Not Aid: Cliffton argued that developing economies (such as in Africa) are tired of low-quality, free NGO services and want high-paying jobs, foreign direct investment, robotics, and entrepreneurship to leapfrog into the future.
  • The Geopolitical Tech Race: Developing nations—where the vast majority of the world's population lives—are choosing whether to build their AI and robotics infrastructure on a US tech stack or a Chinese tech stack, representing a massive, under-discussed global competition.

The Hardware Reality Check and Vertical Integration

  • Full-Stack Advantage: Unlike legacy systems that patch together software from 20 different vendors (e.g., air traffic control towers), Zipline is vertically integrated like SpaceX, making it easier to secure, innovate, and fix hardware bottlenecks.
  • The VC Pivot to Capex: For 12 to 13 years, Capex was a "four-letter word" in venture capital as investors chased SaaS and social media. Now, VCs believe AI will "one-shot" SaaS companies, making hardware and infrastructure the only defensible moats.
    • O'Shea noted that the era of fast, cheap capital for high-margin software is resetting, forcing companies that want to be sovereign through-and-through to carefully weigh Capex and what to build in-house.
  • Hardware Takes Time: Cliffton warned that investors underestimate the timelines for scaling factories, supplier industrialization engineering, end-of-line testing, permitting, and regulation.
    • He pointed to Tesla (15 years to meaningful scale), SpaceX (20 years), and Zipline (over 10 years), noting that even the best-funded AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic are discovering how hard it is to build physical data centers.
  • Culture: Despite its valuation, Cliffton rejected the "Power Law winner" label, describing Zipline as "scrappy," "frugal," "paranoid," and operating like "special forces" against giant competitors investing billions.
