TL;DR: AssemblyAI handles over 120 million weekly voice conversations (four times YouTube's daily volume) and 100 million daily API calls, driven by a 100x TAM expansion from coding agents and breakthroughs in context-aware voice models.

Volume, Scale, and Market Growth

  • Massive Usage Metrics: Weekly Conversations: AssemblyAI handles over 120 million voice conversations and 2 million hours of voice on a peak week, representing a growth of over 800% in the last 3 years.
  • API and Developer Adoption: Daily Volume: Generates nearly 100 million API calls a day across a user base of slightly over 1 million developers, with 40% of those developers signing up in the past year alone.
  • YouTube Comparison: Scale Benchmark: Voice volume on the platform reached over 4x the daily volume of YouTube as of December of the prior year.

Macro Trends Driving Voice AI

  • Model Capability Improvements: Enhanced Voice Models: Models are becoming faster, lower cost, more accurate, and more controllable, including newly launched models capable of receiving environmental context (e.g., recognizing a McDonald's order while filtering out background noise like screaming kids).
  • Broader AI Infrastructure Ecosystem: Supporting Technologies: The maturation of reasoning models, vector databases, and other modal AI tools allows builders to integrate voice into full-stack applications.
  • Coding Agents Expanding the TAM: 100x TAM Growth: Coding agents (such as Lovable, Claude Code, Cursor, and Replit) enable non-traditional engineering customers—such as small businesses and lawn care chains—to build directly on API infrastructure using agents.

Product Architecture, Differentiation, and Internal Operations

  • Infrastructure Focus: Core Layer: AssemblyAI is 100% focused on voice AI infrastructure rather than applications, providing model weights, high-scale cross-region inference, orchestration, developer experience, and specialized models for healthcare, contact centers, drive-thrus, and note-taking.
  • Engineering Efficiency: Customer-Centric R&D: Emphasizes deep collaboration between researchers and customers to understand real-world application errors, spending roughly half its engineering effort on scalable, cross-cloud infrastructure.
  • AI-Native Internal Workflow: Lean Operations: Maintains a team of roughly 80 people while utilizing custom internal AI agents (such as founder Dylan Fox’s personal "Dylan claw" for slides and meeting notes) and Claude to rebuild and deploy the entire company website on Vercel.

Emerging Form Factors, Robotics, and Localization

  • Multi-Modal Interface Integration: Beyond Screens: Voice is establishing itself as a reliable form of data capture that will add a complementary dimension to computing rather than entirely replacing keyboards, mice, or screens.
  • Humanoid Robot Challenges: Disambiguation Issues: While companies use AssemblyAI APIs for humanoid robots (such as Figure or Neo) and field-service tech apps (like Sero AI), robots still face challenges in multi-speaker environments where background voices jumble audio input without visual or contextual cues.
  • Language and Policy Alignment: Localization Complexity: Deploying single models supporting up to 20 real-time languages requires navigating cultural and policy nuances per language, relying on global experts, native speakers, and customer feedback.

Predictions & Forward Calls

  • Consumer Hardware Integration: Dylan Fox predicts that over the next 12 to 36 months, voice will become a core dimension in consumer electronics, toys, games, and hardware, freeing users from screen addiction and enabling passive computing experiences.
  • On-Device Models: AssemblyAI is actively working on on-device models designed to run on phones and low-powered hardware like TV remote controls.

TL;DR

Decagon has scaled rapidly by building enterprise AI agents that follow complex business processes, shifting 90% of its inference to fine-tuned open-source models to optimize latency and task-specific accuracy. Co-founders Jesse Zhang and Ashwin Sreenivas explain how the company operates as a model factory, leveraging a product-driven "glass box" approach and rapid go-to-market execution to serve some of the world's largest enterprises.

Decagon's Open-Source & Model Strategy

  • Shift to Open Source: Jesse Zhang notes that Decagon moved ~90% of its workflow to open-source models primarily to optimize latency for voice agents and gain control over smaller models through fine-tuning.
  • The "Dumber" Model Advantage: Ashwin Sreenivas challenges the false trade-off between expensive smart models and cheap dumber models, stating that fine-tuned smaller models outperform large state-of-the-art models on specific tasks while being faster and cheaper.
  • The Model Factory Approach: Decagon Labs acts as a continuous model factory, training net-new models and deprecating old ones as the AI frontier advances, compressing the time from new model release to task-specific deployment.
  • Dual-Model Architecture: While open-source fine-tuned models handle 90% of primary conversational flows, closed-source frontier models are still utilized for 10% of auxiliary, broad, exploratory tasks (such as "Duet Autopilot" which reviews millions of conversations).

Product Evolution, "Glass Box" Approach, and Forward-Deployed Engineering

  • From Customer Support to AI Concierge: Jesse Zhang shares that Decagon expanded its vision beyond customer support (inbound sales, proactive operational workflows) because the core competency is building agents that follow complex business processes rather than just answering tickets.
  • Duet and Duet Autopilot: Decagon developed "Duet"—a secondary agent that automatically writes Agent Operating Procedures (AOPs), handles system integrations, builds tests, and monitors conversations. "Duet Autopilot" reviews live interactions and drafts continuous improvements.
  • The FDE Trap: Ashwin Sreenivas warns that forward-deployed engineers (FDEs) are only necessary early on to discover new AI workflows, but companies must quickly productize those workflows rather than running a glorified consulting shop.
  • Glass Box vs. Black Box: Decagon differentiates itself from competitors like Sierra by providing a "glass box" approach where enterprises retain control and visibility, allowing customers to spin up new workflows rapidly (e.g., a customer deploying 7 new journeys in a month compared to 3 in a year with previous vendors).

Go-To-Market and Enterprise Sales Execution

  • Founder-Led Intensity: Jesse Zhang estimates spending ~80% of his time on sales and go-to-market execution, focusing heavily on deal speed, breaking down massive enterprise deployments into manageable piecemeal wins, and staying close to the feedback loop.
  • Navigating Enterprise Bureaucracy: Decagon maps out model risk governance, testing, and compliance procedures in granular detail during early meetings, helping regulated financial services and airline customers move from initial meeting to 100% deployment.
  • Early GTM Team: The company built a strong early sales team consisting of nontraditional backgrounds and Ivy League athletes, utilizing top-down pressure from boards and CEOs wanting to adopt AI quickly.

Economic Impacts and the Future of Work

  • Jevons Paradox in Customer Support: Ashwin Sreenivas highlights that when customer support costs drop by 30%, companies do not automatically lay off 60% of staff; instead, latent demand drives them to make support more accessible across every page, resulting in more total support activity.
  • AI Kills Jobs, Not Careers: Jesse Zhang asserts that AI will eliminate mundane, repeatable tasks that "should not be done by humans," but will not eliminate careers because people will pivot to higher-value and revenue-generating human-centric activities.

TL;DR

Google DeepMind introduced the Gemini Robotics 2 model suite, bringing whole-body intelligence, fine-grained dexterity (supporting hands with up to 20 joints per hand), and multi-robot collaboration to general-purpose robotics. The Embodied Reasoning (ER) model is available via AI Studio and Gemini Enterprise Agents Platform, while action models are accessible through a trusted tester program and deep partnerships.

1. Core Model Suite: Gemini Robotics 2

  • Whole-Body Intelligence: Carolina Parada noted that the new models understand the spatial position of every part of the robot simultaneously, enabling complex tasks like organizing a garage or cleaning closets.
  • Dexterity Upgrades: Jie Tan explained that the system handles contact-rich, high-degree-of-freedom manipulation (such as packing lunch, screwing in lightbulbs, and tying trash bags).
  • Multi-Robot Collaboration: Carolina Parada highlighted that robots can now recognize their own limits and coordinate with other specialized robots in parallel to accelerate shared tasks.

2. Research Arc and Technological Evolution

  • From Reinforcement Learning to VLAs: Carolina Parada traced the team's history from pioneering reinforcement learning and sim-to-real transfer for locomotion, to integrating LLMs/VLMs for semantics, transformers for data-driven robotics, and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) foundation models.
  • Frontier Model Leverage: Stuart Bowers emphasized that instead of solving robotics from scratch, the team latched physical tasks onto the world-understanding capabilities of large frontier models like Gemini, treating robot actions as an added modality.
  • The Data Pyramid Challenge: Jie Tan outlined the data bottleneck: while teleoperation provides high-quality action signals, it does not scale; egocentric human video lacks actuation/force labels; and the lack of an "internet of physical interaction" remains the primary open obstacle to physical AGI.

3. Deployment Strategy & Real-World Horizons

  • Broad Tasks Over Brittle Niches: Carolina Parada and Stuart Bowers stressed that training on narrow tasks creates brittle policies; the strategy is to build for general-purpose tasks first, even though initial commercial deployments will focus on semi-structured industrial and retail environments rather than messy homes.
  • The Partner Ecosystem: Stuart Bowers noted that all robot hardware used in the lab is built by deep external partners, with whom Google DeepMind works closely to co-accelerate AI capabilities, safety, and hardware readiness.
  • Timeline to Daily Life: Jie Tan revised his estimate for when general-purpose robots will enter daily life down from 10 years (estimated two years ago) to 5 to 10 years.

4. Safety, Benchmarking, and Accessibility

  • Proactive Safety & Clarification: Carolina Parada stated that ER 2.0 is the team's safest model yet, featuring the ability to detect human proximity, evaluate task completion likelihood, and proactively ask clarifying questions when given ambiguous instructions.
  • Asimov Benchmark: Carolina Parada announced the open-sourcing of Asimov Agentic, a new safety benchmark featuring real-world examples to test semantic physical understanding and common sense.
  • API & Platform Availability: Carolina Parada confirmed that Embodied Reasoning 2 is accessible via AI Studio and coming soon to Gemini Enterprise Agents Platform, while action models are available via API, partner integrations, and a trusted tester waitlist for on-device fine-uning.

Patrick Collison, co-founder and CEO of Stripe, discusses the changing landscape of software development and entrepreneurship in the age of AI, reflecting on Stripe's origin story, the cost of dropping out of college, and why current Stripe data shows that startup formation and growth rates are at historic highs.

Learning, Cognitive Cache, and AI

  • Cognitive L1 Cache vs. AI Agents: Patrick Collison uses a computing analogy to describe human knowledge, comparing factual lookups to L1 cache versus RAM or network requests.
    • *Speed:* Even with advanced AI agents, neuronal lookups in the human brain are significantly faster than generating or reading prompts.
    • *Evidence:* High-end companies and labs continue to place an enormous premium on cognitive ability.
  • Personal Tooling Preferences: Collison still writes his own code and essays rather than relying on automated writing models.
    • *Communication:* He views writing as a fundamental interpersonal medium and feels LLMs are still deficient at reasoning sensibly within multi-dimensional realities.
    • *Adoption:* He reports having sent zero pre-written suggestions from tools like Gmail or WhatsApp in his life.

Dropping Out of College and Startup Urgency

  • Dual College Dropout: Collison dropped out of MIT twice—first after his freshman semester to start a company with Harj Taggar, and later after another year to start Stripe.
  • Cost of Leaving School: He notes that parents often fear dropping out will permanently damage a student's reputation, but in his experience, the cost is *de minimis* and nobody cares.
  • Reassessing Urgency:
    • *Past Mindset:* He felt a false sense of urgency in his youth, driven by a speed-running mentality and a fear that Silicon Valley opportunities were fleeting.
    • *Reality:* Silicon Valley consistently maintains a surplus of opportunities across decades.

The Stripe Origin Story and Development Playbook

  • Inception at Startup School: Stripe was conceived in 2009 after Patrick and John Collison attended Startup School in Berkeley and discussed the idea while walking back from sushi in Potrero Hill, reasoning "we might as well because it probably won't be that hard."
  • Early Obstacles:
    • *Credibility:* Fintech did not exist as a sector, and two young founders trying to build a financial services business looked like "squirrels in a trench coat trying to masquerade as a real business." Banks viewed them with extreme skepticism.
    • *Grounding:* The business succeeded because it was grounded in a concrete, universally felt user pain point.
  • The Long Pre-Launch Phase:
    • *Timeline:* Stripe spent two years building before its public launch in September 2011 (starting full-time in summer 2010).
    • *Production Feedback:* Despite a long internal build, they secured their first production user (Ross Buché at 28 North) in January 2010, just two months into coding, and added customers monthly in private beta to maintain real-world feedback loops.

Macro Data and Enterprise Adoption of Startups

  • Surge in New Businesses:
    • *Stripe Data:* The number of new businesses starting on Stripe is currently growing at nearly 2x year-over-year, the largest relative jump ever recorded outside of the early COVID-19 pandemic inflection (which saw ~50% growth).
    • *Performance:* The median business is performing better than a year ago, with improved probabilities of reaching $1M, $5M, and $10M revenue thresholds and declining time-to-revenue for Atlas-incorporated companies.
  • Enterprise Willingness to Buy:
    • *Risk Reversal:* Traditional corporate buyers (CIOs/CTOs) have a "terror of being left behind with archaic and antiquated ways of operating," making them far more willing to buy from early-stage startups than in past eras.
  • Decentralization vs. Hegemony:
    • *AI Concentration:* Despite fears of AI centralizing the economy around a small number of dominant labs, Stripe's transaction data shows intense retooling and startup formation across the board, pointing toward a decentralized world with thousands of winners.
