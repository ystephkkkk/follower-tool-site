TL;DR

Tibo Sottiaux, Head of Core Products & Platform at OpenAI, discusses the engineering behind Codex, its open-source and multi-provider architecture, and how AI is fundamentally transforming the software development lifecycle, code reviews, and maintenance within OpenAI.

Codex Architecture: Rust, Open Source, and Model Optionality

  • Language Choice (Rust): Codex was built in Rust from first principles to ensure a robust, secure, and scalable agent core. Tibo Sottiaux notes that while internal models weren't initially optimized for Rust, the static verification at compile time proved invaluable for agents.
  • Open Source Strategy: The Codex CLI, SDK, and app server were released as open source to co-design with the community, lower onboarding friction for new hires, and stay aligned with the evolving role of code.
      • Downsides: Tibo highlights the tax of handling an "tsunami of random stuff" (low-quality contributions) and the sting of competitors copying features built in the open before release.
  • Model Agnosticism: Codex is intentionally not tied exclusively to OpenAI models; users can plug in models from other providers. Tibo explains this offers users necessary optionality and prevents lock-in.

Execution Environments and the Harness-Model Dynamic

  • Sandboxing & Local vs. Cloud Execution: By default, Codex runs locally inside a secure sandbox. However, the system is shifting toward managed cloud VMs (Kata containers) to leverage more compute and free developers from keeping laptops open.
  • The Harness as a Crutch: Tibo explains that the harness is always slightly ahead of the model, providing "crutches" (such as developer instructions and guardrails) to ensure reliability.
      • As newer models improve, the developer message and the harness code shrink because the model inherently understands what the user wants.

AI in the Software Development Lifecycle

  • Automated Code Review & Security: OpenAI utilizes specialized review models trained to spot deep logic and security vulnerabilities across multiple dependency layers. Security issues automatically block pull requests from merging.
  • Zero-Cost Maintenance: Maintenance is shifting from a tedious human tax to automated processes—such as automatically upgrading third-party dependencies and generating full rearchitectures in hours instead of years.
  • The "Merge" of Codex and ChatGPT: Bringing Codex into ChatGPT required bridging two entirely different stacks: a fully managed cloud-based system and a local coding agent. The resulting setup runs the full Codex harness inside a powerful cloud container accessible via ChatGPT Plus.
  • Internal AI Logging: Tibo shares that Codex acted as an internal "journalist," documenting Slack debates, document reviews, and the architectural naming decisions ("toggle arc") surrounding the ChatGPT/Codex merge.

TL;DR

AI models developed by Google DeepMind—such as GraphCast, GenCast, and WeatherNext 3—are revolutionizing global weather forecasting by bypassing traditional supercomputer fluid dynamics approximations in favor of learning statistical patterns directly from decades of historical meteorological data, achieving significant performance gains including early warnings for extreme events like Category 5 storms.

  • Early Warning Success: In October 2025, Hurricane Melissa struck Jamaica and the Caribbean. Google DeepMind's weather lab website and live forecasts (developed via partnerships with agencies like NOAA's National Hurricane Center) began showing high confidence that the disturbance would become a Category 5 hurricane about a week before landfall—while it was still a minor disturbance/tropical depression.

  • Historic Benchmark: Peter Battaglia noted this was the lowest-intensity storm the National Hurricane Center had ever forecast to become a Category 5, with forecasters explicitly stating they were heavily informed by the AI model's 80% confidence scores. The storm ultimately produced record winds of 190 mph, resulting in 95 fatalities.

Technological Shift: Traditional Numerical Models vs. AI Models

  • Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP): Traditional forecasting relies on supercomputers running approximate fluid motion physics equations iteratively over coarse, blurry spatial grids. While a historic triumph of engineering, these models are computationally heavy and prone to compounding errors.
  • AI Statistical Learning: DeepMind's approach fits a highly complex function to decades of historical weather records (such as ECMWF data spanning decades) to extrapolate future weather patterns based on past behavior.
  • Error Behavior: Unlike traditional models that can "blow up" or diverge into implausible extremes when errors compound, AI statistical models tend to regress to the mean (predicting average weather when uncertain), yet remarkably retain the capacity to forecast extreme historical outliers like record-breaking hurricanes.

Evolution of DeepMind's Weather Models

  • Phase 1 & 2 (Short-Term & Regional): Early AI applications filled in fine-scale gaps for NWP models or predicted specific phenomena over short horizons (e.g., 90-minute rainfall models).
  • GraphCast (Global Deterministic): One of the first Phase 3 models to simulate all weather over the entire Earth out to 10 days in a single representation, capturing large-scale structures (like entire hurricane widths) alongside local details.
  • GenCast (Probabilistic): Introduced around 2023, shifting from a single deterministic guess to generating multiple plausible scenarios (probabilistic forecasting) using diffusion models and newly invented Functional Generative Networks (which inject statistical perturbations by slightly altering neural network weights).
  • WeatherNext 3 (End-to-End Integration):
    • Raw Data Processing: Moves beyond relying strictly on bureau estimates by ingesting raw satellite imagery and directly predicting high-quality weather station observations (e.g., airports) in an end-to-end architecture.
    • Resolution & Frequency: Operates at a higher spatial resolution with hourly forecasts (updated every hour due to hourly satellite changes) and native 6-hour window state predictions.
    • New Variables: Adds wind and solar variables designed to improve renewable energy supply forecasting and surface temperature/humidity metrics for electrical demand ("load") forecasting.

Downstream Applications & Future Horizons

  • Renewable Energy & Load Forecasting: Weather Next 3 supplies critical wind and solar variables for energy generation, while temperature and humidity metrics help power grids predict electrical demand peaks.
  • Agriculture: Longer-term forecasting (weekly rainfall, monthly averages) aims to inform farmer decision-making regarding planting, harvesting, and deploying drought- or rain-resistant seeds.
  • Cross-Pollination with LLMs: Future research will explore integrating text data (such as public discussions about weather) and allowing Large Language Models to interrogate weather models (e.g., asking conversational questions about specific mountain tops or valleys).

TL;DR

As public AI benchmarks saturate and self-reported model scores become increasingly unreliable, independent and continuously evolving evaluations are becoming critical for both enterprises measuring ROI and governments assessing safety risks.

The Problem with Current AI Benchmarks

  • Saturation and Goodhart’s Law: Public benchmarks are easily hacked and saturated as models optimize directly for the tests.
  • Flawed Self-Reporting: Rayan Krishnan noted that when Meta released Llama 4, public open-source benchmarks showed incredible capabilities, but VALS's held-out private benchmarks revealed the model was actually underperforming.
  • The Need for Neutrality: Rayan Krishnan explained that third-party evaluators must avoid conflicts of interest, drawing parallels to the Enron auditing scandal where mixing consulting with auditing corrupted incentives. VALS explicitly decided never to sell training data to labs to maintain independence.

Enterprise ROI and the Shift to Token Spend

  • Escalating Costs: Ben Horowitz noted the challenge of measuring fluid frontier intelligence compared to traditional Gartner quadrants. Rayan Krishnan shared an example from a Fortune 10 company where an engineer's budget increased from $100 to $300 per day, causing peak productivity to shift to 4:00 PM–6:00 PM when rate limits reset.
  • Token Spend vs. Salaries: Rayan Krishnan recounted a VALS internal experiment where a token-maxing test resulted in spending $1.5 million in a month on free tokens—which was 10x higher than employee salaries for that month.
  • Val Smith Enterprise Product: Rayan Krishnan introduced Val Smith, a tool allowing companies to take their GitHub codebase and build internal coding benchmarks to determine the most performant and Pareto-optimal models for their specific repositories.

Pre-Release Infrastructure and Evolving Testing

  • The 6-Hour Pre-Release Window: Rayan Krishnan described how VALS went from co-founders pulling all-nighters to running distributed evaluations at maximum rate limits using an internal system called "Steve, the economic VALS employee."
  • Handling Complex Workflows: Rayan Krishnan noted that as evaluations become more complex (testing agentic work over hours, days, or weeks), sample sizes shrink while rubrics and criteria grow significantly larger (e.g., testing 50 full-stack web applications rather than millions of static images).
  • Always a Higher Peak: Rayan Krishnan outlined VALS's unofficial motto and practice of deprecating saturated benchmarks and perpetually constructing new mountains for foundation model labs to summit.

Specialized Indexes and Frontier Metrics

  • Economic Applications: Rayan Krishnan highlighted VALS's finance agent benchmark (used by major financial institutions) and VI codebench for full-stack web application generation.
  • Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI): Rayan Krishnan discussed the newly released RSI Index, which creates an apples-to-apples way to benchmark pre-training, post-training, and harness-level engineering proxies across frontier labs.

Policy, Government Coordination, and Sovereign AI

  • Division of Labor: Ben Horowitz compared the AI policy challenge to the MPAA rating system ("I know it when I see it"), arguing that governments are well-suited to set and enforce rules, while competent private companies must evaluate whether those rules are broken.
  • Information Sharing: Rayan Krishnan stated that VALS regularly briefs executive and legislative branches on model capabilities and risks, helping policymakers ground abstract conversations in empirical data.
  • Global Verification: Rayan Krishnan discussed the paradox of sovereign AI investments and suggested that international coordination on catastrophic risks (like cyber, bio, and RSI) will eventually require a shared language of evaluations, comparing it to Reagan's "trust but verify" doctrine for nuclear stockpiles.
