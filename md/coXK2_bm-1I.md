**TL;DR**
The private credit market, specifically direct lending, is entering a period of disillusionment as the long-term tailwinds of low interest rates and undisciplined underwriting collide with AI-driven disruption in the software sector and retail redemption pressures.

## The Evolution of the Credit Sector
* **Historical Chronology:** Howard Marks (Co-Chairman, Oaktree Capital) outlines a 50-year progression from the acceptance of high-yield debt in the 1970s to the current marketing of direct lending to retail investors.
    * **1970s-80s:** Michael Milken popularized non-investment grade debt; the birth of Leveraged Buyouts (LBOs).
    * **1990s-2000s:** Growth of broadly syndicated loans, securitizations (CLOs), and "alternative investments" after the tech bubble.
    * **2010s-2020s:** Post-GFC regulation forced banks to retreat, creating a vacuum filled by non-bank "direct lending" to mid-market PE-sponsored companies.
* **Market Scale:** The private credit sector has ballooned from roughly $150 billion 20 years ago to approximately $2 trillion today.
* **Risk vs. Volatility:** Marks warns that the lack of price volatility in private loans—due to the absence of a public market—has been misinterpreted as a lack of credit risk. 

## The Anatomy of a Credit Bubble
* **The "New Thing" Trap:** Novel investment forms often escape skepticism because they are untested. Early success fuels envy, leading latecomers (the "imitators" and "idiots") to lower standards to deploy capital.
* **The Quality Erosion:** Hundreds of firms entered direct lending post-GFC without experiencing a full credit cycle. This influx of capital forced lenders to compete by accepting:
    * Lower yields and narrower spreads.
    * Reduced safety and "covenant-lite" loan documents.
    * Higher debt levels and "ARR loans" (based on revenue rather than profit).
* **The Role of Liquidity Mismatches:** Drawing parallels to 1929, Marks cites three primary bubble drivers: selling to unsuitable investors, providing heavy leverage, and the mismatch between illiquid assets and short-term redemption expectations.

## The Software Debt Crisis and AI Disruption
* **Concentration Risk:** Software debt now represents a massive portion of credit markets, specifically 20-30% of direct lending (compared to only 4-5% of the high-yield bond market).
* **The AI Tipping Point:** In late 2025 and early 2026, the release of powerful AI coding models (such as those from Anthropic) disrupted the "moat" thesis of the software industry, which previously relied on high-cost human programming and recurring subscription revenue.
* **Sentiment Shift:** As equity cushions for software companies evaporated due to AI disruption, the credit market faced a "cognitive tipping point" in February 2026.
    * **Redemption Pressures:** Investors in semi-liquid Business Development Companies (BDCs) began requesting withdrawals, leading to redemption limits and skepticism regarding Net Asset Values (NAVs).

## The End of the "Sea Change" Era
* **The Interest Rate Tailwind:** Private equity and direct lending matured during a 40-year period (1980-2021) of secularly declining interest rates, which boosted asset values and lowered borrowing costs.
* **The New Reality:** The 400-500 basis point rate hike starting in 2022 has "thrown sand in the gears."
    * **Refinancing Challenges:** Portfolio companies are struggling with lower coverage ratios (earnings vs. interest expense).
    * **Lower Returns:** MSCI estimates that U.S. PE funds saw annualized returns of only 5.8% between 2022 and Q3 2025, significantly trailing the S&P 500's 11.6%.
* **Oaktree’s Positioning:** Marks emphasizes that Oaktree maintained discipline by limiting direct lending to 20% of its performing credit assets and keeping software exposure "extremely small" relative to peers.
