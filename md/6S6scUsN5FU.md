## The $1.5 Trillion FY 2027 Defense Budget Request

*   **Historical Funding Context:** Committee Chairman Mike Rogers emphasized that US defense spending as a percentage of GDP has steadily declined since World War II, allowing adversaries—specifically China—to spend more of their GDP on military build-ups. He advocated for returning US defense spending to 4.5% of GDP to ensure true deterrence.
*   **A Pivot from "Austerity":** Secretary of War Pete Hegseth rejected the previous administration’s "divest to invest" approach, characterizing it as a strategy of austerity that forced the military to trade near-term readiness for long-term modernization. 
*   **Key Budget Allocations:** The proposed $1.5 trillion budget fully funds both sustainment and modernization:
    *   **Operations & Maintenance:** Includes a 24% increase in operation and maintenance, including a 20% increase in core readiness programs such as combat exercises and flight hours.
    *   **Infrastructure & Quality of Life:** Provides a 115% increase in funding to repair and improve living facilities and eliminate all poor or failing barracks for service members.
    *   **Procurement & R&D:** Proposes an unprecedented 76% increase in procurement and a 64% increase in research and development to rapidly expand and field munitions, autonomous systems, ships, and aircraft.
    *   **Personnel & Pay:** Increases military end strength by 44,000 personnel and provides a historic pay raise (7% for lower enlisted, 6% for E6 to O3, and 5% for O4 and above).
*   **Fiscal and Audit Alarms:** Ranking Member Adam Smith raised concerns about the nation's $40 trillion debt, arguing that giving a 50% to 60% budget increase to a Pentagon that has failed to pass successive audits is fiscally irresponsible.

## Revitalizing the Defense Industrial Base (DIB)

*   **Transition to a "Business Model":** Secretary Hegseth testified that the Pentagon has flipped its acquisition process 180 degrees, replacing a slow bureaucratic model with an outcome-driven business model to bypass red tape.
*   **Leveraging Private Capital:** Rather than relying solely on taxpayer funds, the department stimulated over $50 billion in private investments across 39 states and 180 cities. This has resulted in:
    *   280 new or expanded defense manufacturing facilities.
    *   Over 18 million new square feet of domestic manufacturing space.
    *   More than 70,000 new defense manufacturing jobs.
*   **Multi-Year Procurement (MYP) Demand Signals:** To rebuild depleted stockpiles, Hegseth highlighted the importance of 5-to-7-year MYP agreements. These long-term contracts incentivize private industry to build factories and double or quadruple production capacity for 14 critical munitions (including PAC-3, SM-3, SM-6, Patriot, Tomahawk, and AMRAAM).
*   **Next-Gen Autonomous and Triad Spending:** The budget designates $54.6 billion to the Defense Autonomous Warfare Group (DAWG) to scale domestic drone dominance and counter-UAS systems, and $71 billion to modernize the nuclear triad.

## Strategic Debate and Operational Cost of the War with Iran

*   **Strategic Objectives vs. "Realism":** Ranking Member Smith criticized the administration's "Trump Doctrine" as unrealistic, stating it initiated an uncontrolled full-scale war in the Middle East that has failed to alter Iran's nuclear program or proxy capabilities. In contrast, Secretary Hegseth defended the war, stating it successfully shattered Iran's conventional missile and drone "shield" before they could build a nuclear weapon.
*   **Strait of Hormuz and Global Economic Pain:** Committee members highlighted the immense economic fallout of the 60-day conflict:
    *   **Oil and Gas Prices:** The average price of US gas has risen to $4.30 per gallon (up 30 cents in a single week), and Brent crude has spiked to roughly $117–$120 per barrel—a 62% increase since the start of the war.
    *   **Shipping Bottlenecks:** Maritime transits through the Strait of Hormuz have dropped by 81% due to Iran's blockades and the subsequent US counter-blockades.
*   **Direct War Costs and Munitions Depletion:** Acting CFO Jay Hurst estimated the direct cost of Operation Epic Fury at $25 billion to date, driven heavily by munitions replenishment. Critics expressed worry that rapid munitions expenditure is depleting magazine depth below levels needed to deter China in the Indo-Pacific.
*   **Operational Strain and Casualties:** 14 US service members have died during Operation Epic Fury, including six killed and over 30 wounded in an Iranian drone strike at Port Shuaiba in Kuwait. Additionally, the deployment of three carrier strike groups to Central Command (including the USS *Gerald Ford's* historic 312-day deployment) has created severe readiness and maintenance backlogs.

## Personnel Controversies and Accountability Concerns

*   **High-Level Military Firings:** Committee members sharply questioned Secretary Hegseth regarding the abrupt firing of several senior military leaders, including Army Chief of Staff General Randy George and the Secretary of the Navy. Hegseth defended the moves, stating "personnel is policy" and that new leadership was required to steer the department away from previous "social engineering" policies and back to battlefield merit.
*   **Termination of Collective Bargaining:** Secretary Hegseth confirmed his April 9th memo terminating collective bargaining agreements across the DOD, asserting that the Department of War should not be subject to collective bargaining so that leadership can maximize operational efficiency.
*   **Reversing Vaccine Mandates:** To "restore freedom" to the joint force, the department eliminated the mandatory annual flu shot and is actively working to reinstate, with back pay and rank, any service members discharged under the previous administration's COVID-19 vaccine mandate.
