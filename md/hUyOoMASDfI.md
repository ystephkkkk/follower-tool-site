**TL;DR**  
While AI is currently driving a technology-led U.S. growth boom (accounting for 1.5 percentage points of the 2.1% real GDP growth), its transformative productivity "magic" has not yet materialized in aggregate data, and overlapping supply shocks—including AI-related price surges—have kept inflation above target for nearly six years, requiring further monetary policy tightening.

## Current Economic Impact of AI: Growth, Productivity, and Inflation
* **Technology-Led Growth Boom:** Real GDP is growing at 2.1% over a four-quarter/12-month change, but without technology goods, growth would be just 0.6%.
  * **Sustained Pipeline:** Data center construction shows an upward trajectory in 2026 with a large pipeline of planned projects, ensuring that related spending on materials, goods, and software will continue to support growth even if enthusiasm cools.
* **Mixed Productivity Evidence:** Labor productivity growth in 2023–2026 has exceeded the previous decade's 1.5% historical average, but Total Factor Productivity (TFP)—the sustainable "magic" behind transformative tech—has cooled to average or slightly below average after being strong in 2023–2024.
  * **Firm-Level Reality:** A survey by Nick Bloom and his team shows that over the past three years, firms have not yet seen a broad AI impact on productivity growth, though green shoots exist in specific industries.
* **AI-Driven Price Pressures:** After two to three decades of deflation/disinflation in technology goods, prices have reversed sharply to 15.7% (nearly 16%) inflation due to demand outstripping supply.
  * **Modest Headline Spillover:** AI/tech goods currently contribute a modest 0.2 percentage points directly to overall headline inflation, meaning other variables are still doing most of the driving.
  * **Energy & Infrastructure:** Power-hungry data centers are creating a demand shock on top of existing community energy needs, raising electricity and input costs for businesses that must either absorb margin hits or pass prices on to consumers.

## Monetary Policy & Financial Conditions
* **Rethinking Supply Shocks:** Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco President and CEO Mary C. Daly noted that while textbook monetary policy looks through supply shocks (since policy takes 12 to 18 months to take effect), the U.S. is facing three overlapping shocks—tariffs, war/energy prices, and AI—on top of inflation that has been above target for almost six years.
* **Inflation Expectations & Sticky Services:** Medium- and longer-run inflation expectations remain anchored around 2%, but Daly emphasized that price pressures are bleeding into sticky services inflation. She supported the September rate increase and maintains a bias toward further tightening.
* **Financial Conditions Alignment:** SIEPR Director Neale Mahoney noted the 10-year Treasury yield crossed 5% this morning. Daly welcomed tighter financial conditions as complementary to the Fed's goals, noting markets are reacting appropriately to incoming data, though the Fed retains the ultimate responsibility for price stability and full employment.

## Labor Market Dynamics & The Early-Career Slowdown
* **Low Hiring, Low Firing:** Aggregate labor market data shows a stable unemployment rate consistent with the natural rate, characterized by low hiring and low firing rather than widespread AI-driven layoffs.
* **Disproportionate Stress on Young Workers:** Unemployment rates for recent college graduates are rising relative to all workers—an unusual trend—and an Anthropic survey confirms younger and early-career workers are significantly more worried about AI than senior staff.
  * **Why Firms Are "Going Skinny":** Daly's employer networks report holding off on hiring ("going skinny") because they are revamping workflows (e.g., accounting) and do not yet know what skill mixes they will need (such as a CPA with cybersecurity skills). Employers also need senior staff who can evaluate whether AI outputs are correct, a role newly minted graduates cannot easily fill.
* **Medium-Run Labor Scenarios:** Mahoney outlined two competing scenarios: a benign transition where job changes get absorbed in normal labor market churn, versus a worst-case "hollowing out" where a recession triggers structural job losses—noting that historically, nearly 90% of routine jobs were lost during recessions or the six months afterward.
  * **Demographic Accelerants:** Daly highlighted a third scenario: firms automated in response to the post-pandemic labor shortage, and with U.S. labor force growth projected to hit zero by 2030 without immigration, employers are using AI to maintain output with fewer workers.

## Market Valuations, Private Sector Discipline & Research Needs
* **Orderly Valuation Repricing:** Addressing recent volatility where the "Magnificent 7" dropped relative to the S&P 493, Daly described it as an "orderly" repricing of risk rather than an unwinding of AI's underlying value or investment, noting she sees no "burgeoning signs of fragility."
* **Private Sector Cost Discipline:** Market forces are beginning to curb AI-related inflation. The rising share of tokens processed by less-expensive open-weight models on OpenRouter has correlated with token price reductions from OpenAI and Anthropic. Non-tech firms are also extending chip depreciation cycles beyond two years or redesigning products to use fewer/older chips.
* **What the Fed Needs from Researchers:** Daly urged economists to study how overlapping relative price shocks become sticky, to conduct firm-level case studies on how AI changes business processes (similar to how the unit motor transformed manufacturing during electrification), and to focus on proactive training/policy solutions—comparing U.S. worker pessimism to the less pessimistic views in the EU cited by the ECB's Philip Lane.
