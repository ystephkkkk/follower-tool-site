**TL;DR**
Mishaal Ashemimry, the first female aerospace engineer in the GCC, highlights that while technical innovation in the $1.8 trillion space economy is accelerating, the sector must overcome systemic gender biases and "leaky" educational pipelines to retain the diverse talent necessary for future growth.

## The Entrepreneurial Journey and Market Timing
*   **Startup Vision:** At age 26, Ashemimry founded **Mishaal Aerospace**, a rocket company targeting the then-underserved small satellite market.
    *   **Strategic Niche:** She aimed to provide dedicated launches for miniaturized satellites, which previously had to wait years for secondary spots on larger rockets.
    *   **Long-term Goals:** The company’s vision included lunar missions to harvest **Helium-3** to facilitate the growth of nuclear fusion on Earth.
*   **The Investment Gap:** Despite successful static test fires and proven designs, the company was put on hold due to a lack of investor appetite for high-risk, long-term aerospace projects in that era.
    *   **Lesson on Timing:** Ashemimry notes that while her technical vision was "dead on," the financial market was not yet ready. Today, the landscape has shifted toward a significantly higher appetite for venture capital in space.

## Regional Transformation in the Middle East
*   **Establishing a Pipeline:** When Ashemimry began her career, there was no formal space agency in Saudi Arabia. This changed in 2018 with the establishment of the **Saudi Space Commission**.
*   **Advocacy and Influence:** Ashemimry uses her platform as a science influencer on YouTube to provide STEM knowledge in Arabic, aiming to ensure she is not the only woman from the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) in the field.
*   **Economic Diversification:** She emphasizes that aerospace degrees open doors beyond rockets, providing the fundamental skill sets needed for advanced manufacturing and creative engineering across various sectors.

## Barriers to Diversity in Aerospace
*   **Global Underrepresentation:** Women comprise only **13% of the aerospace workforce** worldwide. Ashemimry identifies two primary "hurdles":
    *   **Education Attrition:** Representation "shrinks" at every level of higher education; by graduate school, she was often the only woman in the room.
    *   **Workplace Hostility:** In professional settings, Ashemimry reports having to work "four times as hard" as men to be taken seriously. She recalls being dismissed as a "note-taker" rather than a lead designer during technical reviews.
*   **Retention Challenges:** A lack of supportive ecosystems for family life in high-stress engineering roles often forces women out of the industry.
    *   **Skill Attrition:** When women leave for family reasons, a lack of "returnship" pathways leads to diminishing skill sets, making it difficult to re-enter the field.

## Leadership Mindsets for Pioneers
*   **The "Self-Model" Framework:** For those in pioneering roles without mentors, Ashemimry suggests three pillars of success:
    1.  **Continuous Learning:** Knowledge must grow beyond university levels to keep pace with rapid technological evolution.
    2.  **Redefining Failure:** View every failure as a "data point" rather than a dead end.
    3.  **Resilience:** Developing a "thick skin" to handle being underestimated while focusing strictly on technical delivery.
*   **Decision-Making Strategy:** She advises "sleeping on" difficult decisions rather than responding immediately, allowing logic to balance emotion.
