## TL;DR
Wells Fargo industrials analyst Steve Tusa explains to host Steve Eisman that half of his 16 covered industrial companies are now deeply embedded in the AI data center build-out, driving unprecedented growth—but leaving both the sector and the broader U.S. economy highly vulnerable to a potential recession if AI capex pulls back. Meanwhile, physical construction timelines (roughly three years per gigawatt) and power bottlenecks—rather than Texas political pushback—represent the primary near-term constraints on a massive 450-gigawatt U.S. pipeline.

## The AI-Driven Industrial Boom & Macro Sensitivity
* **Unprecedented Sector Growth:** 8 out of 16 companies in Tusa’s coverage are directly in the thick of AI data center construction, driving group growth at **2x normal GDP rates** (compared to ~3% historically). Companies are beating and raising almost every quarter, trading at a slight premium to the S&P 500.
* **Massive Capex Tailwinds:** Consensus data center capex is projected at **$850 billion to $1 trillion** this year and **$1.4 trillion** next year.
* **"Nothing Else Matters" Thesis:** Tusa initiated on the group in late September under the title *Nothing Else Matters*. Outside of AI, traditional economic drivers like autos, housing, and general industrial volume (e.g., 3M’s core volume ex-data centers was just **1%** this quarter) are sluggish.
* **Recession & K-Shaped Consumer Risk:** Both Eisman and Tusa agree that a material pullback in AI capex (e.g., Google cutting capex in half) would almost certainly trigger a recession—though not a 2008/2009-style financial crisis, as banks are well-capitalized. Tusa notes that high-end consumers in the current K-shaped economy are holding up spending largely because they are benefiting from the AI trade.
* **The Unwind / Safety Trade:** During recent market unwinds, investors rotated into non-data-center safety plays like **Otis** (elevators) and **Lennox** (residential HVAC, zero data center exposure), which benefits if an AI slowdown drives interest rates down.

## Key Players & The Liquid Cooling Transition
* **Vertiv:** **85%** of revenue comes from data centers (both power and cooling). Its market cap has surged to **$70 billion**—matching former parent-style industrial giant Honeywell—up from a leveraged SPAC five years ago.
* **Eaton:** Over **20%** data center exposure, primarily in electrical power, though it recently acquired a liquid cooling/CDU company.
* **nVent:** Smaller-cap player with **20–25%** data center exposure, focused on liquid cooling (coolant distribution units, or CDUs) and some electrical gear.
* **Forgent:** Smaller-cap company that went public last year with **~55%** data center exposure, specializing in electrical equipment (transformers, switchgear) to step down and condition power.
* **Rockwell Automation:** ~**3%** of sales in data centers, adding **2 percentage points** to its ~**8%** total growth (~6% ex-data centers), alongside semiconductor exposure.
* **The Liquid Cooling Shift:** Data center racks have jumped from **10 kW** to **200 kW**, heading toward **1 MW**, packing up to **70 GPUs** (e.g., Nvidia Blackwell B200/B300) per rack. Air/fan walls can no longer cool them, forcing a mandatory shift to cold plates and liquid cooling. This has transformed liquid cooling from a **$1 billion** niche (aerospace/HPC) into a **$7 billion to $10 billion** mainstream industry.
* **Legacy HVAC Chillers:** **Johnson Controls (JCI)**, **Trane**, and **Carrier** remain essential for providing the massive chillers that reject heat from liquid cooling loops into the atmosphere.

## Onshoring & Data Center Construction Bottlenecks
* **Limited Broad Onshoring:** Despite tariff talk and 100% factory write-off incentives, broad manufacturing onshoring is incremental. Life sciences/pharma has a **$35 billion to $40 billion** pipeline—too small to move the needle.
* **Semiconductor & Electrical Exceptions:** Driven by AI demand rather than government subsidies, semiconductors have a **$250 billion** project pipeline over the next two years. Additionally, **16 million square feet** of new electrical/power/HVAC manufacturing capacity has been announced to supply **~$20 billion** in extra data center gear.
* **Scale of the Build-Out:** Prior to AI (2020–2023), the entire U.S. data center installed base was **20 gigawatts** (1% of U.S. power usage), built over 20 years at 1–2 GW/year. Tusa’s database of permits and land buys shows a current pipeline of **450 gigawatts**—more than **20x** the 2023 base.
* **Texas & Construction Realities:** Texas’s official ERCOT queue sits at **375 gigawatts**, yet as of mid-year, only **5 GW** were lit up and **3 GW** approved to be energized (~8–10 GW total). For example, the **Stargate** project in Abilene (800 MW to 1 GW) broke ground in June 2024 and is only **75%** complete.
* **Supply vs. Demand Debate:** Eisman highlights that an electrical contractor in Michigan noted a shortage of transmission gear and even utility poles. While Eisman views physical construction time (~3 years per gigawatt) as the binding constraint that could pause AI growth, Tusa argues Texas politics won't halt in-flight projects; the real long-term risk is whether end-user AI demand justifies the returns.
