## TL;DR
Quantum computing poses a severe, imminent threat to global cryptography by around 2030, necessitating an immediate enterprise shift to post-quantum infrastructure. Meanwhile, the AI landscape is shifting toward open-weight models and task-specific optimization over costly frontier LLMs, driving massive SaaS consolidation.

## Quantum Computing & Cryptography
* **The 2030 Threat Timeline:** **Arvind Krishna** states that the DARPA Quantum Benchmarking Initiative (QBI) midpoint points to roughly 2030 for quantum computers to break today's cryptography. 
  * **Stolen Data Risk:** An encrypted hard drive stolen today can be stored for 5 years and broken once quantum machines reach scale.
  * **Enterprise Unpreparedness:** **Nikesh Arora** warns that organizations are already late, as 3-5 years is insufficient time to overhaul IT stacks, noting that ~50% of current infrastructure spend is not quantum-safe.
* **Evolutionary Milestones:** 
  * **Error Correction:** Krishna predicts demonstrations of error mitigation/correction on running quantum computers by 2027.
  * **Scaling Qubits:** Moving from current toy computers (~10,000 physical qubits) by adding a zero per year could reach ~10 million qubits by 2029.
  * **Logical vs. Physical:** **Arvind Krishna** clarifies that error correction decreases effective capacity, yielding a much lower number of "logical" qubits (e.g., 10,000 physical yielding ~200 logical).
* **AI vs. Hardware for Code-Breaking:** While an AI model might theoretically discover a new number theory algorithm or prime number distribution to break keys, Krishna notes it cannot run efficiently on classical architectures (GPUs/arithmetic) without actual quantum hardware.

## Open-Weight vs. Frontier AI Models
* **The Economics of Tokens:** 
  * **High Costs:** Frontier models cost $20 to $50 per million tokens, making them economically unviable for universal enterprise traffic inspection (e.g., inspecting endpoint traffic).
  * **Open-Weight Cost Reductions:** **Arvind Krishna** notes that running an 800-billion parameter open-weight model (like Kimikay 3) on optimized in-house GPUs with a week of engineering drops costs to $1–$2 per million tokens—a 10x to 40x reduction for 95% of capability.
* **Strategic Deployment Paradigms:**
  * **Phased Adoption:** Arora notes enterprises are in a discovery phase where employees use the best available models to understand capabilities, but will transition to task-specific Small Language Models (SLMs) trained on proprietary data.
  * **Exceptions for Frontier Models:** Frontier LLMs will be reserved for high-stakes scenarios where potential business disruption justifies the cost—such as deploying agent swarms during an active cyber breach.
* **Geopolitics and IP:** Krishna projects that 75% to 90% of token usage will shift to open-weight models driven by economics, IP/brand protection, and geopolitics (with the gap between Western and Chinese open-weight models closing to 6–12 months).

## AI in Cyber Warfare & Security
* **Asymmetric vs. Symmetric Defense:** 
  * **Defensive Scaling:** Krishna outlines that warfare principles require technological superiority and symmetric costs; defenders must make security as cheap as attacks by pooling vulnerabilities and sharing automated patches across trusted industry alliances.
  * **Vulnerability Asymmetry:** **Nikesh Arora** notes that AI-driven tools (like Mythos) can spot vulnerabilities, but attackers only need one exploit while defenders must patch every flaw. Early testing shows AI security outputs have a 60% to 80% real-flaw rate alongside 20% hallucinations.
* **Agentic Code Generation:** Krishna states that agents now write roughly half the code inside his organization, producing superior results because agents can execute evaluations and fixes before deployment. However, fully verifying the correctness of arbitrary input code remains computationally infeasible.

## Geopolitics, Regulation, and the Global Arms Race
* **The Inevitability of the Arms Race:** Panellists agree that global AI development cannot be slowed down; if Western nations or specific states attempt bans or heavy restrictions, countries like Japan, China, and India will accelerate to capture market advantage.
* **Data Center Backlash:** Arora highlights local political populism driving discussions of extreme measures (like hypothetical 50-state data center bans), though uniform adoption across all 50 states is unrealistic.
* **Compute Access Divide:** Arora warns that the primary global divide will not just be economic, but based on supply constraints of compute—leaving companies further from the epicenter (e.g., in Europe) at a structural disadvantage.
* **Capital vs. Labor:** Krishna notes that historical cycles favor capital-intensive nations over those bogged down by left-leaning philosophies focused on protecting every existing job rather than adapting to labor displacement.

## Vertical Integration & SaaS Consolidation
* **The Modern Conglomerate and Infrastructure:** 
  * **IBM & Nvidia Parallels:** Krishna compares modern vertical integration to the historic IBM mainframe (controlling the stack from chip design to software) and points to Jensen Huang at Nvidia as managing a modern vertically integrated stack "down to the dirt" to control profit pools and commoditize layers.
  * **Application Layer Moots:** Arora argues that application providers will build vertical stacks integrating software, reasoning, memory, and context, creating high friction and sticky moots for personal and enterprise agents.
* **Enterprise Software Consolidation:**
  * **SaaS Disruption:** Arora predicts massive disruption in the SaaS space over the next 5 to 8 years as AI transforms software product-market fit.
  * **Vendor Reduction:** Krishna notes enterprises currently use 500 to 5,000 distinct software vendors; AI capabilities will drive massive consolidation down to 5 to 10 main platforms to reduce attack surfaces and distribution costs. Non-interaction-rich systems (like core banking database lookups) will resist easy AI replacement.
