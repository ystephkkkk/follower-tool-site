## TL;DR
Steve Eisman and his team predicted the 2008 housing crisis not on a hunch, but by manually tracking a $10,000-a-year monthly database of subprime loan delinquencies and shorting mortgage securitizations via credit default swaps. Their bet paid off hundreds of millions of dollars as reckless underwriting, predatory teaser-rate loans, and Wall Street's systemic blindness triggered a global financial collapse.

## From Subprime 1.0 to a $600 Billion Market
*   **The 1998 Precedent:** As a sell-side analyst at Oppenheimer in the 1990s, Eisman covered subprime lending when it was a $50 billion to $70 billion-a-year industry. In 1998, he predicted eight specific subprime lenders would go bankrupt based on their accounting; all eight did.
*   **The 2002 Sequel:** After the dot-com crash and Federal Reserve rate cuts, a second generation of subprime lenders—including New Century, NovaStar, and Accredited Home Lenders—went public, often led by the exact same executives from the 1990s collapse.
*   **Underestimating the Scale:** Eisman initially assumed the second wave would be another $50 billion "rounding error." Instead, by 2006, subprime originations had exploded to $600 billion in a single year—accounting for 20% of the entire U.S. mortgage market.

## The $10,000 Database and Predatory Loan Mechanics
*   **The Monthly Spreadsheet:** Around 2005, Eisman's team began paying ~$10,000 a year for monthly loan-level performance data that issuers were required to report to Moody's and S&P.
    *   Manually inputting the data (without AI or modern software), they compared securitizations vintage-by-vintage at the exact same loan age.
    *   At month six, 30-day delinquency rates jumped from 0.75% (three-quarters of 1%) in 2004 deals to 4% and climbing in 2006 deals.
    *   Simultaneously, the share of low-doc or no-doc ("liar") loans climbed to roughly 50% of pools by 2006.
*   **The Teaser-Rate Treadmill:** Standard subprime loans issued between 2002 and mid-2007 featured a 3% teaser rate for two years before resetting to LIBOR (then 3%) plus 6%—meaning the borrower's rate jumped to 9% and payments roughly tripled overnight.
    *   Lenders qualified borrowers only on the 3% rate, knowing they could not afford the reset.
    *   The system relied on endlessly refinancing borrowers into new teaser loans for another 3 to 4 points (e.g., turning a $100,000 loan into $103,000, then $106,000), which only worked as long as home prices kept rising.

## The Trade: Credit Default Swaps and Market Blindness
*   **Moving Beyond Equities:** Eisman's team initially shorted public subprime lenders, but the stocks were small-cap and illiquid, creating massive short-squeeze risk. In late spring 2006, Deutsche Bank's Greg Lippmann (played by Ryan Gosling) called Eisman by mistake—intending to reach a similarly named fund—and educated them on shorting mortgage securitizations.
*   **The "30-Story Building" Flaw:** Securitizations sliced $1 billion loan pools into tranches. The bottom 3% took the first losses (yielding ~20%), while the top AAA tranche was designed to withstand 30% cumulative losses. Models assumed 7% to 10% losses; during the crisis, losses hit 50%, wiping out AAA paper held by pension funds and insurers.
*   **Credit Default Swaps (CDS):** Invented by JP Morgan in the 1990s to hedge bond risk, CDS allowed Eisman to buy insurance (paying ~50 basis points a year) betting that mortgage bonds he did not own would fail.
*   **Doubling Down in Vegas and Spring 2007:**
    *   At a small Las Vegas meeting hosted by Option One (owned by H&R Block), an executive claimed losses wouldn't exceed 5%. Eisman interrupted to say there was a "0% chance" of that, walked out to take a call from his wife Valerie, and doubled his short positions the next day.
    *   In March and April 2007, subprime delinquencies temporarily improved, causing bonds to rally. Recognizing this as a seasonal bounce from borrowers using annual tax refunds to catch up on payments, Eisman's team doubled their positions a second time.

## The Collapse, Government Blindness, and Unpunished Fraud
*   **"Dusseldorf" Stops Buying:** European regional banks and pension funds (nicknamed "Dusseldorf" by Eisman's team) were the primary buyers of these bonds. By summer 2007, loan quality had degraded so severely that they stopped buying, choking off the entire securitization and refinancing chain.
*   **The 2008 Miscalculation:** Eisman's biggest error was assuming the U.S. government knew what his team knew and would intervene early. Instead, regulators were blindsided by a multitrillion-dollar CDS web. In September 2008, Lehman Brothers collapsed, Bank of America bought Merrill Lynch, and the government had to bail out AIG, which had sold billions in CDS.
*   **The Practice That "Should Have Ended in Handcuffs":** Wall Street firms hired third parties to sample ~10% of $1 billion loan pools for quality. By 2006–2007, 30% to 40% of sampled loans failed underwriting standards. Banks would kick the bad 10% sample back to lenders for a refund, then securitize and sell the remaining 90% unreviewed. Despite the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (FCIC) referring this to the Department of Justice as potential fraud, only one trader system-wide went to prison.

## Fact vs. Fiction and Post-Crisis Reforms
*   **Why "Mark Baum":** Eisman's character was renamed in *The Big Short* after he and his wife asked filmmakers to remove a private family tragedy from the script; the director invented a different backstory and changed the name to separate fiction from reality.
*   **Michael Burry:** Contrary to the film's parallel editing, Eisman did not know Burry (played by Christian Bale), who reached the same conclusion independently by reading regulatory filings line by line. Eisman only realized how early Burry had spotted the crisis when reading his 2015 FCIC testimony.
*   **What Changed:** The 2010 Dodd-Frank Act created a dedicated Fed Vice Chair to supervise big banks, ending "regulator shopping." Bank leverage dropped sharply—Citigroup, for example, fell from roughly 40-to-1 leverage before the crisis to 10-to-1—making the regulated banking system's mortgage underwriting safer today than at any point in Eisman's career.
