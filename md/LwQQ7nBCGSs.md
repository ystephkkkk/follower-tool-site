## TL;DR
Hernán Cortés and Francisco Pizarro conquered massive American empires with a few hundred conquistadors, leveraging profound technological differentials, superior tactical adaptability, devastating introduced diseases, and extensive alliances with local indigenous populations who were chafing under Aztec and Inca rule.

## 1. Global Context and Early Spanish Expansion
* **The Global Economy:** In the 15th century, Europe was cut off from Eastern spice and silk markets, forcing innovations like Prince Henry the Navigator's use of wind currents and tides to turn the Atlantic into a highway.
* **The New World Discovery:** Christopher Columbus sailed west in 1492 aiming for Oriental markets and accidentally discovered an unknown continent full of potential wealth, resources, and people.
* **The Expedition Blueprint:** In 1511, de Velázquez seized control of Cuba and began sending coastal expeditions, laying the groundwork for more ambitious penetrations into the interior.

## 2. Hernán Cortés and the Conquest of the Aztec Empire
* **The Rise of Cortés:** Assigned by Velázquez in 1518 to explore Mesoamerica, Hernán Cortés—a former chief magistrate (*alcalde*) with legal training—exceeded his mandate and launched an expedition to penetrate and seize the interior.
* **Alliances with Subjugated Peoples:** Upon arriving in Mesoamerica, Cortés identified that local groups like the Totonacs chafed under Aztec hegemony; after fighting the Tlaxcalans to a standstill, he forged a crucial alliance with them for guides and reinforcements.
* **The March to Tenochtitlan:** Cortés entered the Aztec capital and met Moctezuma II, eventually taking the Great Speaker under protective custody, burning regional officers alive in front of him, and governing through a puppet monarch.
* **Internal Spanish Strife and Recovery:** Governor Velázquez sent Pánfilo de Narváez to arrest Cortés; Cortés defeated him, absorbed his forces, and returned to the capital only to find a popular revolt that led to *La Noche Triste* (The Tragic Night).
* **The Final Siege:** Rebuilding his forces with tens of thousands of indigenous allies, Cortés constructed prefabricated brigantines (transported over mountains by porters), mined sulfur from Popocatépetl for gunpowder, and took control of Lake Texcoco to reduce Tenochtitlan by 1521.

## 3. Francisco Pizarro and the Fall of the Inca Empire
* **The Andean Expedition:** Inspired by Cortés's playbook, Francisco Pizarro secured funding, sailed south, and arrived in Peru in 1532 during an Inca civil war between the late Sapa Inca's sons, Atahualpa and Huáscar.
* **The Battle of Cajamarca:** Atahualpa met Pizarro with tens of thousands of troops; Pizarro unleashed artillery, musketry, and shock cavalry in a confined courtyard, slaughtering thousands of Inca while suffering zero enemy casualties (and only a single self-inflicted cut to Pizarro's hand) and capturing Atahualpa.
* **The Siege of Cuzco:** Pizarro's puppet ruler, Manco Inca, later rebelled, laying siege to Cuzco and cutting off coastal reinforcements, but tactical errors (such as attacking Lima across open ground and failing to sustain relentless night assaults) doomed the native resistance.

## 4. Military Dynamics: Technology, Tactics, and Limitations
* **The Conquistador Advantage:**
  * **Steel Armor and Weapons:** Toledo steel thrusting swords and steel helmets easily defeated local wooden and obsidian weapons.
  * **The Horse:** Decisive shock cavalry that could blast through loose native formations and retreat faster than enemies could pursue.
  * **Fighting Dogs:** Armored war mastiffs added terrifying psychological impact.
* **Native Countermeasures:** Both Aztecs and Incas adapted by fighting in urban environments, breaking lake dikes to flood cities, building man-sized holes in buildings to evade horses, and attaching Spanish swords to poles to improvise pikes and use bolas.
* **Cultural Constraints on Warfare:** Pre-Columbian societies fought to capture victims for religious sacrifice rather than to kill, making them reluctant to eliminate leaders on the ground and hampering their strategic flexibility.

## 5. Demographics, Governance, and Long-Term Outcomes
* **Hierarchical Vulnerability:** Both the Aztec and Inca empires were centralized autocracies governed by a tiny elite; capturing the single apex ruler destabilized the entire administrative structure.
* **The Demographic Catastrophe:** Europeans introduced Old World diseases like smallpox, to which native populations had zero acquired immunity, leading to catastrophic long-term demographic collapse.
* **Economic Impact on Spain:** The influx of American gold and silver funded European wars against the Ottomans and Protestants, but imported severe inflation, depressed real wages, and prevented Spain from developing functional capitalism.
* **Global Trade Integration:** Silver from mines like Potosí in Bolivia was shipped across the Pacific to Manila, allowing the Spanish to trade excess liquidity for Chinese silk, spices, and porcelain, finalizing the first truly global trade network.
