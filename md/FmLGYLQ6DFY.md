## The AI Revolution: Constraints and Valuations

    * **Probabilistic Software:** AI differs from human-written software because it is "probabilistic," recomputing answers each time, which makes it computationally expensive but capable of superhuman tasks.

## Formative Frameworks: The Fidelity Masterclass
* **Outcome-Based Culture:** At Fidelity, Baker learned that investing is about finding a process that fits one’s "unique emotional makeup" to remain rational when the market moves against you.
* **Commodity Training:** Baker began his career covering cement and aggregates.
    * **Supply/Demand Discipline:** This taught him that every business eventually comes down to price, volume, and fixed costs—a framework he used to call the semiconductor inventory buildup that preceded the 2000 crash.

## Conviction in "Deep Tech": Nvidia and Tesla
* **The Nvidia Story:** Baker identifies Jensen Huang, Hock Tan (Broadcom), and Lisa Su (AMD) as the rare semiconductor CEOs who successfully retain "exceptional engineers."
    * **Gaming Roots:** Baker’s early conviction in Nvidia was bolstered by his hobby as a gamer, where he realized a superior GPU allowed even a less skilled player to see enemies first.
* **The Tesla Bull Case:** Baker’s investment was rooted in first principles, viewing EVs as the only transportation form with "deflationary core inputs" (batteries).
    * **Engineering Superiority:** He highlights that EVs allow for lower centers of gravity, better traction control, and massive "crumple zones" because they lack an 800-lb internal combustion engine.
* **Mission-Driven Talent:** Baker argues Elon Musk wins because he offers engineers "mission-oriented" work (decarbonization, multi-planetary life) rather than optimizing "clicks on a blue link" for ad-tech giants.

## The Craft of Investing and Career Advice
* **The Binary Choice:** Baker cites mentor Jennifer Yurg’s philosophy: An investor must either "panic early or double down late," as almost no one can successfully do both.
    * **Self-Knowledge:** Baker identifies himself as a "double down late" investor, a realization he only reached after failing early in his career.
* **The "Kind and Scrappy" Rule:** Baker advises young professionals to be both kind (to build a long-term "keiretsu" or network) and scrappy (persistent).
    * **Reputation as Capital:** He references Steve Schwarzman’s view that your reputation is formed in your 20s; the people in your training class will remember how you treated them 20 years later.
* **Seeking Hardship:** Baker emphasizes that "even God would get fired as an active manager" due to probabilistic volatility. 
    * **Early Failure:** He argues that experiencing a major drawdown early is the greatest gift an investor can receive, as it builds the resilience necessary for higher-stakes roles later.
