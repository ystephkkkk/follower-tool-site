**TL;DR**
Paul Sheard traces the 25-year evolution of Quantitative Easing (QE) from a 2001 Japanese experiment to a global monetary standard, highlighting how the technical ability to pay interest on bank reserves fundamentally changed how central banks manage liquidity and interest rates.

## Origins and Global Adoption of Quantitative Easing
*   **The Japanese Experiment:** The modern era of QE began on March 19, 2001, at the Bank of Japan. Initially viewed as a "Japanese curiosity," it remained a local phenomenon for seven years before the 2008 financial crisis forced its adoption globally.
*   **US Implementation:** In response to the 2008 collapse, the Federal Reserve pivoted from emergency "credit easing" to purposeful QE, buying Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities to inject liquidity.
*   **Balance Sheet Growth:** The Fed’s balance sheet ratcheted up from pre-2008 levels to a peak of approximately $9 trillion by late 2023. It has since entered a "quantitative tightening" phase, currently sitting at roughly $6.5 trillion.

## Mechanics of the Central Bank Balance Sheet
*   **Asset Composition:** Central bank assets primarily consist of government bonds and debt securities. These are paid for through the creation of "reserves"—base money created "out of thin air."
*   **Key Liabilities:** The Fed’s balance sheet is balanced by three primary liability components:
    *   **Reserves:** Deposits that commercial banks hold at the central bank.
    *   **Banknotes:** Physical currency in circulation (Federal Reserve Notes). Notably, these are signed by the US Treasurer, not the Fed Chair.
    *   **Government Deposits:** The Treasury’s own bank account held at the Fed, which fluctuates based on tax revenue and government spending (e.g., Social Security or military payments).
*   **The Accounting Identity:** The level of reserves in the banking system is mathematically determined by the other components. If the public withdraws cash or the government collects taxes, reserves go down unless the central bank offsets this by purchasing more assets.

## The "Interest on Reserves" (IOR) Turning Point
*   **Legislative Shift:** Under the 2008 TARP legislation, the Federal Reserve was granted the legal authority to pay interest on reserves. This power was originally slated for 2011 but was accelerated to manage the crisis.
*   **Decoupling Policy:** Before 2008, the Fed could not pay interest on excess reserves. If banks had too much liquidity, they would try to "dump" it, naturally forcing the interest rate to zero. 
*   **The "Floor" System:** By paying interest on reserves, the Fed gained the ability to raise the federal funds rate even with a "bloated" balance sheet. This allowed them to tighten monetary policy without having to sell off all the assets they acquired during QE.

## Operational Dynamics of Monetary Policy
*   **The Federal Funds Rate:** This is the interest rate at which commercial banks trade reserves among themselves overnight on the Fed’s balance sheet.
*   **Open Market Operations:** The Fed acts as the "last mover" in the money market. Because they can track fiscal flows and banknote demand daily, they use the purchase or sale of assets to precisely hit their reserve and interest rate targets.
*   **Transition from Reactive to Proactive:** Historically, the Fed reactively adjusted reserves to match minimum requirements. In the post-QE era, they proactively manage the massive "cloud" of excess reserves through interest rate floors.
