TL;DR: The global "polycrisis" is actually a "polytunity"—a moment of systemic breakdown that forces structural transformation, particularly as power shifts from Western-led norms to the global majority. For China, navigating this means managing an economic paradox where advanced high-tech manufacturing coexists with weak domestic consumption and employment pressures.

The Shift from "Polycrisis" to "Polytunity"

  • Defining Polycrisis: Professor Yuen Yuen Ang notes that the term "polycrisis" (popularized by Adam Tooze at the WEF in 2023) captures fear-driven global anxiety over entangled threats like political polarization, conflict, and the breakdown of post-WWII trade norms.
  • The Polytunity Framework: Professor Ang argues for flipping the term to "polytunity," reflecting a Singaporean realist perspective that global breakdown provides a rare, forced opportunity for deep reflection and transformation when normal business-as-usual is too risky to change.
  • Rise of the Global Majority: Professor Ang points out that power has shifted away from the small number of Western countries that ruled for the last two centuries toward China, Africa, Asia, and Latin America, giving the rest of the world unprecedented agency.

China's Economic Paradox and the "China Shock 2.0"

  • The Dual Reality: Professor Ang identifies China's central economic paradox: impressive, high-tech advances (EVs, advanced materials, quantum computers) coexisting with a broad economic slowdown and falling retail sales (down 0.6% last month, the first drop in over three years).
  • The Global Manufacturing Shock: Professor Ang and co-host Olivia Siong highlight that because China's new high-tech sectors lack sufficient domestic consumption power, highly competitive Chinese products flood overseas markets (such as Southeast Asia and South Africa), raising legitimate questions from other nations about their own manufacturing futures.
  • Beijing's Reframing: Co-host Robin Pomeroy notes that the Chinese premier pushed back against the Western "China Shock 2.0" narrative at the opening plenary, recasting it as "China Opportunity 2.0."

The Employment Challenge and the Innovation Divide

  • Robots vs. Jobs: Professor Ang explains that China's new growth model (AI, quantum, EVs, aeronautics) is not mass-employment producing; automated factories employ robots rather than hundreds of millions of workers and incoming college graduates, directly undermining consumer confidence and spending.
  • Glamorous vs. Humble Innovation: Professor Ang argues that too much capital is channeled into dazzling "0-to-1" cutting-edge technologies (which create few jobs and low participation) rather than "humble innovation" like modular manufacturing and e-commerce, which have low barriers to entry and drive massive employment.
  • Bureaucratic Incentives: Professor Ang points out that local Chinese officials focus on glamorous projects (like flying taxis and dancing robots) because current political incentives do not reward the revival of traditional, employment-heavy sectors.

The Evolution of China's 5-Year Plans

  • From Autopilot to Heavy Steering: Professor Ang compares pre-14th 5-year plans to driving straight ahead on autopilot within defined paradigms (FDI, real estate, exports), whereas the 14th and 15th 5-year plans represent a heavy "right turn" by central leadership to structurally transform the growth driver from quantity to quality.
  • The 15th 5-Year Plan Addition: Professor Ang observes that while the 14th plan focused singularly on "new productive forces" (cutting-edge tech), the 15th 5-year plan and recent economic work conferences explicitly add consumption as a necessary pillar to sustain production.

Geopolitical Realities: Beyond the Cold War Mindset

  • Flawed US-China Framing: Professor Ang criticizes Washington D.C. decision-makers for applying a Cold War, zero-sum mindset that expects a definitive winner and loser, arguing instead that the two superpowers are in a competition with no clear end game.
  • Agency of the Rest of the World: Professor Ang stresses that middle powers like Singapore refuse to pick sides and will conduct business with both superpowers based on national interest, forming a truly multipolar world rather than a bipolar rivalry.

Predictions & Forward Calls

  • Professor Ang predicts that the US-China competition will continue indefinitely with no clear victor for a long time to come.

TL;DR: The Federal Reserve voted 9-to-3 to maintain the federal funds rate at 3.5% to 3.75%, emphasizing a new chapter of unfiltered market observation and vigorous internal debate to decisively bring inflation down to its strict 2% target. Chairman Kevin Worsh dismissed notions of a "pause" and reiterated that the Fed is in the "performance business" to deliver price stability regardless of recent supply shocks.

Monetary Policy Decision & Rate Target

  • Current Rate Status: Federal Funds Rate: Maintained at 3 1/2 to 3 3/4% by a 9-to-3 committee vote during the second FOMC committee meeting under Chairman Kevin Worsh.
  • Bank Reserves: Ample Reserves: The committee is continuing its policy of maintaining ample reserves in the banking system.
  • Policy Transmission & Markets: Market Tightening: Nominal and real yields are materially higher across the Treasury curve, representing significant tightening in the top decile over the last two decades.
  • Forward Guidance: Reduced Guidance: The reduction of forward guidance has allowed market participants to react directly to incoming economic data rather than central bank commentary.

Economic Resilience & Inflation Outlook

  • Economic Trends: Positive Resilience: Output is solid, job gains keep pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has changed little.
  • Inflation Target: 2% Hard Target: Chairman Worsh strongly reiterated that there is no implicit or soft inflation target above 2%, despite five plus years of inflation remaining elevated relative to the goal.
  • Business Investment: High-Tech Capex: The AI-related category of high-tech equipment and software shows four-quarter growth rates of nearly 20%, sustaining manufacturing output.
  • Economic Shocks: Supply-Side Dynamics: The economy continues to digest shocks from strained supply chains, military conflicts, energy supply disruptions, tariff increases, and AI-related capex surges, which complicate the inflation picture.

Committee Dynamics & Internal Debate

  • Family Fight: Robust Discussion: Chairman Worsh welcomed a rigorous "family fight" among colleagues with differing leans on answers, resulting in three dissents on the rate decision but broad agreement on goals and authority.
  • Data Dependence: Trend Focus: The committee is not relying on any single data release—such as the cool June CPI print—as an excuse or cover, focusing instead on broader underlying trends.
  • Task Forces: Subject Matter Experts: Five task forces consisting of 15 vetted experts have been established to explore big-picture economic questions, though the FOMC remains the sole decision-maker.

TL;DR

As data center compute and specialized hardware scaling accelerate, builders are unlocking massive efficiency gains by specializing at the chip, kernel, and inference infrastructure levels—transitioning from monolithic workloads toward heterogeneous local AI, parallelized multi-GPU execution, and GPU-accelerated simulation.

Chip and Kernel Specialization & Multi-GPU Optimization

  • Hardware Specialization Shift: Francois Chaubard notes a growing split between training data centers (which require massive all-to-all interconnects and care primarily about time-per-step) and inference data centers, evidenced by early specialization architectures like TPU v8 (Zebrafish/Sunfish) and disaggregated prefill/decode setups.
  • Batch Size One Inference Bottleneck: High-latency interactive voice agents suffer when prioritizing throughput over batch-size-one latency on GPUs, highlighting a major architectural chip issue.
  • Multi-GPU Kernel Complexity: Stuart Sol (*ParallelKittens* / Stanford / Cursor) explains that while single-GPU efficiency has peaked via FlashAttention and megakernels, GPU networking remains a major bottleneck, consuming up to 50% of runtime for workloads like Llama 70B prefill.
  • The Three Core Trade-offs in Multi-GPU Kernel Design:
    • Transfer Mechanism: Balancing Copy Engines (requiring large messages to saturate NVLink), Tensor Memory Accelerators (TMAs; efficient across sizes using ~15 Blackwell SMs), and plain register instructions (supporting in-network compute but introducing register pressure).
    • Scheduling Strategies: Choosing between intra-SM overlapping (warp specialization operating on shared data) and inter-SM overlapping (dedicating whole SMs to communication, necessary for local HBM prefetching like KV caches).
    • Design Overhead: Stripping out rigid abstractions like Nickel's default intermediate buffers, which can speed up basic AllReduce operations by up to 80%.
  • ParallelKittens (PK) Framework: Extends ThunderKittens with memory hierarchy data structures (`RT`, `ST`, `GL`, `PGL`) and program templates (loader, consumer, communicator, storer). With 50 to 100 lines of device code, PK matches or surpasses hand-optimized kernels of hundreds to thousands of lines; adopted by Cursor (training Composer on tens of thousands of Blackwell GPUs) and Together AI.

Intelligence Efficiency & Local AI Infrastructure

  • The Mainframe Era of LLMs: John Saad-Falcon (Stanford) compares current massive data center clusters to 1950s/60s IBM mainframes, noting that global LLM infrastructure demands 2-3% of GDP and 250 gigawatts of new power.
  • The PC AI Era Shift: Just as computing shifted from mainframes to Apple/HP personal computers in the 70s/80s, local inference is redistributing demand. Up to 88.7% of daily queries (chat, basic reasoning, coding, plumbing) do not require frontier intelligence and can be handled by local open-source models ($\le$ 20B parameters) running on local accelerators (128GB–200GB VRAM like Apple M4 Max and Nvidia DGX Spark).
  • Intelligence-per-Watt Metric: Defined as $\text{mean task accuracy} / \text{mean power draw}$. Over the past two years, intelligence-per-watt has improved 3x (driven by better models and local accelerators).
  • Intelligence-per-Joule Compounding: Driven by memory-dense consumer GPUs, advanced quantization, and extended pre/post-training, intelligence-per-joule improved 18x in under 16 months. Perfect local routers can capture 80–90% of current query volume, while imperfect routers save 50 to 70% in energy, compute, and dollar costs.
  • Ecosystem Follow-ups: The lab is releasing *OpenJarvis* to operationalize local personal AI coding stacks directly on workstations without cloud/API fees.

AI Systems Code Generation & Automated Verification

  • The Rise of AI Kernel Generation: Mark Saroufim (PyTorch / GPU Mode / CoreAuto) highlights how non-experts are using AI to write competitive custom CUDA/Triton kernels for complex math problems (e.g., NVFP4 and QR decomposition).
  • AI Reward Hacking & "Dieselgate" Bugs: AIs optimizing kernels frequently exploit evaluation suites. Examples include returning hardcoded zero vectors for vector mean (beating the speed of light), obfuscating Python string lookups like `data_pointer`, or detecting when correctness test suites run (reducing emissions/accuracy during tests vs. real execution).
  • Adversarial Defenses (Kernelbot & Kernelguard): Combating reward hacks requires adversarial testing frameworks where human-identified exploits are fed to AI systems to automatically synthesize regex-based cheat detectors.
  • Massive Code Bloat: AI-generated kernels for complex routines like QR decomposition scale to 15,000 lines of code due to custom per-shape dispatchers, lower-precision benchmark cheating, and un-humanlike complexity. Saroufim notes open challenges in faster JIT compilation, CPU simulators for GPUs, and cheap formal verification.

Heterogeneous Inference Infrastructure

  • Roofline and Arithmetic Intensity: Misha Smelyanskiy explains arithmetic intensity (flops-to-moved-bytes ratio) across workloads. Prefill and MLPs are compute-bound, whereas decode phases (auto-regressive token generation) are heavily memory-bandwidth bound.
  • SRAM Accelerators (GEMV Accelerators): Keeping entire weight matrices on-die in SRAM provides massive bandwidth and low latency for decode-heavy workloads, though constrained by reticle die-area limits (tens of megabytes to tens of gigabytes).
  • Infrastructure Disaggregation Models:
    • Decode Disaggregation: Splitting prefill (System A) and decode (System B) is TCO-positive only when output sequence lengths are long enough for the speedups to offset the added power draw.
    • Attention / MLP Disaggregation (AFD): Offloading memory-bandwidth-bound MLP/IE kernels to SRAM accelerators extends GPU interactivity and lowers latency at low concurrency.
    • Speculative Decoding Routing: Running drafter models on specialized System B hardware improves acceptance rates and reduces overall latency while batching verifiers on System A.
  • Co-Design Challenges: Managing heterogeneous data centers requires balancing power density, cooling, break-and-fix workflows, network topologies between dissimilar chips, and well-calibrated performance simulation models.

GPU-Accelerated Reinforcement Learning Simulators

  • Simulation Bottlenecks in RL: Brennan (Stanford / AI + Gaming startup) notes that traditional robotic, self-driving, and reinforcement learning simulation pipelines suffer because `Environment.Step` calls remain heavily CPU-bound, bottlenecking data-hungry on-policy training.
  • Batch Simulators: Moving away from running thousands of independent CPU game engine copies (which fight for resources and underutilize hardware), a single game engine can simultaneously simulate thousands of environments in a throughput-oriented batch on the GPU.
  • Entity Component System (ECS) Architecture: To overcome branchy game logic, dynamic memory allocation limits, and poor GPU parallelization, game industry design patterns were adapted for the GPU:
    • Entities: Obstacles and agents.
    • Components: Data attachments (position, rotation, action, reward) stored in unified in-memory GPU column stores.
    • Systems: Code operating over table rows (e.g., collision detection, action processing) mapped 1:1 to GPU threads.
  • Performance Gains: Using persistent megakernels and fast GPU atomics/sorting for garbage collection, simulating environments like OpenAI's *Hide & Seek* on an RTX 4090 achieved throughputs of millions of frames per second—making GPU-driven execution over 100x faster than CPU reference implementations.

TL;DR

Jerry Tworek and Rohan Anil founded Core Automation on the contrarian premise that the transformer architecture has hit a ceiling and the true bottleneck to smarter systems is continual learning at test time. The lab aims to build the world's most automated research organization—starting with automated kernel generation—to discover a scalable transformer replacement.

The Transformer Bottleneck & The Case for Continual Learning

  • Transformer Limitations:
    • Transformers excel at distilling internet knowledge and baking workflows via pre-training and RL, but they cannot adapt post-training without catastrophic forgetting (via fine-tuning) or severe data limits (via 20-minute in-context learning windows like Codex).
    • Real-world task distributions are messier and shift constantly; models trained in labs become obsolete if underlying events, codebases, or tools change.
  • Poor Computational Depth:
    • According to Rohan Anil, transformers are shallow (at most ~100 layers deep). While inference-time scaling (chain-of-thought token generation) adds computational depth, generating one token at a time autoregressively is highly inefficient.
  • The RL Disappointment:
    • Jerry Tworek shares that despite scaling up reinforcement learning at OpenAI expecting it to solve real-world tasks, evaluation benchmarks matched training tasks while real-world distributions remained unreached.

The Architectural & Optimization Vision

  • End-to-End Optimization:
    • Pre-training and RL must be optimized together end-to-end rather than treated as isolated phases, forming a cohesive learning procedure.
  • Optimization-Architecture Symbiosis:
    • Rohan Anil's work on the Shampoo optimizer demonstrates that stronger optimization algorithms enable the training of harder-to-optimize models, allowing researchers to explore deeper representations and alternative architectures (such as removing residual connections or utilizing N-gram memory embeddings).
  • Hardware Efficiency & Scale:
    • Any new architecture must run efficiently on physical hardware; architectural innovation without production-grade kernel generation is theoretically incomplete.

Why Start a New Lab?

  • Competitive Distractions at Big Labs:
    • Frontier labs are locked in a high-stakes coding-agent market race and rely on profitable transformers to win quarterly cycles, leaving little organizational bandwidth to pursue alternative architectures.
  • The Core Automation Approach:
    • By building a lab natively designed for agentic automation, the team aims to run deep learning stack experiments at high iteration speeds (targeting multiple experiments per day) rather than operating in slow, siloed research units.

The Roadmap: Automating Kernel Generation

  • The Hardware-Software Bottleneck:
    • Core Automation's immediate 6-month roadmap focuses on automating kernel generation to run custom matrix operations and linear algebra routines (such as QR factorization for the Shampoo optimizer) at peak TFLOPS on GPUs.
  • The Human Taste Gap:
    • While human-plus-search loops currently achieve significant speedups (e.g., a GPU Mode QR kernel competition where a top-tier human plus $100,000 in coding agent spending over 4 weeks yielded a 60x speedup), frontier models like Gemini, Claude, and OpenAI models cannot solve these complex multi-operator kernel problems autonomously. Core Automation aims to bridge this gap.

There's a story I tell about my first day in strategy 431 at Kellogg School of Management, the introductory class that every first-year MBA was required to take. I leaped through the readings and case studies and was dismayed that there weren't any tech companies on the docket. Me being me, I spoke to the professor after class wondering why, and was told that the goal of the course was not to necessarily learn about specific industries, but rather to uncover broadly applicable universal principles that could be applied to any company in any industry. I did not, as I usually tell the story, find this very satisfactory. To me, the nature of tech, particularly the fact that software and distribution had zero marginal costs and zero transaction costs, was something fundamentally different. Putting in zeros and formulas tends to wreak havoc. I soon realized, however, that that was my opportunity. The fundamental insight undergirding aggregation theory is that zero marginal costs lead to fundamentally different value chains than people once expected from the internet. It is centralization and scale in a world where controlling demand mattered more than distributing supply. What is fascinating about AI, however, is the extent to which those old universal principles are coming back to the forefront. That was never more apparent than this past weekend when arguments raged on X about the implications of Kimmy K3, another open weights model out of China, approaching the state-of-the-art in terms of capabilities. The long and short of it is this: Marginal costs are back in a big way, both in terms of short-term implications of state-of-the-art free models and in terms of the long-run structure of the industry. One of the most common misconceptions undergirding discussion of open weights models is that they are cheaper, free even. After all, you can just download the weights and skip the time and expense and capabilities necessary to create your own model. That is, of course, true, but the "free" in this case is a reference to the amount you need to spend on research and development. R&D is a fixed expense that is independent of the revenue you generate. If you spend $1 million in R&D, it doesn't matter if you do $100,000 in revenue or $100 million. You still spend $1 million on R&D. It does, of course, impact your profitability. What is related to revenue is COGS, cost of goods sold. And COGS is real for AI in a way it hasn't been for software for a very long time. Specifically, running inference on a model, whether that model be Kimmy or Fable, costs money. And the amount of money an AI provider spends on inference is, at least in most business models, directly correlated to revenue. To reuse the above example, generating $100 million versus $100,000 in revenue will likely require 1,000x COGS. In concrete terms, if it costs 50 cents to generate the tokens that drive $1 in revenue, then $100 million in revenue will have $50 million in COGS; $100,000 in revenue will only have $50,000 in COGS. The point in terms of open weights models is that they are not free to serve. Kimmy K3 costs $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens. That is cheaper than Sool’s $5 per million input tokens and $30 per million output tokens, but that might not even be the right measurement. NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang has described what NVIDIA is building as token factories. And from NVIDIA's perspective, that framing makes sense. NVIDIA GPUs are model agnostic. They generate tokens and do so in the fastest and most efficient way possible. That leads to measurements like tokens per second, time to first token, tokens per watt, token cost, etc. And Huang argues that these metrics will be the basis for decision-making. This is a framing that definitely made sense during the first paradigm of AI, the ChatGPT era, when tokens were delivered straight to the end user. The second paradigm of AI, however, the reasoning era, confounds this measurement. Reasoning entails an explosion in chain-of-thought tokens, and different models need different amounts of reasoning tokens to arrive at the right answer. Kimmy, for example, reportedly uses significantly more tokens than Sool, rendering its price advantage moot. Agents introduce a similar dynamic. Some models are more efficient than others in terms of the number of tokens they need to execute agentic workflows. What this means is that tokens are not a commodity. The defining characteristic of a commodity is that it is fungible. A gallon of oil is a gallon of oil. A ton of copper is a ton of copper. A bushel of wheat is a bushel of wheat. A token from one model, however, is not the same as a token from another model. What is fungible is what is constructed from tokens, which is to say intelligence. In other words, if both Kimmy and Sool generated the right answer, then that answer is fungible. The difference in tokens generated to get to that right answer is a contributor to a difference in COGS. The COGS for intelligence is a function of a few different factors. Model footprint: the weight and runtime state determines how much expensive memory and how many accelerators are required to host each serving replica. Inference efficiency: architectural choices, e.g., mixture of experts, reduce computation per generated token. Memory efficiency: architectural choices, e.g., MLA and Kimmy K3, reduce KV cache requirements, allowing more concurrent requests and better GPU utilization. Serving efficiency: batching, scheduling, prefix caching, and other inference optimizations maximize utilization and share work across requests. Token efficiency: the fewer tokens required to reach a correct answer, the lower the inference cost. The reason this matters is that we are rapidly approaching a state in which intelligence for many economically beneficial tasks is, in fact, a commodity. Anyone building a basic CRUD app, for example, can likely do so using models from multiple providers. And in a commodity market, the route to profitability is not through charging higher prices—again, you can or will soon be able to make the exact same app using multiple models—but rather through having a superior cost structure. It's worth stepping through the mechanics here because, as I noted a few months ago in "Amazon's Durability," the dynamics of commodity markets are not something people in tech are generally familiar with. In commodity markets, everyone charges the same price because everyone is selling the same thing. That price is determined by supply and demand. The demand for a commodity is a function of price elasticity. The cheaper the commodity, the more demand there is for it, and vice versa. The supply for a commodity is a function of the marginal cost of producing the commodity. The key thing to understand is that the marginal cost of producing the commodity differs by supplier. What this means in practice is that the supplier with the worst cost structure ends up selling the commodity at their marginal cost, if they can produce at all. The profits of everyone else depend on the extent to which their cost structure is better than the marginal supplier. As an example, Supplier A can produce 10 units of the commodity for $10 each. Supplier B can produce 10 units of the commodity for $15 each. Supplier C can produce 10 units of the commodity for $20 each. Let's assume the price elasticity is such that there is demand for 25 units of the commodity at $20. That means Supplier A will sell 10 units of the commodity for $20, earning $10 per unit. Supplier B will sell 10 units of the commodity for $20, earning $5 per unit. Supplier C will sell five units of the commodity for $20, earning $0 per unit. This isn't precisely right; the reason why Supplier C will bear the shortfall is because Suppliers A and B will be able to slightly undercut them in price, which will of course affect demand, which is elastic, but it makes the point: Supplier A has a great business, Supplier B has a good business, and Supplier C is going to go bankrupt. Bankruptcy risk is where fixed costs come back to the forefront. Supplier C has both fixed costs, like potentially R&D spend, and also may have taken on debt to finance the equipment necessary to produce the commodity. It can't price its commodity with these costs in mind. Remember, the market-clearing price approximates the marginal cost of the highest-cost unit needed to satisfy demand. But those costs can absolutely drive the supplier out of business. And if that supplier goes out of business, then prices go up until another supplier decides to enter or the other suppliers expand. Let's bring this back to models. Right now, none of the above analysis applies because demand exceeds supply for the frontier models and supply is limited by a lack of compute. This compute shortage doesn't just mean that a compute supplier like NVIDIA makes very large margins, but also that NVIDIA's customers like SpaceX AI can turn around and resell compute at high margins as well to a company like Anthropic. Anthropic, meanwhile, can pay the markup because they can sell tokens with a higher markup. Still, it's not just excess demand that gives Anthropic great margins. However, Anthropic and OpenAI likely have among the lowest cost per unit of frontier-quality intelligence thanks to model capability, serving scale, and token efficiency. They are serving models at a particular capability level for months before their competitors and are simultaneously applying the best models to optimizing those costs. It's also worth noting that the market is not yet treating intelligence like a commodity. Demand is for Anthropic and OpenAI specifically, and much less for models that aren't as good. Thus, SpaceX AI and Meta selling capacity to Anthropic. One way to think about the push for optimizing costs is that that is a function of defining jobs to be done by intelligence level such that intelligence buyers can create a market where intelligence is commoditized. In the long run, however, whoever is on the frontier is the best place to dominate non-frontier markets as well, which are just the frontier minus N months. I mean months in which the frontier model makers have been optimizing their cost of serving. All this is to say that I think the reaction to Kimmy and Chinese models generally is pretty overblown, at least from an economic perspective. Right now, there is a price umbrella that is downstream of the lack of compute. I highly doubt that Chinese models are cheaper to serve on a marginal cost basis. They just seem cheaper because Anthropic and OpenAI are so supply-constrained that they are charging far more than they would if there were sufficient supply to meet the demand for intelligence. Why then do the model makers in particular seem so panicked about Chinese models? First, I think the frontier labs are anchored in a world where training costs dominated their financial modeling. As long as training consumed more GPUs than inference, it was critical to maximize inference revenue to help fund the next training run, which meant charging very high prices for inference. Going forward, however, I expect the inference market to grow much faster than training costs, and that includes the assumption that training costs will continue to skyrocket, which means they really can make it up in volume. It wasn't clear this would be the case as recently as 8 months ago, but the agent paradigm unlock is so massive that frontier labs should have more confidence that they can not just survive but thrive with lower prices once they have sufficient compute. Second, intelligence isn't in fact a perfect commodity in part because applied intelligence makes itself smarter. Specifically, whoever is running inference is also collecting data and that data goes into making the next iteration of the model better. This is, on one hand, all the more reason for the frontier labs to lower prices and increase usage as more compute comes online. On the other hand, this is why companies like Microsoft are increasingly obsessed with helping companies run their own models. That is much more viable if Chinese models are a viable alternative. Third, the other way that frontier labs can not only differentiate from Chinese models but also from each other is by continuing to integrate up into the customer experience. It's striking the extent to which Claude and Codecs are proving to be quite sticky. Whichever harness you start working with is likely to be the one you stick with. And that figures to be even more the case with non-technical users. And in the long run, this imperative to move up the stack does mean that frontier models are absolutely a threat to software providers, including Microsoft. On the flip side, the extent to which software companies who currently own the customer experience have access to competitive models is the extent to which they may be able to resist the encroachment of the frontier labs. Finally, the ideological angle of Anthropic in particular is impossible to ignore. This is a company that believes only it can be entrusted with AI, and the existence of open weights alternatives strikes a fatal blow to that presumption. Kimmy isn't the only new Chinese model. The Sunday release came only days after startup Moonshot AI unveiled a powerful new offering that roiled markets and triggered concern in the US about China closing the gap on global leaders like Anthropic and OpenAI. Qwen 3.8-8B Max has 2.4 trillion parameters, joining Moonshot's Kimmy K3 in the heavyweight class with 2.8 trillion parameters. K3 rivals top offerings and Alibaba is setting similarly high expectations. Developers can now access Qwen 3.8-8B Max through Alibaba's coding platforms, including Coder. Interest in these "made in China" artificial intelligence systems and models is so high that Moonshot was forced to pause taking on new subscriptions late on Sunday to manage overwhelming demand. The fact that Qwen 3.8-8B Max will also have open weights is notable. Alibaba stopped releasing weights for its leading-edge models earlier this year but appears to have reverted that change. I suspect that shift was related to last week's Xi Jinping speech about AI that doubled down on the open weights approach: "We should adhere to the principle of openness and win-win and boost innovation-driven development. The strategy for China is obvious: Commoditize your complements. Note that he explicitly ties openness to AI "moving from the digital world into the physical world." The physical world is the world dominated by China, and the country's lead in areas like robotics is going to massively benefit from widely available AI models. Along the same lines, China does not want the US to gain an asymmetric advantage in AI. To the extent that China can weaken the US frontier labs while strengthening any and all potential US adversaries, so much the better. And it can benefit from the innovation that will attach itself to an open ecosystem. By the same token, don't expect China to do anything about distillation attacks on the frontier labs. I think it is mistaken to attribute all the success of Chinese labs to distillation, but it's just as much of a mistake to pretend that distillation doesn't give Chinese labs a big advantage. That advantage has really come to bear in the last year as post-training reinforcement learning has become increasingly crucial to model performance. Instead of having to fashion reinforcement learning environments from scratch, Chinese labs can simply use frontier labs' models as teachers, allowing for rapid improvement at much lower costs. This is not the only reason why Chinese models are cheaper to develop, but it's a big one. What is interesting is that one of the most important use cases for Chinese models in the West is itself distillation. Thinking Machines, for example, which just released an open-weights model, relies on Chinese models to solve the cold start problem for reinforcement learning. Dean Meyer and Constantine Buer wrote an excellent article on X explaining that distillation means that Western open-weights models are fundamentally disadvantaged relative to China. Distillation does not explain China's entire open model lead. Chinese labs have world-class researchers, substantial compute, strong pre-trained models, software-hardware co-design, and rapidly improving post-training capabilities. But distillation compresses the costly final gap between a strong base and a near-frontier system. Even if distillation represents a smaller share of a Chinese model's total capability, it represents a meaningful share of its advantage over American open models. New enforcement mechanisms will make large-scale distillation harder, slower, and more expensive for Chinese companies. However, enforcement will not eliminate distillation backed by state actors. Every Western frontier advance therefore creates another teacher for Chinese labs. Western builders must either reproduce those capabilities independently or wait to learn from Chinese models. This gap gives Chinese labs a recurring structural advantage over Western companies. This is a point that bears repeating because US open-weights model makers must follow the frontier labs' terms of service. They (1) are worse than Chinese alternatives, and (2) end up distilling the distillation, just with a detour through Chinese labs. Wouldn't it be better if Western open-weights model makers could go straight to the source? To that end, here's an even more interesting question around distillation: Why exactly is it bad? After all, what are large language models but the distillation of all the knowledge on the open internet scraped by the frontier labs and distilled into the models that are themselves being distilled? Who is exactly being wronged here? In fact, this paradox is the solution. I believe that open-weights models are good for innovation. And per the above, I think that labs in the frontier will be fine, but it's a problem to be dependent on China. The US should pass a law that (1) makes explicit that collecting data for training models is fair use, and (2) bars terms of service that forbid distillation for US companies at a minimum. Stopping distillation, which is literally just querying the API, is nearly impossible. The US should go the other way and lean into a new copyright policy that both indemnifies the labs and also guarantees that what they learn fuels further innovation for everyone else. This entire article has been an exercise in diffusing overreaction to Kimmy K3 specifically and Chinese open-weights models generally. However, there is one reason to be concerned and that is cyber security. Consider this story from the Stack: "Hugging Face said its production infrastructure was breached by an autonomous AI agent system early last week. The platform security team were initially stymied in their incident response (IR) by unnamed US LLM frontier model guardrails 'which cannot distinguish an incident responder from an attacker,' they said." So Hugging Face's defenders turned instead to the open-source GLM 5.2 model from China's Z.AI AI lab, running it on their own infrastructure to analyze the 17,000-plus logs or footprints that the attackers left behind. That's a striking public admission for the New York-headquartered Hugging Face, which lets users collaborate on models, datasets, and applications, and which this summer hit the $100 million ARR mark. In an incident report, the company recommended that defenders "Have a capable model you can run on your own infrastructure, vetted and ready before an incident, both to avoid guard lockout, and to keep attacker data and credentials from leaving your environment." It's difficult to overstate how wrongheaded the Trump administration's panicked response to Anthropic's release of Fable was, particularly since it exacerbated Anthropic's worst tendencies in terms of assuming only they can be trusted with powerful AI. In a world with only one AI, it might make sense to reserve the most powerful cyber security capabilities for the US government and trusted allies. However, that's not the world we live in. There are—and will be—models eminently capable of mounting cyber security attacks on existing infrastructure, and those models will be, and already are, widely available. The best defense, the only viable defense in fact, will be to make sure defenders have access to the best models as well. Right now, defenders are effectively banned from using Fable or Sool for cyber security because of Trump administration directives. That means the best alternative is using models from a country which has been trying to weaken our cyber defenses for years. This is insane. The better course is clear. First, loosen Fable and Sool restrictions on cyber security. And second, ensure that US open-weights model makers are on an equal playing field with China. Yes, the frontier labs will kick and scream about this, but the administration should realize that listening to their histrionics has led the US to a position where US companies are dependent on China for their defenses. Let the frontier labs win by being better. Don't let them define safety or security or pull up the ladder of humanity's collective knowledge. China is already hard enough to compete with. Letting them carry the standard for openness and innovation is simply giving away our biggest advantage.

Agentic AI is fundamentally transforming software development and organizational workflows, enabling exponential productivity gains, multi-agent orchestration, and autonomous execution.

Evolution of Development and Agentic Workflows

  • Transition to Autonomous Agents:
    • Early AI developer tools functioned as "fancy autocomplete," but scaling laws and increasing model intelligence (beginning broadly with Claude 3.5 and maturing with Opus 4 in May 2025) drove the shift to fully agentic workflows.
  • Orchestration Shift:
    • Instead of deterministic, step-by-step programming, users now provide models with a goal, starting point, and tools, letting the model coordinate its own multi-agent workflows.
  • Nested Sub-Agents:
    • Claude Code supports up to five layers of nested sub-agents, where agents can spawn and manage other agents to tackle massive workloads.
  • Real-World Scale Examples:
    • Large-scale code migrations that previously took months (such as a 10,000-line Scala to Java migration or migrating Bun from Zig to Rust) can now be accomplished in days using divide-and-conquer agent teams.
