## At The Money: Shopping in an Era of Surveillance Capitalism | Masters in Business

- Channel: Bloomberg Podcasts
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4mDpEnGQU04
- Published: 2026-11-18
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20261118.html#video-4mDpEnGQU04
- Speakers: Lindsay Owens (CEO, Groundwork Collaborative); Barry Ritholtz (Host, At The Money)

---

**TL;DR:** Fixed price tags—a 150-year-old retail norm—are disappearing as companies deploy algorithmic surveillance pricing, loyalty penalties, and subscription traps to maximize profits, leaving consumers to fight back through collective price-checking, public backlash, and emerging right-to-repair and click-to-cancel regulations.

### The End of the Fixed Price & Surveillance Pricing
* **Historical Context:** Lindsay Owens (CEO of Groundwork Collaborative and author of *Gouged*) notes that fixed price tags have existed for nearly 150 years, originating in the late 1800s with the Quakers and Philadelphia retailer John Wanamaker to replace haggling with fairness and transparency.
* **Algorithmic Exploitation:** Electronic shelf labels, algorithmic pricing, and personalized "surveillance pricing" are making uniform price tags obsolete, allowing companies to charge different customers different amounts for the exact same item.
* **Consumer Countermeasures:** While Owens argues lawmakers should ensure fair markets, she advises consumers to turn corporate experiments back on companies—such as having multiple people check airline ticket prices or Uber rides simultaneously and booking the cheapest option.

### Loyalty Penalties & Data Harvesting
* **The Loyalty Penalty:** Companies use initial perks to harvest consumer data, but once they determine a customer is a "sure thing," they stop offering discounts.
    * A *Washington Post* reporter who requested his Starbucks data received a 55-page report showing his perks disappeared once he became a regular.
    * A *Wired Magazine* reporter received a 500-page dossier from McDonald's revealing the company had calculated his "lifetime attrition score" as zero.
* **Monetizing Customer Data:** Retailers increasingly bundle and sell loyalty data. At Kroger, where 95% of transactions are tied to loyalty programs, its "alternative profits" business—driven largely by selling customer data—accounts for 30% of its net operating income.

### Drip Pricing, Dark Patterns, & Subscription Traps
* **Drip Pricing & Unbundling:** Companies lure shoppers with teaser rates and tack on fees throughout checkout (e.g., airlines charging extra for luggage and seat selection), aided by manipulative "dark patterns" in user interfaces.
    * **Regulatory Pushback:** An FTC rule finalized at the end of the Biden administration now prohibits drip pricing in short-term lodging (such as Airbnb and VRBO) and event ticketing, requiring all-in upfront pricing.
* **Subscription Traps ("Roach Motels"):** Barry Ritholtz notes that canceling subscriptions often requires hours of navigating broken customer service, leading him to rely on his bank to block un-cancelable charges as fraud. Owens recounts being trapped in negative-option billing with Columbia House Records in high school and being billed for a 3-month gifted toy subscription a year later.
* **Model Legislation:** Groundwork Collaborative released the *Shopper's Bill of Rights*, proposing 12 consumer protections, including the right to a refund on the original payment method without battling a chatbot for 3 hours, and one-click cancellation. New York City implemented a click-to-cancel rule roughly a week prior to the broadcast.

### Right to Repair & Corporate Overreach
* **A "Daisy Chain of Price Gouging":** Owens argues that restricting repairs forces consumers to pay high upfront prices, followed by proprietary parts, proprietary labor, and premature replacements.
    * **Corporate Examples:** Car manufacturers locking down engine control units; John Deere preventing farmers from repairing combines during harvest season; Taylor Commercial Food Services prohibiting McDonald's franchisees from fixing McFlurry machines; Keurig machines rejecting non-Keurig K-cups; and GE refrigerators blocking non-GE water filters.
    * **Printer Toner:** Ritholtz shares that he once paid roughly 5x the cost of an HP printer in toner cartridges—noting HP would "brick" printers using generic refills—before switching to Brother.
* **Advocacy Wins:** Nathan Proctor at the U.S. Public Interest Research Group (PIRG) is leading a growing right-to-repair movement, securing memorandums of understanding with John Deere and prompting federal action from the FTC.

### Public Backlash, Fair Pricing, & AI Shopping Assistants
* **The Power of Outrage:** Both Ritholtz and Owens emphasize that vocal consumer backlash and voting with one's wallet remain the fastest ways to discipline companies.
    * Ritholtz canceled his BMW deposit over proposed monthly subscriptions for heated seats.
    * Owens cites Coke walking back a mid-1990s plan to put thermometers in vending machines to hike prices on hot days, Delta walking back its Fetchr partnership after Groundwork Collaborative exposed it, and Instacart issuing a letter abandoning certain pricing practices two weeks after Groundwork's study.
* **Companies Doing It Right:** 
    * **Costco:** Uses a membership model and cost-plus pricing, capping markups at around 14% with no dynamic or personalized pricing.
    * **Savannah Bananas:** Uses a lottery system for tickets to keep prices fair, contrasting sharply with FIFA using dynamic pricing for World Cup tickets for the first time.
* **The AI Arms Race:** While consumer AI assistants like Muse promise to automate comparison shopping and subscription cancellations, Owens warns of "agentic commerce" risks. Two early meta-analyses of AI shopping assistants found that, more often than not, AI agents return *higher* prices even when asked to find lower ones—raising concerns about whether enterprise agents (like Walmart's Sparky or Meta's agents) work for the consumer or the corporation.
