## Is Walmart Backing America’s Next Financial Super-App? | Compound Interest

- Channel: Semafor
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T60ttyZVYBY
- Published: 2026-07-21
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20260721.html#video-T60ttyZVYBY
- Speakers: Omer Ismail (CEO, OnePay); Liz Hoffman (Host, Compound Interest); Rohan Nadkarni (Host, Compound Interest)

---

**TL;DR** 
One (formerly OnePay) is a Walmart-backed "financial super-app" leveraging a massive, built-in distribution network of 150 million monthly shoppers and 1.5 million employees to provide integrated banking, credit, and investment services with virtually zero customer acquisition costs.

### Scale and the "Super-App" Strategy
* **Growth Metrics:** In four years, One has reached 7 million monthly active customers and processes approximately $50 billion in annualized payment flow.
* **The "One-Stop Shop":** CEO Omer Ismail describes the platform as an "open-loop" financial ecosystem designed to help users manage money holistically, moving beyond the single-product focus of early fintechs.
* **Product Suite:** The app currently offers digital banking, credit building, high-yield savings, installment loans, credit cards, and investment tools (including crypto).
* **Usage Depth:** One focuses on "high-engagement" products—the "Google toothbrush test" of items used daily. Currently, users average more than two features per person, with a goal of reaching five or six.

### The Distribution Moat: Walmart’s Ecosystem
* **Zero CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost):** While most fintechs struggle with high marketing costs, One acquires customers for free through Walmart’s existing digital and physical infrastructure.
* **Associate Integration:** Walmart employees (associates) can sign up for One in "20 seconds" during HR onboarding. Walmart shares data (with permission) to pre-fill applications, creating the fastest onboarding in the industry.
* **Point-of-Sale Acquisition:** Shoppers on Walmart.com are prompted to use One for installment loans at checkout, converting retail traffic directly into banking customers.
* **Open-Loop Ambitions:** While Walmart is the primary anchor, One is expanding into other employer ecosystems (e.g., UKG and Workday) to reach an additional 30–40 million employees.

### Operational Structure and Partnerships
* **Independent Governance:** Though Walmart is the majority owner, One operates as an independent company outside the "four walls" of the retail giant. This structure was designed by Walmart CEO Doug McMillon and the board (including OpenAI CFO Sarah Friar) to ensure agility and talent retention.
* **Asset-Light Model:** One is not a bank. It utilizes a partner-banking model to avoid the heavy regulatory and capital requirements of a banking license:
    * **Banking Partners:** Coastal Community Bank and Lead Bank.
    * **Credit & Loans:** Partnered with Klarna (installment loans) and Synchrony (credit cards).
    * **Brokerage:** Partnered with DriveWealth for clearing and execution.
* **Data Integration:** The platform uses "North Star" principles to never ask a customer for information the ecosystem already possesses, using Walmart’s data to personalize financial offers.

### Competitive Positioning and Market Outlook
* **Lessons from Goldman Sachs’ "Marcus":** Ismail, who previously led Marcus, noted that One is different because it focuses on high-frequency "front door" products (banking/credit) rather than niche, "set-it-and-forget-it" products like high-yield savings or personal loans.
* **The "Rabbit" Culture:** Ismail describes the company culture via a fable where a wolf hunts a rabbit: "The wolf was running for his dinner; the rabbit was running for its life." This emphasizes the urgency of a startup despite having a Fortune 1 backer.
* **Market Share:** Despite the rise of fintech, legacy banks still hold the vast majority of the market. Ismail argues that One’s distribution edge and multi-product integration allow it to compete where other "point-solution" fintechs have hit a ceiling.

### Notable Quotes
> "We have the ability to acquire customers at zero CAC [Customer Acquisition Cost] through the Walmart ecosystem... it’s not just distribution that’s a competitive moat, it's also the ability to use data with the customer's permission to offer them products catered to them." — **Omer Ismail, CEO of One**

> "I am a big believer that distribution beats content. You don’t have to have a perfect product—you have to have a good product, but you have to get it to people in a way that makes sense for them and that is cheap for you." — **Liz Hoffman, Semafor Business & Finance Editor**

> "One big lesson is structure... [One] is not inside of [Walmart's] four walls... which has allowed us to be able to get the business to the stage where it's at, with the right team and the right incentives in place." — **Omer Ismail, CEO of One**


## What Happens To Baby Lawyers When AI Does Their Jobs? | Compound Interest

- Channel: Semafor
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m0TSvHABD-A
- Published: 2026-07-14
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20260714.html#video-m0TSvHABD-A
- Speakers: Rachel Proffitt (Partner and CEO, Cooley); Liz (Host, Compound Interest); Rohan (Host, Compound Interest)

---

**TL;DR**
The traditional "Big Law" business model is facing a structural reckoning as AI automates the high-margin "grunt work" of junior associates, forcing a transition from the billable hour toward value-based pricing and a talent model that prioritizes strategic EQ over manual execution.

### The Implosion of the Billable Hour
* **Value-Based Pricing:** Rachel Proffitt (CEO, Cooley) suggests AI provides an opportunity to realign incentives through "value-based" or "outcome-based" pricing, similar to investment banking fees, rather than traditional "time and materials" billing.
* **Client Pressures:** Corporate General Counsels are increasingly unwilling to pay $1,500/hour for junior associate tasks that AI can perform faster and more accurately.
* **The "Wachtell" Model:** While the industry has resisted the death of the billable hour for decades, Proffitt notes that elite firms may move toward a "services rendered" model where clients pay for specialized expertise and execution certainty rather than six-minute increments.

### The Workforce "Sorting" Crisis
* **The Shrinking Pyramid:** Cooley currently operates with a relatively low leverage model (approx. 400 partners to 1,000 associates). Proffitt acknowledges that while the quantity of lawyers may change, the *nature* of their work will shift away from "laboring oars" (execution) toward strategic design.
* **Merit-Based Sorting:** AI may act as an earlier filter for talent. Firms might identify "superstars"—those with high EQ, technology fluency, and networking skills—by year two or three, rather than year seven or eight. 
* **The Apprenticeship Gap:** A significant industry concern is how "baby lawyers" will become seasoned partners if the "grunt work" they traditionally "cut their teeth on" is fully automated. The "watch and learn" model is being disrupted by the lack of manual tasks for juniors to observe.

### AI Integration and Native Competition
* **AI Wrappers vs. Proprietary Tech:** Cooley is experimenting with "legal wrappers" like Harvey and Leya (referred to in transcript as Legora) but anticipates firms will eventually build proprietary models trained on their own data to maintain a competitive edge.
* **The "Cooley Go" Approach:** For lower-stakes work, such as startup fundraisings, Proffitt envisions "firm-branded interfaces" or portals where clients interact with AI bots for routine tasks, preserving human intervention for "bet-the-company" moments.
* **New Competitors:** Proffitt expresses "healthy fear" regarding "AI-native" law firms—startups built without legacy baggage or the need to protect a $1,500/hour associate billing model.

### The Resilience of the "Trust" Economy
* **The Limits of Automation:** Proffitt argues that for high-stakes litigation and transformative M&A, clients buy based on **Brand and Trust**. These "bet-the-company" decisions are unlikely to be handed over entirely to AI because the "relational element" is the ultimate insurance policy for a board of directors.
* **Talent Consolidation:** The industry is seeing a "revolving door" of elite talent moving for massive guarantees. Proffitt notes this "NFL/NBA-style" talent war is disruptive and may eventually break the current economic model unless firms shift to value-based pricing.
* **Non-Lawyer Ownership:** While current regulations prevent private equity from owning law firms, the commercial pressure of AI may eventually force a reconsideration of these rules to allow for the massive capital investment required for high-end technology.

> "Most people have just been sort of 'why fix what isn't broken?' But this may force the conversation to really ask yourselves, is it in fact not broken?" — **Rachel Proffitt, CEO of Cooley**

> "I can't—unless I want to go to a total dystopian state—I can’t say [the workforce] goes to zero because we’re never going to get to a place of only robots. That’s where the trust and relationship element breaks down." — **Rachel Proffitt, CEO of Cooley**

> "If I were starting any business model from scratch on AI, I might pick a law firm. It feels incredibly ripe." — **Liz Hoffman, Semafor Business and Finance Editor**
