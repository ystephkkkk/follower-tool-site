## Reverse-Engineering the AI Buyer — Aliisa Rosenthal, Acrew Capital

- Channel: AI Engineer
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wdTRsfw0KG0
- Published: 2026-08-26
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20260826.html#video-wdTRsfw0KG0
- Speakers: Aliisa Rosenthal (Partner, Acrew Capital)

---

**TL;DR:** OpenAI grew enterprise revenue to several billion dollars by doing the go-to-market sequence backward—building an expensive enterprise tier before self-serve, which cannibalized sales. Founders should invert this: automate the go-to-market machine first using modern tools, launch self-serve upfront, avoid pilots, and only hire humans (starting with AI-native talent) to solve specific bottlenecks.

### 1. Go-to-Market Sequencing & The OpenAI Origin Story
- **Early Growth Metrics:** Aliisa Rosenthal joined OpenAI a little over four years ago when revenue was at a couple million and powered by a self-serve API for developers; she helped scale the organization to several hundred people and several billion in enterprise revenue.
- **The Backward Enterprise Rollout:** 
    - Launched ChatGPT in late 2022 with zero enterprise features (no SSO, NDA, or invoicing).
    - Begged the technical team for enterprise features for **9 months** while large enterprises bombarded the sales team with inbound requests.
    - Shipped an expensive, fast, high-end enterprise product targeting up-market buyers first.
- **The Cannibalization Lesson:** Shipped a self-serve version 4 months later in January 2024; it grew exponentially faster, cannibalized the enterprise tier, frustrated sales reps who had to compete with self-serve, and proved that mid-market/SMB self-serve was higher-growth than heavy enterprise.
- **Corrected Playbook:** For all subsequent OpenAI product launches, the team enforced a self-serve first motion, learned what features were missing, and only then tacked on human-led enterprise tiers.

### 2. Automating the Go-to-Market Machine
- **"Build the Machine First":** Traditional sales leaders hire large teams (BDRs, SDRs, RevOps, sales engineers) and augment with AI later; founders should do the exact opposite by automating go-to-market workflows first and adding humans only at bottlenecks.
- **Inbound Management & Data Capture:** 
    - OpenAI faced a tidal wave of 10,000 inbounds per day with just 4 sales reps.
    - **Signup Form Optimization:** Add in-depth fields (like phone numbers, even if optional) to enable future AI or human outreach.
    - **Automated Acknowledgment:** Send immediate automated waitlist/feedback emails to inbound prospects so they feel heard; missing this at OpenAI led prospects to buy Microsoft Copilot instead.
- **Outbound Automation:** Leverage modern tooling (like Clay and Nooks) to automate research, sequencing, and dialing so reps focus exclusively on qualified leads and high-value conversations.

### 3. Removing Buyer Friction & Eliminating Pilots
- **Ease the Buyer's Journey:** Never give buyers homework or tell them to "go do five things and come back"—do the heavy lifting via live Zoom sessions, in-office hackathons, or direct hand-holding to maintain control of the sales cycle.
- **Streamlined Buying Process:** Keep pricing options simple, paperwork fast, and approval flows manageable.
- **Avoiding "Pilot Hell":** 
    - Treat pilots/POCs as rare exceptions for high-value revenue opportunities rather than the default, because they drain resources and require restarting the sales cycle.
    - **Pilot Alternatives:** Bypass POC objections by introducing them to another customer, running evaluations on part of their data via Zoom, offering a 90-day opt-out clause in a signed contract, or writing a 1-year POC instead of a standard 3-year agreement.
- **Security Automation:** Combat security bottlenecks by building trust portals where buyers can auto-sign NDAs, access pen tests, and use AI tools to auto-fill security questionnaires.

### 4. Pricing Evolution
- **The Seat-Pricing Mistake:** ChatGPT Enterprise launched at **$60 per user per month** (priced based on serving costs before market comparables like Copilot, Gemini, and Anthropic existed).
- **The Seat Ceiling:** High per-seat pricing restricted adoption, as companies only purchased licenses for narrow subsets of teams (e.g., developers or investors) rather than whole companies.
- **The Usage-Based Pivot:** OpenAI lowered the barrier to entry by shifting to a flat base license fee coupled with usage-based pricing, causing adoption to spread like wildfire.
- **Controlling Usage Costs:** Counter customer fears of spiraling usage bills by providing spending cap dashboards per month or per employee.

### 5. Hiring Humans and Compensation
- **Timing the Human Layer:** Hire humans only when hitting ceilings in automated processes where buyers explicitly demand human contact, trust-building, or up-market navigation.
- **Resisting Premature Upmarket Pull:** Avoid chasing massive enterprise deals ($1M to $10M) too early, as they consume disproportionate legal, security, product, engineering, and sales resources.
- **Profile of Early Sales Hires:** Hire AI-native account executives who live and breathe AI, practice what they preach, and are motivated by equity and building the company rather than W-2 cash.
- **The "Revenge of the Steak Dinner":** As automation handles outbound, demos, and security, buyers increasingly crave in-person interaction; effective, low-cost human-led strategies include conferences, booths, and dinners.
- **Compensation Plans:** OpenAI operated for years without giving out formal commission comp plans (relying on equity and company momentum), but founders eventually need variable comp to attract coin-operated enterprise sellers. Simpler comp structures with out-performer upside work best.

### 6. Q&A Insights: PLG vs. POCs, FDEs, and Design Partners
- **PLG Access vs. POCs:** Product-led growth (self-serve access) is distinct from high-touch POCs; PLG funnels can be safely feature-gated, time-boxed, or consumption-limited without requiring heavy sales engineering support.
- **Forward-Deployed Engineers (FDEs):** 
    - FDEs are exceptionally difficult to hire (noting roughly 55,000 open job requisitions against a talent pool of about 5,000 qualified people).
    - While expensive and scarce, FDEs make products deeply sticky because customized integrations bind the customer to the tool long-term.
- **First 10 Design Partners:** 
    - Early design partner engagements should rely on existing relationships, investor introductions, or warm connections.
    - Target companies with strong logos by having them pilot the product on internal, non-production workflows to bypass heavy security hurdles while securing a valuable reference logo.
