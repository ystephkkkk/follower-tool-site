## 211. How one small American manufacturer is dealing with Trump’s tariffs

### TL;DR
When hit with sudden tariff spikes of up to 125%–145% in 2025, small Massachusetts seat cushion manufacturer Klear Vu could not instantly swap its specialized Chinese fabric suppliers or easily pass costs to retail giants like Walmart, exposing how taxing raw material inputs harms domestic manufacturing competitiveness.

### Company Background & Operations
- **Business Identity:** **Klear Vu Corporation:** A 60-year-old, three-generation family-owned company in Fall River, Massachusetts, with 50-something employees operating a union factory that provides health care and 401(k) benefits.
- **Leadership:** Run by co-owner and Senior Vice President of Business Development Sam Cooper and his brother.
- **Core Product:** **The Gripper Chair Cushion:** Invented by Sam's father in the 1990s, this cushion features a built-in gripper material on the bottom instead of ties; it accounts for over 80% of total sales.
- **Major Retail Customers:** Walmart, Kohl's, Meijer, Amazon, and Wayfair; home goods purchasing by these retailers is typically planned 18 to 24 months in advance.

### Supply Chain & Input Dependencies
- **Fabric Sourcing:** **China:** Upholstery fabric is primarily imported from Chinese mills because they offer unmatched selection, deep specialties down the chain (from dyes to specialized yarns), consistency, competitive pricing, and efficient production of specific textures like chenille and woven patterns.
- **Domestic Sourcing:** **North Carolina:** Klear Vu does source some upholstery fabric and domestic gripper material from the United States, but options are much more limited.
- **Alternative Sourcing Limitations:** **Non-China Mills:** Exploring suppliers in India, Vietnam, Turkey, and North Carolina proved difficult due to lack of specific styles (e.g., India lacking chenille specialization), shipping delays, loose ends, or prohibitive costs (Turkish fabric costing triple the price). Developing new fabric lines takes years, not days.

### Impact of Tariffs (2018–2025)
- **First Trump Administration (2018–2019):** Section 301 tariffs hit fabric inputs unevenly—chenille was taxed at 7%, while other upholstery fabrics faced a 25% rate. 
- **Inverted Tariff Penalty:** Raw fabric inputs were often tariffed at higher rates than fully finished cushions imported from abroad, discouraging domestic cutting and sewing jobs in the US.
- **2025 Tariff Escalation:** 
  - February 2025 brought initial 10% tariffs, followed by another 10% in March.
  - April 2025 brought a **125%** tariff increase on China (effectively a trade embargo), later escalating to **145%**, while broader Liberation Day tariffs hit all countries.
  - Later in 2025, countries like India were hit with tariffs adding up to 50%.
- **Operational Disruption:** Klear Vu halted shipments in production, held less inventory, walked closer to delivery deadlines, experienced a sales freeze/dip, and had to mandate weekend and overtime shifts to meet certain orders. Front-loading inventory was deemed too dangerous due to massive upfront cash outlays, storage limits, and the risk of un-sold stock.
- **Supplier & Customer Negotiations:** Chinese suppliers could only offer single-digit percentage cost-sharing discounts because they operate on tight margins and sell globally. Klear Vu delayed raising prices as long as possible before eventually securing modest price increases from major retail partners.

### Legal Challenges & Broader Business Pressures
- **Supreme Court Case (February 2026):** Addressed the legality of President Trump's tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). Klear Vu hired an attorney to pursue tariff refunds owed by the government, though ultimate recovery remained uncertain.
- **External Shocks:** Emerging challenges included potential supply chain ripple effects from the Iran war, such as temporary chemical supply glitches for US gripper-makers and higher energy-driven input costs from Chinese suppliers.
- **Overall Business Costs:** Tariffs compounded existing non-tariff pressures of running an American manufacturing business, including high domestic energy, insurance, and labor costs.
