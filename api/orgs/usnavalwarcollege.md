### TL;DR
Wars are easy to start and difficult to end on desired terms, largely driven by the tension between limited versus unlimited objectives, geographic realities, and whether conflicts inadvertently escalate into global wars or trigger costly insurgencies.

### The Russo-Japanese War: A Model of War Termination
- **Asymmetric Conflict:** Russia heavily outweighed Japan in territory, population, industrial base, resource endowment, and army/navy size, yet Japan successfully secured its war aims.
- **Limited Objectives:** Both sides fought for limited objectives rather than regime change; Japan's primary goal was getting Russia out of Korea and Manchuria, which it achieved alongside gaining railway concessions and the southern half of Sakhalin Island.
- **Clausewitzian Concepts:** 
  - **Culminating Point of Attack:** Operational terms applied to a battle or campaign (e.g., Japan taking 203 Meter Hill to spot and sink the Russian fleet at Port Arthur). Exceeding it risks a strong reaction.
  - **Culminating Point of Victory:** Pertains to the entire war. Indicators that a victory point has been passed include third-party intervention or exhaustion of resources.
  - **The Bologna Flask Analogy:** Clausewitz's description of war termination where an enemy either acts like a hammer or shatters like a flask upon being scratched.
- **Alternative Culminating Points of Victory for Russia:**
  - **Domestic Implosion:** The 1905 Russian Revolution and the unrest following "Bloody Sunday" that forced Tsar Nicholas II to establish a weak legislature.
  - **The Battle of Mukden:** The massive land battle where Japan exhausted its last reserves against a rapidly expanding Russian troop transport rate via the Trans-Siberian Railway (scaling from 20,000–40,000 troops/month early on to 100,000/month by the end).
  - **Financial Ruin:** Russia's inability to secure foreign loans after Mukden, whereas Japan successfully lowered interest rates and financed its war effort.
  - **The Battle of Tsushima:** The lopsided naval battle where Russia lost its entire Baltic fleet while Japan commandeer-captured ships.
  - **Territorial Loss:** Japan's seizure of Sakhalin Island, which President Roosevelt suggested using as a bargaining chip.
- **Diplomatic Exit Strategy:** Japan deliberately utilized the United States to mediate the Portsmouth Peace Treaty, employing Harvard alumni (Viscount Kaneko and Baron Komura) to engage Theodore Roosevelt when Field Marshal Yamagata recognized Japan was out of men.

### Limited vs. Unlimited Objectives and Strategic Success
- **Gulf War (Gulf War I) — The Model Success:** 
  - **Objectives:** George H.W. Bush utilized National Security Directive 54 to enforce limited, clear objectives: force Iraq to withdraw from Kuwait, restore the Kuwaiti government, and promote regional security.
  - **Financial Efficiency:** The United States did not pay for the war; 34 coalition allies sent over 200,000 troops, paid the US over **$50 billion** for troop costs, and gave an additional **$14 billion** to coalition partners.
  - **Casualties:** Iraqi military deaths reached **20,000 to 35,000** (plus up to 3,000 civilian bombing deaths), while coalition forces suffered only **392 total deaths** (**258** Americans) out of roughly 1 million belligerents. Iraq was completely diplomatically isolated.
- **Korean War — The Cost of Shifting Aims:**
  - **Geography:** Featured a short 250 km land border in the South, but an expansive 1,400+ km northern border with China, ensuring uninterruptible supply lines through Manchuria.
  - **Waffling Objectives:** The US suffered from shifting aims—moving from a limited objective (defending the South) to an unlimited objective (reuniting the entire peninsula), which triggered a devastating third-party Chinese intervention and caused high casualties and a permanently divided peninsula.
- **Sun Tzu's "Death Ground":** 
  - Ground where an army fights with the courage of desperation because there is no alternative. Unlimited objectives (like regime change or annihilation) place adversaries on death ground, turning failed states into lethal, highly resilient adversaries (e.g., Ukraine).

### Pathologies of War: Escalation and Insurgency
- **Pathology 1: Regional Wars Going Global**
  - **American Revolution:** Limited for the 13 colonies, unlimited for London (seeking to eliminate the Continental Army/Congress), which dragged out the conflict and allowed Britain's rivals to join (France in **1778**, Spain in **1779**, attacking the Dutch in **1780**).
  - **World War I:** Multiple powers entered with divergent, unaligned objectives (Britain wanted status quo, France wanted Alsace-Lorraine, Italy and Austria-Hungary wanted the Balkans, Russia wanted Catholic Austria-Hungary out, Germany wanted colonies), making a unified settlement impossible.
  - **World War II in Asia:** U.S. Smoot-Hawley Tariffs deepened the Great Depression, driving Japan toward autarky and expansion into Manchuria and China. Japan's total-conquest strategy put China on death ground, and when Japan attacked Western assets on **December 7–8** (Pearl Harbor, Malaya, Philippines, Singapore, Wake Island, Guam, Thailand, Shanghai), it brought in a massive Allied coalition rather than deterring them.
- **Pathology 2: Conventional Victory vs. Endless Insurgency**
  - **Victory-Denial Strategy:** Defeated conventional armies shift to guerrilla warfare and insurgencies, imposing massive ongoing security and financial costs on the occupier.
  - **Iraq War (Gulf War II):** Unlike Gulf War I, this involved an unlimited objective (regime change in Baghdad). While removing Saddam was easy, Iraq's mosaic society fractured along ethnic and sectarian lines over national identity rather than just state institutions. Guarding a **2,000+ mile** border against regional ambitions from Saudi Arabia, Iran, and Turkey proved nearly impossible to stabilize.
