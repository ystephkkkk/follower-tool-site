**TL;DR:** Pat Gelsinger explains that while AI tools are accelerating chip design, manufacturing scale, memory bandwidth, and power constraints remain massive bottlenecks. Overcoming these physical limits—such as transitioning to optical I/O, 800V DC power, and next-gen memory—will dictate the future of AI hardware and economic capacity.

### Chip Design & The AI Shift
* **Historical Parallels:** **Pat Gelsinger** noted that modern AI chip design mirrors the 486 era, where Intel pioneered high-level HDL design and EDA tools because automated solutions didn't yet exist.
* **Analog Design Limitations:** **Pat Gelsinger** pointed out that while AI handles logic functions well, analog components like Serdes still require massive silicon data and remain difficult to automate via AI.
* **Consolidation of Architectures:** **Pat Gelsinger** predicted that the current explosion of roughly 100 specialized inference accelerators will inevitably consolidate due to shifting workloads, capital market realities, and major labs picking winning hardware-software co-designs.

### Manufacturing & Packaging Bottlenecks
* **Scale-Up Timeline:** **Pat Gelsinger** emphasized that despite a 3-month design cycle, getting real silicon to scale still takes 9 months of processing time, plus another 3 months for advanced 3D packaging.
* **Die Stacking Limits:** **Pat Gelsinger** argued against extreme 16- or 32-high memory stacks due to exponential defect and yield challenges, projecting that 2- or 4-layer mid-rises will remain the sweet spot.
* **Optical Networking:** **Pat Gelsinger** reaffirmed that copper has reached its limit, predicting that scale-up environments will aggressively adopt optical I/O and optical circuit switching (OCS) by 2028–2029 to handle massive GPU clusters.

### Memory Innovation
* **The HBM Dilemma:** **Pat Gelsinger** described High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) as a "hideous memory" with poor bit density, thermal issues, and shoreline bandwidth limits, yet acknowledged it is currently the best available option.
* **30-Year Drought:** **Pat Gelsinger** noted that despite widespread industry attempts (including Intel's Optane), the industry has produced zero major new memory architectures in the last 30 years.
* **Emerging Breakthroughs:** **Pat Gelsinger** expressed excitement about upcoming memory innovations driven by AI capital—specifically highlighting vertical stack integration, new materials like ferroelectrics, and stealth startups tackling high-density non-capacitive memories.

### Power Capacity & Grid Constraints
* **Energy Equals Economic Capacity:** **Pat Gelsinger** stated that in the AI digital age, energy capacity equates to economic capacity, warning that U.S. national energy capacity flatlined for 10 to 15 years while coal plants were retired.
* **Data Center Defaults:** **Pat Gelsinger** predicted an increase in defaults on data center projects because the required energy supply will fail to materialize, serving as an upper bound on AI growth.
* **Power Delivery Innovations:** **Pat Gelsinger** highlighted the shift toward 800-volt DC data centers, vertical GaN technology for single-step power conversion (800V to 5V/12V), and solid-state transformers to eliminate conversion inefficiencies.

### Virtualization & Agent-Driven Infrastructure
* **Redefining Virtual Machines:** **Pat Gelsinger** (drawing on his VMware tenure) discussed how infrastructure virtualization must be reinvented to serve **agent swarms** rather than humans.
* **Agent-Centric Management:** **Guido Appenzeller** and **Pat Gelsinger** noted that future infrastructure must feature rapid startups, agent-level security profiles, policy guardrails, and concepts like "V Motion for agents," while humans manage high-level constitutions and dashboards.

### Predictions & Forward Calls

## AI Has a Memory Problem | Gelsinger, SK hynix & More | RAISE Summit 2026

- Channel: RAISE Summit
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NsdT-0bmltg
- Published: 2026-07-22
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20260722.html#video-NsdT-0bmltg
- Speakers: Pat Gelsinger (Partner, Playground Global); Hoshik Kim (SVP & Fellow, SK hynix); Vijay Shilpiekandula (Investor, Dilation Capital)

---

**TL;DR:** AI demand is outstripping memory supply, creating a "memory wall" bottleneck where high-bandwidth memory (HBM) is thermally and bitwise inefficient yet remains the necessary stopgap until advanced stacked architectures arrive later this decade.

### The AI Memory Bottleneck: Why Memory is the Limit
* **Insatiable Token Demand:** **Pat Gelsinger** noted a threefold problem: insatiable demand for AI, rapidly growing context windows and model sizes, and the necessity for extremely low first-token latency.
* **The von Neumann Architectural Tax:** **Vijay Shilpiekandula** explained that modern AI inherits an 80-year-old von Neumann architecture where processors sit far from memory, creating a narrow highway (data bottleneck) between the computational "factory" and the memory "warehouse."
* **Systemic Memory Shift:** **Hoshik Kim** stated that memory is no longer viewed merely as a commodity cost, but as a core investment and foundational infrastructure for AI data centers.

### HBM's Limitations and the "Waffle Maker" Production Problem
* **HBM Imperfections:** **Pat Gelsinger** called HBM a "lousy memory" that is thermally constrained (creating a thermal sandwich), shoreline bandwidth-limited, and bitwise inefficient (sacrificing four bits of traditional DRAM for every HBM bit created).
* **The "Waffle Squeeze":** **Andrew Homan** used a waffle maker analogy to illustrate that manufacturing HBM is significantly more complex and slower than commodity DRAM, severely limiting output.
* **Compound Yield Losses:** **Hoshik Kim** explained that HBM requires significantly larger die spaces (roughly three times bigger, known as "HBM squeeze") and stacks DRAM 12 to 16 levels high, resulting in compounding yield losses across front-end wafers, TSV stacking, and micro-bumping.

### Industry Consolidation and Changing Business Models
* **Survival of the Fittest:** **Louis Delahaye** highlighted that the memory industry has consolidated from roughly 20 players down to three primary leaders: SK hynix, Samsung, and Micron.
* **Custom Designs vs. Commodities:** **Andrew Homan** pointed out that tight co-optimization between logic (GPUs) and memory makes suppliers harder to swap, moving HBM away from a pure commodity. **Pat Gelsinger** added that the shift toward custom stacked memory designs will disrupt traditional JEDEC-governed business models.

### Geopolitical Realities and Supply Chain Vulnerabilities
* **Concentrated Geopolitics:** **Vijay Shilpiekandula** noted that front-end memory is concentrated in Korea, packaging is in Taiwan, and NAND is in Japan. 
* **Lack of Resilience:** **Pat Gelsinger** warned that the tech supply chain lacks resilience, noting that a three-week blockade of the Taiwan Strait would brown out the industry, and observed that building a major semiconductor factory takes about four years and roughly six years to achieve profitability.

### Predictions & Forward Calls
* **Breakthrough Memory Architectures:** **Pat Gelsinger** predicts that novel memory architectures (such as stacked memory directly on or integrated into compute layers by companies like Dmatrix, Next Silicon, and acquisitions like Nvidia's purchase of Grock) will achieve 10x to 50x better bandwidth than shoreline-limited HBM, fundamentally changing the industry by the end of the decade.
* **Persistent Shortages:** **Pat Gelsinger** and **Andrew Homan** assert that supply limits and strong demand for HBM will persist for several more years, with no quick fix for supply constraints regardless of capital investments.
* **Long-Term Contracting:** **Hoshik Kim** notes that memory makers are shifting away from pure commodity models toward contract-based, long-term agreements lasting five years or more.

### Notable Quotes
> "HBM is a lousy memory... You're stacking DRAM. DRAM doesn't like to be stacked because essentially you're creating a thermal sandwich... It is just the best one we have right now." 
> — *Pat Gelsinger*

> "We semis investors sleep like a baby... but we wake up every 3 hours to check what's going on in the Asian markets." 
> — *Vijay Shilpiekandula*

> "Memory is no longer a cost anymore; we see memory as an investment to AI infrastructure." 
> — *Hoshik Kim*

---

### TL;DR
As AI workloads evolve rapidly beyond standard transformers toward agentic, continuous-learning, and multi-dimensional physical models, fixed ASICs face obsolescence, necessitating software-driven, flexible architectures that can balance high-performance computing (HPC) and AI on a unified chip.

### The Convergence of HPC and AI Workloads
* **Unified Mathematical Principles:** 
  * **Elad Raz:** Emphasizes that at their foundation, HPC (fluid dynamics, security, defense, physical simulation) and AI share identical mathematical principles, with AI workloads actually being simpler.
  * **Shared Code Execution:** NextSilicon’s Maverick accelerator was designed with a data-flow architecture for parallel code sections, complemented by an integrated RISC-V CPU to accelerate serial code.
* **Legacy Compatibility:** 
  * **Diverse Software Support:** NextSilicon's architecture is agnostic to MLOps frameworks, running code written in Triton, CUDA, C, PyTorch, and even legacy Fortran used extensively in supercomputing and scientific workloads.

### The Renaissance of the CPU in the AI Era
* **Shifting Compute Ratios:** 
  * **Pat Gelsinger:** Notes that historical CPU-to-GPU ratios (1 CPU to 8 or 16 GPUs) are shifting toward 1-to-2 or even 1-to-1 ratios to handle agentic multi-model environments and workload affinity.
* **Workload-Driven Design:** 
  * **Architecture Follows Function:** Gelsinger stresses that computing has never been about underlying architecture, but rather how workloads are executed across the software-hardware stack.
* **Agentic Tools Integration:** 
  * **Elad Raz:** Points out that as LLMs execute tasks using multiple agentic tools and generate codebases for physical simulations, local memory retention on a single chip running both AI and physical simulation is critical.

### The Flaws of Specialized ASICs and Fixed Architectures
* **The Danger of Single-Purpose Chips:** 
  * **Elad Raz:** Criticizes the trend of building custom ASICs exclusively for specific architectures (such as the Llama transformer), noting that rapid innovations in MLOps—like attention heads, multi-latent attention, sparse attention, and Mamba models operating in frequency space—render single-purpose hardware obsolete.
* **Absorbed Specialization:** 
  * **Pat Gelsinger:** Draws historical parallels to floating-point and media processors, which initially emerged as specialized units before being absorbed into general machine architecture as workloads evolved.
* **Avoiding Compute Islands:** 
  * Carving workloads up into isolated specialized zones (such as splitting training farms from pre-fill and decode) creates unscalable "islands of compute."

### NextSilicon’s Architecture and Industry Validation
* **Development Milestones:** 
  * **Elad Raz:** Reports that NextSilicon spent 8 years and completed 4 tape-outs. Their first generation is deployed in national laboratories, and their second generation is in production and installed at the rack level with Department of Energy (DOE) entities.
* **The Arbel RISC-V Spin-Off:** 
  * Spun off their internal RISC-V chip (named Arbel) from the Maverick accelerator to create a best-in-class silicon core for high-performance serial code execution alongside data-flow parallel acceleration.
* **Capital and Innovation Costs:** 
  * **Elad Raz:** Notes that every chip generation costs approximately $150 million to develop, requiring distinct architectural advantages and early testing with scale customers.

### Predictions & Forward Calls
* **Architectural Agnosticism Requirement:** **Elad Raz** states that because chip design takes 3 years and future models cannot be accurately predicted, hardware must be built as an architecture capable of accelerating whatever comes next.
* **Evolution of AI Models:** **Pat Gelsinger** predicts that models will transition from today's two-dimensional transformers into three- and four-dimensional models to accommodate molecular structures and physical AI.
* **Decaying Boundaries:** **Pat Gelsinger** predicts the boundary between training and inferencing will decay over time, giving rise to algorithms that enable continuous self-training.
* **The 10,000x Efficiency Target:** **Pat Gelsinger** asserts that computing infrastructure must become 10,000 times better to make AI energy- and capital-efficient enough to support next-generation chemistry, biology, material science, and human-centric computing, potentially incorporating technologies like cryogenic computing.

### Notable Quotes
> "Anybody that can predict the AI future 2 years from now is lying. It's just dynamically changing so rapidly associated with it."
> — **Pat Gelsinger**

> "We have to make AI 10,000 times better than today, right? It isn't going to be a little bit better, we need to make it 10,000x, it is much too energy inefficient, much too capital, uh, inefficient if we're fully going to realize the benefits of AI for humanity's future."

#### TL;DR
Pat Gelsinger, Partner at Playground Global and former Intel CEO, reflects on the technical missteps that caused Intel to fall behind Nvidia and TSMC, evaluates the geopolitical vulnerabilities of global semiconductor manufacturing in Taiwan, and forecasts a multi-decade AI and quantum computing boom bounded only by energy capacity.

### Intel's Missteps and the Tech-Led Shift
* **Business vs. Technical Leadership:** Intel went off the rails when it stopped being run by deeply technical leaders and was instead taken over by business people and bean counters focused on spreadsheets and finance.
    * Gelsinger noted that when he joined Intel as an influencer of the executive staff, roughly 15 to 20 people in the room were PhDs.
    * He became the first technical leader in roughly 15 years when he took over in 2001.
* **Financial Misallocation:** In the 5-6 years prior to Gelsinger's return, Intel returned $100 billion to shareholders through dividends and stock buybacks rather than investing in future capacity.
    * The company had not built a new factory in a decade and failed to purchase EUV machines.
    * Meanwhile, Apple similarly accumulated massive capital while making only small marginal acquisitions like Beats.
* **Apple and NVIDIA Dynamics:** 
    * **Apple Silicon:** Steve Jobs recognized years in advance that he could not rely on Intel to outpace the industry, secretly preparing internal silicon competencies and porting Mac OS to x86 across four releases before breaking away.
    * **NVIDIA's Rise:** Jensen Huang built high-performance throughput machines and progressively improved the CUDA software stack and SIMT multi-threading technology, transforming graphics cards into general-purpose computing devices for HPC and AI.
* **The Rise of TSMC:** TSMC pioneered a pure foundry model, providing standardized PDKs, design tools, and manufacturing capacity to any designer regardless of who owned the chip.
    * When Gelsinger returned to Intel in 2001, TSMC was already producing 5x the wafers of Intel; today that ratio is roughly 7 to 1.

### Geopolitical Risks and the Chips Act
* **Chips Act Progress:** The US is making measurable progress, with leading-edge manufacturing rising from roughly 12% when Gelsinger returned in 2001 to about 18% today, alongside operational TSMC and Intel factories.
* **Taiwan's Energy Vulnerability:** According to a *Wall Street Journal* report, the island of Taiwan maintains less than three weeks of energy reserves.
    * A blockade exceeding three weeks causes the island to brown out.
    * Turning off a semiconductor fab prevents it from coming back on for 90 days, creating an economic impact worse than the Great Depression without a single shot being fired.
    * China has blockaded the Taiwan Straits seven times over the past four years, highlighting the urgent need for more resilient supply chains.

### The AI Buildout and Token Economics
* **Energy as an Upper Bound:** Energy capacity growth (expanding at 4-5% globally after a decade at 1% in the US) acts as a natural governor preventing runaway hype and bubbles, as no one will build data centers or buy GPUs without power.
* **Token Economics & Jevons Law:** Gelsinger views the potential value unleashed by an infinite intelligence/token economy as vast, citing labor shortages across developed countries.
    * His explicit objective is to make AI 10,000x better by dropping the cost and energy per token by five orders of magnitude.
    * This price drop will explode access to AI, driving a multi-decade buildout rather than a short-term trend.
* **Robust Enterprise Demand:** Unlike the dot-com era, current AI leaders are generating real revenues and margins (e.g., ElevenLabs and Lovable reaching $500M to $600M in revenue). Periodic market corrections are healthy to keep multiples grounded.

### The Computing Trinity and Quantum Timeline
* **The Trinity of Computing:** The convergence of classical computing, AI computing, and quantum computing marks the most exciting era in human history for technologists to solve problems in chemistry, biology, materials science, and medicine.
* **Quantum Modalities and Engineering:** Multiple quantum modalities—including trapped ions, photonic approaches, and spin approaches—have proven error correction and algorithmics.
    * The remaining challenge is purely engineering scale.

### Predictions & Forward Calls
* **Quantum Supremacy Timeline:** Meaningful results and quantum supremacy across multiple industries will be achieved *this decade*, before 2030 (approximately 40 months from the July 2026 summit) — Pat Gelsinger.
* **Encryption Vulnerability Timeline:** Fundamental Qday implications, such as solving and breaking traditional encryption, will occur between 2030 and 2033 — Pat Gelsinger.
* **AI Buildout Duration:** The AI buildout will span multiple decades rather than just a few years — Pat Gelsinger.
