## John Armitage: What Makes a Company Worth Investing In? | Podcast | In Good Company

- Channel: Norges Bank Investment Management
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6431POnuLKw
- Published: 2026-10-07
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20261007.html#video-6431POnuLKw
- Speakers: John Armitage (Investor, Egerton Capital); Nicolai Tangen (CEO, Norges Bank Investment Management)

---

### TL;DR
John Armitage, co-founder of Egerton Capital, outlines his 30-year investment philosophy emphasizing pricing power, rigorous qualitative and numerical modeling, realistic management teams, and downside valuation protection. Hosted by Nicolai Tangen, CEO of Norges Bank Investment Management, the discussion covers stock selection, the risks of AI and over-information, and the realities of running a long/short equity firm.

### Defining a Great Company & Investment
- **Moats Versus Management:** **Moats are critical but insufficient.** A company with a moat will fail under poor management (e.g., Boeing under its previous CEO, or Rolls-Royce before its new chief executive).
- **Capital Allocation & Culture:** **Culture and capital allocation drive long-term value.** Berkshire Hathaway transformed from a closed New England textile mill into a powerhouse through capital allocation, while Intel lost its way due to a culture that did not dare to innovate.
- **Filtering New Ideas:** **Initial idea assessment requires time-value and understandability.** Armitage avoids fairly priced businesses to save time and avoids complex industries where management themselves cannot predict outcomes (citing a drug company CFO who treated phase 3 clinical trials like "a series of options").
- **Great Companies vs. Great Investments:** **Historical greatness does not guarantee future outperformance.** Coca-Cola remains a great company with no substitute, but it likely underperformed the market over the trailing 25 years due to a lack of successful new product expansion ("new Coke" failed).

### Pricing Power, Margins & Industry Views
- **Definition and Necessity:** **Pricing power is the ability to raise prices despite competition.** It is essential because all companies face cost inflation driven by lower trade barriers and technology-enabled competition. Sustaining pricing power structurally drives margin growth.
- **Cyclicality and Style:** **The firm invests across cycles without a rigid style definition.** Armitage prefers businesses where downside valuation is protected, the market could close without causing worry, and management is realistic rather than overly optimistic or reliant on aggressive accounting.
- **Sectors Avoided:** **Drug companies are generally avoided.** Over a 30-year career, investments have spanned Russian metals, oil and gas, oil services, technology, and retail, but certain industries are avoided today due to high technology-driven competition.
- **Worldview:** **Macro views need not be overly sophisticated.** Armitage views current markets as high, US bond yields and oil prices up, and two wars ongoing, noting that the global economy relies heavily on AI-driven growth and strong US corporate earnings.

### Financial Modeling & Analysis
- **The Art of Modeling:** **Models are for juniors, but fine-art modeling requires focus on key factors.** Too much detail leads to "garbage in, garbage out" where minor assumption changes break the model. 
- **Private Data and Inference:** **Many company metrics are private and require detective work.** 
  - *Airbus (major holding):* Modeled by evaluating units sold times contractually private prices, incremental unit margins, order backlog revenue timing, lumpy R&D costs, and FX.
  - *Reinsurance:* Contracts are private; analysts must evaluate historical reserve development to assess how past losses were estimated.
  - *Nvidia:* Modeled by piecing together bill of materials composition and the impact of memory price inflation on 75% gross margins.
  - *Visa:* A simpler model with clear costs, 67% margins, and less relevant mix effects.
- **Profit & Loss and Balance Sheets:** **Incremental margins reveal potential outperformance.** High incremental margins combined with high starting margins generate major profit beats during sales overshoots. Balance sheet health requires tracking inventory changes, trade terms (payables/receivables), and artificial cash-boosting tactics like factoring receivables.

### Evaluating Management & Red Flags
- **Assessing Management:** **Look for executives who admit mistakes.** Michael O'Leary (CEO of Ryanair) is praised for his intense focus on detail, self-awareness, and relentless execution despite treating operations as a series of fixes. In contrast, Pat Gelsinger at Intel was criticized for adding back stock-based compensation to earnings.
- **Red Flags:** **Consistently missing expectations and unearned optimism.** Armitage avoids management teams that present a constantly rosy picture and cannot admit when things go wrong.
- **Case Study (Altis):** **Selling without margin for error.** Altis was a French cable/telecom company utilizing leverage and cost-cutting with flat top-line growth. Armitage sold the position after a slight quarterly miss because a stagnant top-line leaves no room for operational mistakes.
- **Paralysis by Analysis:** **Information overload increases the risk of over-analyzing.** Proliferating global data and analyst commentary risk creating psychological imprinting and decision paralysis.

### Valuation & Portfolio Construction
- **Valuation Metrics:** **Conventional metrics are mandatory; EV-to-EBITDA is dismissed.** Armitage relies on P/E ratios, free cash flow multiples, and EV-to-EBIT. He rejects EV-to-EBITDA for capital-intensive or serial acquirers because depreciation and amortization are real costs required to replace capital.
- **Multiples Threshold:** **Growth-adjusted P/E tolerance.** Buying a fast-growing company with double-digit growth at roughly 30 times next year's earnings is considered attractive in the current environment, whereas 35 times earnings (like GE) feels rich.
- **Permanent Capital Rejection:** **Lockups and permanent capital are potential traps.** Armitage rejects permanent capital because it removes the operational tension and discipline required to grapple with proper investment decisions, risking complacency during drawdowns.
- **Portfolio Construction:** **Concentration carries catastrophic risk.** Egerton holds roughly 40 stocks, with top positions sized at 6% to 8%. ## Jon McNeill: The Algorithm Behind Tesla and SpaceX | Podcast | In Good Company

- Channel: Norges Bank Investment Management
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hovdgJI5NMc
- Published: 2026-09-16

- Speakers: Nicolai Tangen (CEO, Norges Bank Investment Management); Jon McNeill (CEO and Co-Founder, DVx Ventures)

---

#### TL;DR
Jon McNeill outlines a five-step hypergrowth formula—question assumptions, delete steps, simplify, accelerate, and automate last—drawn from his experiences scaling Tesla, SpaceX, Lyft, and Lululemon.

### 1. The Five-Step Hypergrowth Formula
* **Step 1: Question Every Requirement**
  * **Interrogate Base Assumptions:** Challenge legacy processes and rules that haven't been examined in years; McNeill noted that rules that make sense are strictly requirements of safety, law, or physics—everything else belongs in the "dumb category until proven otherwise."
  * **Example (Tesla E-Commerce):** When McNeill joined Tesla in 2016 to sell €100,000 cars online, the build-to-order system had 64 clicks and over 300,000 vehicle combinations. Data showed customers essentially bought two models (performance or long-range). Streamlining to just two base models with color choices made manufacturing 10x easier according to the head of manufacturing.
  * **Example (Auto Financing):** Questioning legal counsel revealed that *none* of the dozens of paragraphs in Tesla's 12-page auto lease/loan documents were required by law or regulation (case law already protected repossession for non-payment), proving most of the document was unnecessary baggage.
* **Step 2: Delete Every Step**
  * **Process Mapping:** Have managers map entire manufacturing or sales processes on a wall with sticky notes for every sub-step, then circle the steps the customer actually pays for (the product itself). Roughly 90% of intermediate steps are often unnecessary.
  * **Eliminating Quality Checks:** Tesla removed intermediate quality-check waiting steps between workers. Instead, workers were told not to pass work along until ready, and downstream workers could pass defective work back while tracking pass-backs.
  * **The Best Part is No Part:** Combine functions to eliminate components. For example, Tesla replaced two separate cooling systems (one for the battery, one for the cabin) with a single heat pump system.
* **Step 3: Simplify and Optimize**
  * **Manual Testing First:** Test the newly slimmed-down process manually before writing code or deploying digital solutions.
  * **Financial Benchmarks:** Aim for world-class metrics that investors understand. Tesla targeted Apple-level gross margins of roughly 30% (compared to the 10% auto industry average) and consistently hit 24% to 28%.
* **Step 4: Accelerate Cycle Time**
  * **Speed as a Diagnostic:** Speed reveals where processes break down. You cannot run fast with a faulty process; perfecting the process comes first, then accelerating.
  * **Velocity of Cash:** Borrowing a mindset from Toyota (where it took 4 days from aluminum pile to finished car compared to Tesla's initial 15 days), velocity of cash measures how fast a dollar in working capital turns into a dollar of profit. Toyota required 2.5x less working capital.
  * **Weekly Heartbeat:** Rather than chaotic daily or quarterly panic, maintain a weekly cadence linking demand and supply so leaders can accurately forecast hitting quarterly targets.
* **Step 5: Automate Last**
  * **The Tesla Production Hell Mistake:** Tesla famously designed the Model 3 manufacturing line entirely digitally with maximum automation before laying factory bricks. When installed, machines were 6 inches apart and required hourly human calibration. The automated line failed, nearly bankrupting the company.
  * **The Tent Recovery:** Tesla saved itself by tearing down the automated line, building a manual production line in an outdoor tent, and building cars by hand (100/week, then 500/week) until the process was perfected.
  * **The Concrete Analogy:** Automation is like pouring concrete over a process; once poured, removing it requires a jackhammer. Therefore, automate last.

### 2. Cultural Execution & Avoiding Burnout
* **Psychological Safety vs. Fear:** The common trait at SpaceX and Tesla is humility combined with confidence—admitting "I have no idea how to do this, but I'm going to figure it out." Fear is the enemy of innovation.
* **Special Forces Model:** Operating at extreme intensity (such as sleeping on the factory floor during vehicle launches) is sustainable only as short bursts for "Special Forces" mission deployments, not continuous operations. Regular daily hours run standard shifts.
* **Recruiting for Curiosity:** When interviewing candidates, McNeill looks for three core traits: (1) curiosity (dissatisfaction with the status quo when presented with difficult problems), (2) bias to action, and (3) high intelligence.
* **Culture and Founders:** Corporate culture is deeply embedded in founder DNA and personality traits. Changing a deeply rooted culture across a large organization takes long periods of time.

### 3. Management Hacks & Common Pitfalls
* **Eating Your Own Dog Food:** McNeill's biggest business mistakes stemmed from getting disconnected and lazy about sampling his own product daily—taking inspiration from Sam Walton, who called his own customer service line every morning.
* **Frontline Feedback:** Spending 20% of time on the front lines (driving a Tesla off the line every day, wearing Lululemon gear, visiting stores) provides a quick cheat sheet because frontline employees hear customer frustrations all day long.
* **The Mentorship Framework:** Grab two types of mentors: (1) *vertical mentors* (a generation ahead for wisdom and experience) and (2) *horizontal mentors* (5 or 6 non-competitive peers at your growth stage meeting quarterly under Chatham House rules to solve hard problems).

> — Jon McNeill

> — Jon McNeill
