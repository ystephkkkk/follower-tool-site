### TL;DR
Former U.S. Secretary of Commerce Gina Raimondo warned at the 2026 Bloomberg New Economy Forum in New Delhi that while AI is accelerating faster than its developers anticipated, global governance is lagging behind in an increasingly fragile world. She called on governments, businesses, and civil society to reject isolationism and collaborate on shared AI safety standards, workforce preparation, and cross-border partnerships—highlighting the U.S.–India relationship as central to this effort.

### Geopolitical Fragility and the Case for Collaboration
* **A More Fragile World:** Raimondo noted the world feels more fragile than it did a year ago in Singapore, citing:
  * The ongoing war in Iran with no obvious end, driving high energy prices and disrupting shipping lanes and supply chains.
  * The multi-year conflict in Ukraine continuing unabated.
  * Tariffs and energy shocks compounding global instability, demonstrating that in an interconnected world, conflict anywhere quickly disrupts commerce and security everywhere.
* **Rejecting Isolationism:** While political waves sometimes favor isolation, Raimondo argued it has never been good for prosperity and, in the age of AI, is a bad bet for safety and security.
  * Reiterated her message from last year's conference on reindustrialization: *"America first shouldn't mean America alone."*
  * Noted that over the past year, countries have begun working more closely together on supply chain coordination and semiconductors, and urged that this cooperation continue.

### The Rapid Acceleration and Risks of AI
* **Still in the "First Inning":** AI is advancing faster than even its developers expected, bringing both massive upside and serious downside risks.
  * **Optimism & Real-World Impact:** Highlighted AI's productivity enhancements and life-saving potential, sharing the story of a mother whose two-year-old child with an incurable form of cancer is beginning to see potential cures because of AI.
* **Withheld Capabilities:** Some AI models are now so powerful—particularly at finding and exploiting software weaknesses—that developers are holding them back from public release and pacing access to only a small number of trusted organizations.
* **Autonomous "Rogue" Agents:** AI agents can now plan, act, and operate with real autonomy. What was dismissed as a thought experiment a year ago—rogue agents behaving in unintended ways—is happening today.
* **The Governance Gap:** Raimondo expressed greater concern over governance challenges than engineering challenges, noting that technology is moving far faster than governing institutions and bureaucracies in both the U.S. and India.

### Three Challenges for Global AI Leadership
* **1. Establish Shared Global Standards:** No single country, company, or industry can manage AI alone, as a model built in one place is trained on data from another and runs on chips designed, made, and packaged across different nations.
  * A patchwork of national rules will leave everyone less safe, as a software vulnerability or rogue agent knows no borders.
  * Governments, businesses, and researchers must set common expectations for developing powerful AI, monitoring and controlling agents, and establishing protocols when things go wrong.
  * Pointed to existing precedents in aviation, financial services, and nuclear safety, as well as the U.S. AI Safety Office (created during her tenure) working with international AI Safety Institutes.
* **2. Prepare the Workforce:** Warned against repeating the "China shock," which left millions of Americans out of work and fueled political division and populism on both the left and the right.
  * Cited Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella's warning: why would Microsoft have a social license to operate if it only creates technology that puts Americans out of work?
  * Urged leaders not to slow down AI, but to implement it alongside retraining, redeployment, and safety nets so no American, Indian, or global worker is left behind.
* **3. Bring Everyone to the Table:** Having spent 15 years in government, Raimondo emphasized that governments move too slowly and lack all the necessary knowledge to act alone.
  * Collaboration must include businesses, scientists, universities, and civil society (noting global leaders in attendance, such as the Chairman of Hyundai from Korea).

### India's Role and the U.S.–India Partnership
* **Leadership & Optimism:** Praised Prime Minister Narendra Modi's energy and "power to empower" message, as well as India's scale, ambition, entrepreneurship, and national pride.
* **AI as "America India":** Recounted a one-hour meeting at Prime Minister Modi's home at 9:00 PM three years ago, where Modi told her: *"Secretary, let's not call AI artificial intelligence. Let's call it America India."* When she saw him again prior to her remarks, his first words were *"AI, America, India."*
* **Workforce Advantage:** With a growing, young, and entrepreneurial workforce, India is positioned to be at the center of the AI economy; Raimondo noted that the country that prepares its people best will hold a global advantage in using AI.

### TL;DR
At a Federal Reserve Productivity and Jobs Task Force hearing moderated by Kevin Warsh, Gina Raimondo and Marc Andreessen agreed that AI's economic impact will be determined less by the technology itself than by human, political, and regulatory bottlenecks. While Raimondo urged immediate reform of U.S. workforce and permitting systems to prevent a spike in unemployment and political backlash, Andreessen argued that heavy regulation in "slow" sectors like healthcare and education will limit both mass job displacement and near-term productivity growth.

### The Pace of AI Adoption: "Fast" vs. "Slow" Sectors
* **Disappointing Both Extremes:** Marc Andreessen argued that both AI "Utopians" and "Doomers" will be disappointed by the scope and magnitude of AI's real-world economic impact because deployment is messy and often stalled by institutional friction.
  * Cited civilian nuclear power as a historical precedent of a transformative, zero-emission technology that was effectively frozen in the U.S. and Europe.
* **The "Fast" vs. "Slow" Economy:** Andreessen previewed his task force framework dividing the U.S. economy into two distinct categories:
  * **Fast Sectors:** Consumer electronics, media and entertainment, and commodity goods experience rapid technological change, high job turnover, and rapidly falling prices. As prices crash, their share of GDP shrinks (similar to agriculture dropping from 97% of the U.S. economy to 3%).
  * **Slow Sectors:** Healthcare, education, housing, and legal/governmental administration are characterized by restricted supply (cartels), heavy demand subsidization (e.g., federal student loans, housing subsidies), and government monopolies. Prices rise rapidly, and radical technological transformation is largely outlawed.
* **Regulatory Barriers to Job Displacement:** Andreessen noted that AI cannot legally replace many roles—such as becoming a licensed hairdresser in California (which requires 900 hours of training), an MD, a lawyer admitted to the bar, or a K–12 public school teacher.

### Workforce Transition, Education, and Inequality
* **Public Fear and Backlash:** Gina Raimondo warned that the passage of the vast majority of local data center moratoriums in recent elections signals deep public fear over job loss, children's mental health, and national security.
* **Broken Workforce and Education Systems:** Raimondo argued that the U.S. must use the AI transition to fix a failing workforce infrastructure:
  * The U.S. spent hundreds of billions of dollars last year subsidizing higher education, yet 35%+ of four-year college students do not graduate, leaving them with debt and no degree.
  * State workforce and community college funding incentivizes attendance (e.g., Pell grants) rather than outcomes.
  * Unemployment insurance was created 100 years ago during the Great Depression and has not been reformed since.
* **Economic Insecurity:** Raimondo highlighted that 10-plus million Americans work full-time yet remain poor enough to rely on food stamps and Medicaid, warning that unleashing AI without safeguards could "put a match in this powder keg of inequality."
* **Tech Industry Responsibility:** Andreessen acknowledged that the tech industry has not done a great job providing leadership, with some figures spreading high levels of fear, and called on the industry to design AI products for "human potential and human enablement."

### Permitting, Regulation, and the Weakest Link
* **Red Tape Slowing Growth:** Raimondo recounted that after passing the bipartisan CHIPS Act and negotiating deals with TSMC and Samsung, she was told it would take seven years just to get a shovel in the ground due to permitting layers. Bipartisan Congressional intervention was required to clear the path for chip manufacturing in Arizona and Texas.
* **The Human Weak Link:** Asked by Kevin Warsh whether the weak link in productivity revolutions is electricity, data centers, chips, or humans, Raimondo answered "all of the above," but emphasized that political divisiveness and human policymaking are by far the biggest obstacles.
* **The AI "Elixir":** Andreessen noted that the core ideas behind modern AI were formulated in the 1940s and were considered completely discredited when he earned his computer science degree in the 1980s. Now that they work, policy decisions—not technological capability—will determine whether the U.S. captures the productivity upside.

### Geopolitics: The U.S.–China AI Race & Global Allies
* **21st Century Cold War:** Andreessen framed U.S.–China AI competition as a straight-up geopolitical rivalry.
  * **U.S. Advantage:** A decentralized, free-market system where the private sector and financial system recalibrated roughly $7 trillion toward AI over the last two years.
  * **China's Advantage:** A centralized, command-and-control system with a clear, top-down national plan—though handicapped by authoritarian crackdowns on entrepreneurs.
* **Winning Through Allies:** Raimondo argued that China is too big, rich, and determined for the U.S. to compete alone. Rather than withholding chips and compute from allies—which she warned will only push countries like Korea, Japan, India, Southeast Asia, and Europe toward China—the U.S. should export compute and models to set global standards for safety, cyber, and national security.

### Predictions & Forward Calls
* **Muted Economic Impact:** Both AI "Utopians" and "Doomers" will be disappointed by the scope and magnitude of AI's real-world economic impact due to regulatory and organizational friction. — *Marc Andreessen*
* **Administrative Job Shifts:** 10 to 13 million administrative, benefits verification, and back-office jobs "are either going to go away or be different" during the transition to an agentic economy. — *Gina Raimondo*
* **Healthcare's Expanding GDP Share:** Healthcare, already 20% (a fifth) of U.S. GDP, will continue to grow unchecked toward "a fourth, a third and then half, and then ultimately 80%" of GDP because technological transformation is legally blocked in the sector. — *Marc Andreessen*
* **U.S.–China AI Lead:** China is currently "maybe a year behind" the U.S. in AI; the U.S. must work to remain "at least a year ahead," and ideally three to five years ahead, in three years' time. — *Marc Andreessen*

### Notable Quotes
> "When the average American hears AI, they hear, 'I'll lose my job, my kids will lose their job, my children's mental health, national security'... And so when people are afraid, they say stop. Except that would be, I think, very bad for America." — *Gina Raimondo*

> "We've mostly outlawed the kind of job transition that I think both the Utopians and the Doomers talk about... Healthcare, like you literally legally cannot have technological transformation in most of healthcare." — *Marc Andreessen*

> "If you tell another country, 'Sorry, you can't have a chip, you can't have compute,' what do you think they're going to do? They will go to China." — *Gina Raimondo*

> "I think we're at a hinge point for the U.S. economy, the United States' role in the world... But I'd rather have our problems than anyone else's in the world." — *Kevin Warsh*

### The Experimental Strategy for "AI-Ready" States
* **Private Capital for Public Policy:** The initiative uses philanthropic and corporate risk capital to run state-level workforce pilots. Once these programs prove successful, states can confidently scale them using public funds.
* **Redefining "AI-Ready" States:** Raimondo argues that current state systems are unprepared for technological disruption and must be modernized to include:
    * **Flexible Unemployment Insurance (UI):** Legacy UI systems currently penalize workers who attempt to start businesses or go back to school. AI-ready states will reform UI to allow displaced workers to leverage AI tools to build new startups while maintaining temporary financial support.

* **Solving the Mid-Career Retraining Gap:** The current educational and workforce landscape fails older workers (ages 45 to 55) who cannot afford to go a year without a paycheck. Raise US aims to build and support "earn-while-you-learn" models.
* **Corporate Self-Interest over Altruism:** Raimondo challenges tech CEOs who claim workforce transition is solely a government responsibility.
    * **Avoiding Systemic Backlash:** She warns that failure to manage job displacement will lead to deep recessions, social unrest, and swift regulatory intervention that could halt AI development entirely.

**TL;DR**
To prevent a repeat of the "China shock" displacement of the 1980s, the U.S. must establish a "grand bargain" where employers and universities co-design a continuous, "K to grey" learning system that prioritizes individual empowerment over shareholder-only gains.

### The "Grand Bargain" for Workforce Security
*   **Addressing Public Anxiety:** Gina Raimondo notes that over 70% of Americans fear AI-driven job loss. She warns that failing to address this anxiety with a concrete plan will lead to political over-regulation that stifles U.S. competitiveness.
*   **The Employer Mandate:** Companies must transition from being passive consumers of talent to active drivers of the education system.
    *   **Skill Definition:** Employers must continuously define the skills needed for today and tomorrow.
    *   **Bridging the Gap:** Unlike the manufacturing shift to China, which left millions without a path to the service economy, the AI transition requires a "bridge" for workers of all ages to pivot.
*   **Continuous Learning:** Education can no longer be a "one-and-done" event; it must be a lifetime of continuous upskilling to match the pace of technological change.

### Transforming Higher Education: The "CEO" Model
*   **Institutional Inertia:** Sethuraman Panchanathan argues that universities are currently too slow and remain in denial, often settling for "adaptation" when "transformation" is required.
*   **The Five Pillars of Co-Creation:** Panchanathan proposes a "CEO" mindset (Collaboration, Innovation, and Transformation) based on:
    *   **Co-creation & Co-design:** Developing curriculum alongside industry leaders.
    *   **Co-habitation:** Eliminating the "four walls" of the university to embed faculty and students within real-world industry environments.
    *   **Co-evolution:** Updating the roles of tenure-track faculty to include practitioners and "professors of practice" who live in the real world.
*   **Democratizing Opportunity:** While talent is universal, opportunity is not; universities must leverage AI to scale access to elite learning resources for the "massive" population rather than just the "best and brightest."

### AI as a Tool for Student Success
*   **The Three Competencies:** Marie Lynn Miranda outlines three pillars every student must possess to survive the AI era:
    *   **Fluency:** The ability to incorporate AI into sophisticated process workflows.
    *   **Criticality:** Understanding the limitations of Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically how they miss information on the "tails" of distribution and can be manipulated.
    *   **Ethics:** Knowing when to disclose AI use and identifying inherent biases in data models.
*   **AI-Informed Data Science:** The University of Illinois Chicago uses data science to proactively support first-generation students.
    *   **Predictive Support:** By analyzing non-cognitive assets (e.g., financial stability, support networks) and academic history, the university provides tutoring *before* a student fails a midterm.
