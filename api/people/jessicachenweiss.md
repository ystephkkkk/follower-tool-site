### TL;DR
President Xi Jinping’s high-stakes state visit to Washington highlights a strategic stalemate between the U.S. and China, where neither side can deliver a knockout blow, prompting an ongoing debate among experts regarding whether a durable détente or escalating K-shaped competition defines the future of the relationship.

### 2. The Trade Truce and Strategic Stalemate
- **Escalation Timeline:** **Jessica Chen Weiss** details the escalation cycle leading to the truce:
  - **April:** Liberation Day tariffs followed by Chinese retaliation with restrictions on rare earths and permanent magnets.
  - **October:** The U.S. expanded export controls on affiliates of entity-listed Chinese companies.
  - **China's Response:** Sweeping restrictions on rare earths and permanent magnets that threatened the global economy.
  - **Busan Truce:** The two sides reached a truce pausing escalatory controls, which has remained in effect until today.
- **The K-Shaped Relationship:** **Julian Gewirtz** describes the U.S.–China relationship as "K-shaped," meaning it is accelerating in two directions simultaneously: leader-level diplomacy and targeted stabilization on the upward leg, with intensifying competitive pressures below on the downward leg.
- **A Durable Détente vs. Coexistence:** **Jessica Chen Weiss** argues for embracing a durable détente that abandons disruptive fantasies of decisive victory, noting that neither side can land a knockout blow and both must cohabit the planet. Conversely, **Julian Gewirtz** doubts a durable détente is possible under Trump, arguing Beijing is attempting to raise and remake the floor to constrain future American policy.

### 3. Policy Prescriptions and Taiwan
- **Advice for President Trump:** **Jessica Chen Weiss** advises Trump to stay away from the issue of Taiwan altogether, noting it should not be treated as a bargaining chip, and to pursue regular and sustained technical dialogue on AI safety alongside working on wins for American farmers.
- **Polling and Public Opinion:** **Jessica Chen Weiss** cites a recent Chicago Council poll showing a majority of Americans (57%) favor cooperation and engagement with China, compared to 40% wanting to limit China's power.
- **The Competition Label:** **Jessica Chen Weiss** critiques "competition" as a poorly defined buzzword, preferring terms like deterrence and conflicting interests regarding Taiwan to avoid zero-sum escalation dominance. **Julian Gewirtz** counters that competition is an undeniable dynamic and diagnosis of reality that any coherent strategy must reckon with.
- **Bipartisan Consensus Shift:** **Jessica Chen Weiss** expresses surprise at how quickly Trump turned on a dime from escalatory tariffs to pursuing a good relationship with Xi, and how Republicans have gone along with it, suggesting the bipartisan hawkish consensus was not as hardened as it appeared. **Julian Gewirtz** emphasizes that future administrations of any political stripe will inherit a very different starting point regarding domestic strengths and alliances.

### 4. AI Safety and Strategic Intentions
- **Parallel AI Safety:** **Julian Gewirtz** argues that the U.S. and China should develop AI safety in parallel rather than jointly, as neither government has fully figured out how to govern the technologies domestically.
- **Conflicting Concepts of Control:** **Julian Gewirtz** and **Jessica Chen Weiss** note a fundamental divergence in risk perception: American labs focus on human control over models, whereas China's paramount concern is party control over models and societal stability. **Jessica Chen Weiss** adds that China's regulators classify "extreme loss of control" as a paramount risk to political control, yet the AI safety communities see potential for technical best-practice and threat-information sharing.
- **Intentions and Global Strategy:** **Julian Gewirtz** points to Xi Jinping's intensive diplomatic outreach—visiting the Shanghai Cooperation Organization (SEO) in Central Asia, BRICS in India, and making a state visit to Egypt—as evidence that China is actively building alternative institutions where it is the central player, putting the U.S. in the position of a spectator.
- **Domestic Imperatives:** **Jessica Chen Weiss** argues in her forthcoming book, *What China Wants*, that Beijing is driven primarily by domestic imperatives (sovereignty, security, development) rather than a grand design to global hegemony. She notes that China chose not to crush the U.S. economy despite having the leverage to do so via permanent magnet and rare earth export controls, proving its priority is stability and development.

### 5. Quick Hits: Taiwan, Iran, and Russia
- **Taiwan Military Action:** **Jessica Chen Weiss** predicts that while kinetic bloodshed is unlikely, grey-zone military actions short of the kinetic threshold will undoubtedly continue.
- **Iran and Bargaining:** **Julian Gewirtz** warns against reports that Xi Jinping may link an offer to help the U.S. on Iran with unilateral concessions on Taiwan (such as the pending $14 billion arms package), calling it a "win-win" where Beijing wins twice by benefiting from a reopened Strait of Hormuz while securing concessions on Taiwan.
- **Russia Partnership:** **Jessica Chen Weiss** asserts that China views a surviving Putin as vital strategic ballast and insurance against a U.S. policy bent on coercion and containment. **Julian Gewirtz** notes that the personal and geopolitical partnership remains robust, with rumors pointing to a potential trilateral meeting among Putin, Xi, and Trump if Trump travels to Shenzhen for APEC later this year.
