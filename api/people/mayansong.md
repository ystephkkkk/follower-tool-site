**TL;DR** 
Award-winning architect Ma Yansong argues that modern urban design must move beyond stark, functional "concrete jungles" to embrace a poetic, nature-integrated philosophy where buildings serve as democratic public spaces that evoke deep emotional and spiritual connections.

***

### Redefining the Relationship Between Architecture and Nature

*   **The Eastern Philosophy of Coexistence:** Unlike Western architectural traditions that often treat buildings as isolated monuments, traditional Chinese design treats nature, landscapes, and buildings as an integrated whole ("I am part of you and you are part of me"). Modern urban environments desperately need to recapture this balance to restore human feeling and experience.
*   **Chaoyang Park Plaza (Beijing):** 
    *   This project translates traditional Chinese calligraphy and landscape paintings into a modern skyline.
    *   Designed as a sculptural "mountain in the park," it transitions the boundary between high-density urban infrastructure and natural parkland.
*   **Tunnel of Light (Japan):** 
    *   An art installation designed to invite people directly into nature rather than presenting a detached, artificial structure to be admired from afar. 
    *   The design emphasizes that surrealism and emotional resonance are essential functional requirements of modern architecture.
*   **One River North (Denver):** 
    *   A residential building featuring a "canyon" carved directly into its glass facade, introducing a walkable trail, vegetation, and a waterfall.
    *   This brings Colorado's mountainous landscape directly into a dense city grid while maintaining standard residential utilities.

### Transforming Infrastructure and Corporate Spaces into Public Parks

*   **Jiaxing Railway Station (Zhejiang):** 
    *   **The Railway Station in a Forest:** Rather than building a massive, imposing monument typical of Chinese transit hubs, MAD Architects placed the train station entirely underground. 
    *   This allowed the ground level to be developed as an open forest park, creating a highly democratic public space accessible to commuters and non-commuters alike.
*   **Tencent Headquarters (Shenzhen):** 
    *   **The Cloud Center:** Designed as a "floating cloud" island, this corporate headquarters hovers above the ground.
    *   By raising the corporate spaces, the ground floor is fully opened to the public, setting a precedent for how private tech campuses can share spatial resources with the surrounding city.
*   **Quzhou Sports Park (Zhejiang):** 
    *   **Walkable Grass-Covered Stadiums:** Designed to look like natural green volcanoes, these sports arenas are fully covered in grass and accessible to pedestrians. 
    *   This design solves the common urban planning issue of dead stadium space by keeping the facility active as a daily public park even when no athletic events are scheduled.

### Bridging History, Emotion, and Surrealism

*   **Coexistence of New and Old:** In historic areas like old Beijing courtyards, new installations (such as a kindergarten and upgraded restroom facilities) are built low to the ground. This ensures they do not overpower historical heritage structures, facilitating a harmonious dialogue between different eras.
*   **Fenix Museum of Migration (Rotterdam):** 
    *   An industrial warehouse renovated into a public museum.
    *   Features a dynamic, double-helix "tornado" staircase that is both highly aesthetic and functional, allowing visitors to cross paths unexpectedly to symbolize the journey and encounters of global migration.
*   **Cultural Center of Shenzhen Bay:** 
    *   Designed as a cluster of monumental, primitive "cobblestone" structures set within a high-cost waterfront district.
    *   Features a 100,000-square-meter underground stadium and a glass ceiling covered in water (which inspired the famous "Sky Cleaner" photograph). 
    *   The project prioritizes "uncertainty" in space, encouraging visitors to explore, wander, and become co-creators of their environment.

### Navigating the Realities of Cost, Craftsmanship, and Construction

*   **Balancing Poetic Vision with Budget Constraints:** 
    *   Ma emphasizes that "good buildings are not all ones with strange shapes" and do not inherently require massive budgets. 
    *   **Material Efficiency:** The Harbin Opera House achieved its warm, futuristic, and highly organic interior curve by using inexpensive local Northern Chinese wood and high-level craftsmanship. Similarly, the complex-looking Shenzhen Bay structures utilize cheap local granite from Hunan.
    *   **Strategic Allocation:** The highly visible, complex artistic features of Ma's designs typically account for only 20% to 30% of a project's overall construction budget.
*   **The Architect’s Role Post-Delivery:** 
    *   Because architects lose direct control over a building once it is operational, Ma advocates for architects to remain physically on-site during the years-long construction phase to foster emotional connections with the builders and minimize deviations from the design.

### Notable Quotes

> "We see everywhere materials, but the missing part is human experience and feeling. I think this is the key point for the future." — Ma Yansong

> "I really don't like buildings where the functions are so clear—you go somewhere and you do something, everything is certain. I want something uncertain. The uncertainty makes us know what we really want to do." — Ma Yansong

> "For the future, buildings should not be a vacuum of functions. They should pay more attention to the poetic connection between people and nature. It is hard to experience a poem, but all of us should have a poetic self." — Ma Yansong
