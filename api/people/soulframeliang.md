### TL;DR
Soulframe Liang, producer of *Phantom Blade Zero*, discusses his 15-year journey from an indie developer to leading S-GAME, the deep influence of Gu Long's dark *wuxia* aesthetics on his work, and the evolution of the *xia* spirit into modern individual self-actualization.

### 1. Defining the *Xia* and Dark *Wuxia* Aesthetics
- **The True Nature of *Xia*:** **Liang Qiwei** explains that a *xia* differs from an ordinary good person by operating under extreme conditions, bearing massive risks, and maintaining human compassion amidst systemic injustice and collapsing order.
- **Grassroots Roots:** **Liang** notes that unlike Western knights who protected feudal lords or Japanese samurai serving shoguns, Chinese *xia* emerge from the margins (*jianghu*) in opposition to the imperial court.
- **The "Pathological" Aesthetic:** Drawing from Gu Long's works (e.g., *Sentimental Swordsman, Ruthless Sword*), **Liang** highlights that *wuxia* characters invariably carry flaws or "pathologies" (e.g., Li Xunhuan’s tuberculosis, Fu Hongxue's lameness), creating an aesthetic collision between extreme beauty and extreme darkness.

### 2. Evolution of *Wuxia* and Modern Values
- **Shift from Self-Sacrifice to Self-Actualization:** **Liang** contrasts classic *wuxia* tropes of self-abnegation for another person with modern values where young people prioritize personal growth, inner peace, and the "power fantasy" of leveling up (*"In hardship, cultivate yourself; in success, better the world"*).
- **The "Wuxia Punk" Paradigm:** **Liang** argues that traditional *wuxia* moves from the self outward to others, whereas modern "wuxia punk" moves from the self back to the self—mirroring the closed-loop growth found in modern web novels and Soulslike games.
- **Rejection of Preachy Tropes:** **Liang** admits early trailers for *Phantom Blade Zero* failed when they pasted on grand, hypocritical slogans like *"The greatest xia serves the nation and the people,"* which players immediately saw through as insincere "self-gilding."

### 3. Liang Qiwei's Entrepreneurial Journey and S-GAME
- **The 2011 Startup Pivot:** After failing an engineering economics course and having his Tsinghua grad school recommendation revoked, **Liang** studied architecture at Yale before returning to China in May 2011 with a backpack and a dream to make games.
- **Early Angel Funding:** **Xu Xiaoping** (ZhenFund) personally wired **Liang** 1,000,000 RMB without a term sheet to start S-GAME in Beijing's Huaqing Jiayuan.
- **The Trap of Capitalization (2015–2017):** **Liang** reflects on the era following *Phantom Blade 1*'s commercial success (pulling in 10 to 20 million RMB monthly with a 20-person team), where he and his team drifted into roadshows, business meetings, and deal-making, losing their core *jianghu* edge.
- **Tencent Investment and Self-Publishing:** Following mobile titles like *Phantom Blade 2* and *3* (which peaked at 150 million RMB in monthly revenue), S-GAME bought back their shares from investors, secured over 400 million RMB from Tencent for a ~20% stake, and ultimately chose to self-publish *Phantom Blade Zero* to maintain complete creative control.

### 4. Game Design, AI, and Industry Perspectives
- **Craft vs. Dao:** **Liang** humbly describes his team's work as possessing solid technical craft (*shu*), while acknowledging their inner cultivation (*dao*) still falls short of legendary industry creators.
- **The 99% Internal Rationale:** **Liang** asserts that external hot trends (like esports or anime styles in *Phantom Blade 2*) account for only 1% of success; 99% relies on the creator's internal rationale, lived experience, and genuine personality.
- **The Limits of AI in Single-Player Games:** While pro-AI for technical optimization, **Liang** emphasizes that AI cannot replace the brand premium and consumer trust built by human labor, painstaking artistry, and auteur-driven creation over decades.

### Predictions & Forward Calls
- **Liang Qiwei** predicts that the era of relying on external industry tailwinds or quick market pivots is over for Chinese AAA games, forcing developers to rely entirely on authentic, original IP and deep internal cultivation.
