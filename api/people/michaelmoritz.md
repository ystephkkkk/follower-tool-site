### TL;DR
Michael Moritz reflects on how his family’s history as Jewish refugees from Nazi Germany shaped his outsider mentality, drive, and vigilance. He shares lessons from decades of venture capital investing at Sequoia, working with extraordinary and flawed leaders like Steve Jobs and Elon Musk, and why complete obsession often comes at a high relational cost.

### Family History, Heritage, and the Outsider Mentality
* **Refugee Background:** Grew up in Wales during the 1960s and 1970s as part of a minority family of Jewish refugees who fled Nazi Germany in the 1930s.
* **The Two Grandfathers:** 
  * *Paternal Grandfather:* A civil servant and WWI veteran in Bavaria who trusted the state and its pension, delaying his exit until it was too late; he and his wife were shipped east and perished.
  * *Maternal Grandfather:* A streetwise cattle trader who got out with his wife 72 hours before war broke out, demonstrating the entrepreneurial instinct to live by one's wits.
* **The Lesson of Vigilance:** Learned the constant need for a "bolt hole" and to never assume safety, noting that minorities in the US and Western Europe face enduring insecurity.
* **Small Deeds and Moral Courage:** Highlighted the story of Anna, a Roman Catholic former family nanny in Munich who risked her safety to shelter the Morris family during the 1930s, embodying the rare "resister" mindset compared to the silent majority or collaborators.

### The Psychology of Exceptional People and Monomania
* **The First 14 Years:** Uses an investigative method focusing on a person's formative childhood years to understand their underlying drives, slights, and motivations.
* **Cost of Monomania:** Exceptional creators (such as artists like Frank Auerbach or leaders like Steve Jobs) often exhibit complete obsession that locks out the rest of the world, with the primary cost being strained relationships.
* **Absence of Awe:** Refuses to hold anyone in awe—including Steve Jobs—because getting close to extraordinary people reveals profound personal flaws alongside their accomplishments.
* **The Great Person Theory:** Strongly believes in the impact of singular leaders, citing Elon Musk as potentially the greatest industrialist in US history and being completely "gobsmacked" by the scale of his multi-industry achievements.

### Journalism, Apple, and Joining Sequoia
* **Early Break:** Secured an early break at age 22/23 joining *Time* magazine in San Francisco without prior experience, encouraged by Daily Telegraph editor Bill Deeds to move to America.
* **The Apple Profile Fallout:** Authored an early book on Apple and contributed to *Time*'s famous "Computer of the Year" edition; a harsh editorial rewrite alienated Steve Jobs and permanently severed their relationship.
* **Joining Sequoia (1985):** Hired by Don Valentine when Sequoia was tiny, despite Moritz having no technical degree or operational engineering background. Valentine modeled this on Arthur Rock backing Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel.
* **Evolution of Venture Capital:** Notes that today's VC environment differs vastly from the 1980s and 90s due to pre-internet information asymmetry, too many intermediaries, and a lack of close, personal founder relationships.

### Leadership, Management, and San Francisco Journalism
* **Managing with Alex Ferguson:** Co-authored a book with Manchester United manager Sir Alex Ferguson, learning that great leaders do not need to be loved or feared, but must be respected and fiercely supported during their lowest moments.
* **Stepping Down:** Left leadership at Sequoia around 2012–2013 via a "cold turkey" exit, believing leaders should not linger.
* **The San Francisco Standard:** Co-founded *The San Francisco Standard* with Griff Gafney as a for-profit local journalism business to prove regional news can be profitable while providing reliable reporting and center/right-of-center opinion.
