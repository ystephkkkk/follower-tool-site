## AI’s Giant Money Loop Is Feeling Very 2008 | Big Take

- Channel: Bloomberg Podcasts
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DLVE5OnmsRM
- Published: 2026-10-12
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20261012.html#video-DLVE5OnmsRM
- Speakers: Shawn Donnan (Reporter, Bloomberg); Tom Orlik (Chief Economist, Bloomberg Economics); David Gura (Host, Bloomberg)

---

### TL;DR
The AI infrastructure boom is being fueled by circular global capital flows—with foreign chip-maker savings recycling back into US markets—creating global imbalances and economic risks reminiscent of the run-up to the 2008 financial crisis. If the AI trade fails to deliver profits quickly enough, export-reliant "techno-states" and the broader global economy face severe multi-pronged fallout.

### Global Imbalances and the "Techno-State" Vulnerability
* **Global Imbalances Rivaling Historic Highs:** According to Bloomberg’s Sean Donnan, global imbalances between savers and spenders are back up near their highest levels in 150 years—levels not seen since the early 2000s prior to the 2008 financial crisis.
* **The Rise of Private Savers Over Central Banks:** Unlike the early 2000s when cautious central banks in China, East Asia, and petrostates piled trade surpluses into ultra-safe US Treasuries, today's top savers are nimble, return-focused private companies—such as chip makers Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) and Samsung Electronics—whose cash piles rival central bank foreign exchange reserves.
* **Dutch Disease Risks in Asia:** Beatrice Weder di Mauro of the Center for Economic Policy Research warns that South Korea and Taiwan are operating as "techno-states" whose economies are increasingly concentrated in extractive-like technology export dependencies, making them highly vulnerable to "Dutch disease" if the AI cycle downturns.

### The Historic Scale of the AI Buildout
* **A Record-Breaking $9 Trillion Capital Wave:** Citing a Brookings and National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) paper by a Columbia Business School professor, Tom Orlik notes that total capital heading into the AI build-out between 2025 and 2032 will hit approximately **$9 trillion**, or about **3.2% of GDP per year**.
* **Surpassing Past Historical Booms:** This represents the largest capital investment boom in US history—eclipsing the 19th-century railway investment boom.
* **Growth Dependence in the US:** Sean Donnan points out that US GDP is currently growing at **2 to 3%**, with as much as a full percentage point of that growth potentially driven by the AI investment boom.

### Divergence from the 2008 Playbook: The US Treasury Squeeze
* **Crowding Out Safe Assets:** In the early 2000s, foreign savings flooded into US Treasuries, driving down interest rates and yields. Today, high-yielding AI assets (bonds and equities) are actively siphoning demand away from the US Treasury market, driving yields higher.
* **Exploding Fiscal Deficits:** Compounding the pressure, years of wide fiscal deficits following the Global Financial Crisis and COVID-19 have pushed US national debt to **$40 trillion**.
* **Soaring Interest Costs:** With the US 10-year Treasury rate sitting above **5%**, annual US interest costs now exceed **$1 trillion**—crowding out spending on education, defense, and infrastructure.

### Bubbles, Warning Signs, and Historical Parallels
* **Early Indicators of Frothy Valuations:** Tom Orlik highlights recent market friction, including a pulled Australian data center IPO due to rich valuations and OpenAI's 2026 revenue run rate falling short of aggressive investor expectations.
* **The "Double Blow" Risk:** If the AI trade busts like the dotcom bubble or 1890s railway boom, export-heavy Asian economies will suffer a double hit: declining chip demand alongside heavy losses on their US market investments.
* **The Duration Problem:** Former IMF chief economist Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas and other experts point out a severe duration mismatch: if the promised AI productivity payoff does not materialize quickly enough, chip makers face simultaneous hits to product demand and investment returns.

## Howard Marks Flags the Uncertainty in AI Investing

- Channel: Bloomberg Podcasts
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-ZdgxiRWuS0
- Published: 2026-09-20
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20260920.html#video--ZdgxiRWuS0
- Speakers: Howard Marks (Co-founder & Co-chair, Oaktree Capital Management); David Gura (Host, Bloomberg); Christina Ruffini (Host, Bloomberg)

---

### TL;DR
Markets have been dominated by optimists driven by strong economic aggregates, the TINA trade, and AI enthusiasm, though this requires heightened investor caution. While AI's transformative potential is undeniable, extreme uncertainty surrounding future profitability and societal impact makes it impossible to definitively label current market exuberance as irrational.

### The Macro Environment & Market Psychology
- **Federal Reserve Communication:** Howard Marks argues that less central bank communication is preferable, as it forces businesses and portfolios to become antifragile rather than relying entirely on Fed projections.
  - Expresses a personal preference for a less activist central bank that lets the free market determine natural interest rates through borrower-lender negotiations unless the economy is heading off the rails.
- **Optimism-Driven Markets:** Markets have been ruled by optimists since roughly October 1, 2022, with the S&P 500 more than doubling in that period.
  - The S&P surge is supported by positive underlying truths, including a US economy that performs as the best-developed world economy, the "TINA" (there is no alternative) trade, and AI excitement.
  - Marks attributes persistent optimism partly to the absence of a truly severe downturn since the global financial crisis market bottom in March 2009, leading people to extrapolate good times.
  - When optimists are in charge, they slough off bad news and react positively to settlement rumors without getting depressed when deals fall through, creating a positive market bias that calls for built-in defense and caution.

### AI Investment & Valuation Challenges
- **The Definition of Value Investing:** Marks defines his approach as a "small v" value investor whose goal is to calculate an asset's intrinsic value and buy it at a fair price relative to that value.
- **The "Too Hard" Pile:** Evaluating pure-play AI companies is extremely challenging because they may not have produced profits yet, making it difficult to quantify earnings potential 5 years out.
  - Hyperscalers like Microsoft and Amazon have supporting legacy businesses, but companies all-in on AI present an unusual degree of uncertainty for intrinsic valuation, fitting Warren Buffett's description of the "too hard pile."
- **Exuberance vs. Irrationality:** Alan Greenspan's 1997 phrase "irrational exuberance" defines a bubble, and while AI exuberance is undeniable, Marks hesitates to label it definitively as irrational.
  - Because nobody can definitively scope what AI will accomplish, its profitability, or its broader effects, nobody can prove the exuberance is irrational.
  - The presence of uncertainty does not mean investors should do nothing, but it must be acknowledged to properly inform investment sizing and strategy.

### Societal Impact & Government Policy
- **Existential Economic Concerns:** Marks previously warned about the acute societal ramifications of AI displacing workers in Middle America and across the country.
  - Reiterates that sentient human beings must acknowledge these risks, though he notes his wife defines worrying as "bleeding before you're shot" since individuals cannot personally stop the trend.
- **Policy Vacuum:** Marks criticizes the lack of government foresight, stating there should be a dedicated task force examining the coming effects of AI.
  - Poses a critical unanswered fiscal question for policymakers: If AI replaces human workers, will it replace them as taxpayers, and what will happen to government revenues when people lose their jobs?

### Corporate Integration & Critical Thinking
- **Enterprise Usage at Oaktree:** Oaktree Capital Management uses AI internally to marshal data and perform early analysis on tasks that used to take a long time, but has not turned business or investment decisions over to algorithms.
  - Exceptional investing does not come from computers, but from a superior ability to assess the import of data—a superiority AI does not yet possess.
- **Preserving Cognitive Muscles:** Oaktree does not encourage younger hires to outsource critical thinking to AI.
  - Stopping critical thinking causes individuals to "lose the muscle," and the ultimate edge in investing remains fundamentally human.
  - Marks compares AI's disruptive potential to indexation in equity investing—putting many active investors out of business, but not the best.

### Notable Quotes
> *"When society thinks—when everybody in society thinks they know exactly what the Fed's gonna do and prepares for that, then the society is dependent on that outcome. And if if if something else happens, then, you know, bad things can happen."* — **Howard Marks**

> *"My wife defines worrying as bleeding before you're shot. And in this case, there's nothing we can do about it. Nothing certainly nothing I can do about it personally. So, you roll with it."* — **Howard Marks**

> *"If people are put out of work, they won't pay taxes. If AI takes their place as as workers, will it take their place as taxpayers and how? So what will happen to government revenues when when and if people lose their jobs?"* — **Howard Marks**

## Former Goldman Sachs CEO Talks AI, Private Credit and Politics

- Channel: Bloomberg Podcasts
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eTCLJyPjOtI
- Published: 2026-03-01
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20260301.html#video-eTCLJyPjOtI
- Speakers: Lloyd Blankfein (Former CEO, Goldman Sachs); David Gura (Host, The Big Take Podcast)

---

**TL;DR**
Former Goldman Sachs CEO Lloyd Blankfein warns that the financial markets are in the late stages of a cycle characterized by a dangerous lack of discipline in opaque assets like private credit. While acknowledging AI’s potential to remove human emotion from trading, he advocates for extreme caution regarding the expansion of illiquid investments into retail retirement accounts due to the high political and regulatory stakes.

### AI: From Algorithmic Trading to "Parlor Tricks"
*   **Technological Continuum:** Blankfein views AI as an evolution of technical analysis and algorithmic trading, which historically aimed to remove human emotion—such as the fear of buying during a market dip—from investment decisions.
*   **Operational Reality:**
    *   **Headcount Neutrality:** In the short term, AI is unlikely to reduce headcount. Firms must run new AI systems in parallel with legacy systems to ensure reliability, often requiring *more* staff during the transition.
    *   **Information Quality:** Blankfein describes current AI as a "parlor trick" that provides "delightful" essays rather than the verifiable bibliographies provided by traditional search engines.
*   **Investment Risks:**
    *   **Founder-Led Risk:** Much of the $100 billion+ annual R&D in AI is driven by hyper-scale companies run by founders. Blankfein notes they are "playing with their own money," making them more likely to be right than outside observers.
    *   **Hindsight Bias:** He anticipates that while the technology will succeed, many individual company investments will inevitably be viewed as mistakes in hindsight.

### Market Cycles and the "Opaque Asset" Threat
*   **Private Credit Concerns:** Blankfein expresses concern over the growth of private credit and other "opaque assets" that lack precision in mark-to-market pricing.
    *   **Illiquidity:** These assets are difficult to test in the market because knowledgeable buyers require extensive credit analysis that is often not worth the effort for small positions.
*   **The Necessity of Volatility:** 
    *   **Loss of Discipline:** Long periods of market prosperity inevitably lead to a lack of discipline. Blankfein argues that market problems and "crashes" are necessary to purge "bad blood" and force capital allocators to be careful.
    *   **Late-Cycle Indicators:** He suggests the market is currently in the "late stages" of a cycle, making a reckoning or write-offs for illiquid assets highly probable.

### Retail Investment and Regulatory Trepidation
*   **Institutional vs. Consumer Risk:** While institutional investors and high-net-worth individuals can afford losses, Blankfein warns against moving illiquid private credit into consumer portfolios (401ks/mutual funds).
    *   **Political Consequences:** Losses for retail "taxpayers and citizens" trigger intense reactions from regulators and politicians that institutional losses do not.
*   **Franchise Risk:** He cautions firms against extending their franchise into the consumer sector during late-cycle volatility, questioning if the marginal growth is worth the potential reputational and regulatory damage.

### The Evolution of the Goldman Sachs Culture
*   **Partnership vs. Public Corporation:** Blankfein credits the firm with preserving a "partnership culture" 25 years post-IPO, defined by co-ownership, cross-departmental "busybody" behavior, and socializing decisions.
*   **Shift in Financial Priorities:**
    *   **PE Multiples:** As the ownership transitioned from partners to the public, the focus shifted from pure earnings (E) to the price-to-earnings (PE) multiple. 
    *   **Strategic Transition:** He notes that current CEO David Solomon has moved many investing opportunities off-balance-sheet to reduce volatility and satisfy public shareholders who prioritize stable earnings growth.
*   **Leadership in Crisis:** Reflecting on the 1994 bond market crisis, Blankfein emphasizes that leaders must project calm. He uses the analogy of a flight attendant during turbulence: "If you look like you're terrified, guess how the passengers are going to respond?"

### Corporate Neutrality and Political Polarization
*   **Against "Blue" and "Red" Companies:** Blankfein opposes the trend of forcing corporations to take positions on controversial social issues that are properly the domain of the political sector.
*   **The "Core Expertise" Exception:** He believes CEOs should only speak out when an issue hits their core expertise (e.g., government shutdowns and the economy) or directly impacts their employees’ ability to work (e.g., marriage equality).
*   **Skepticism of Government Intervention:** He warns against centralized government control of the economy, noting that the U.S. economy's strength lies in its "millions of decision makers" and its ability to quickly "plow over" failed investments—a process government ownership tends to slow down.
