## The brain helped us build AI. Now AI is explaining the brain.

- Channel: Google DeepMind
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K8rdrWQRd20
- Published: 2026-11-24
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20261124.html#video-K8rdrWQRd20
- Speakers: Hannah Fry (Professor); Maria Eckstein (Neuroscience Researcher, Google DeepMind); Kim Stachenfeld (Neuroscience Researcher, Google DeepMind)

---

**TL;DR:** Since 2019, the relationship between neuroscience and AI has flipped: instead of looking to the brain to build smarter machines, Google DeepMind researchers Maria Eckstein and Kim Stachenfeld are using neural networks and large language models to automatically discover the hidden algorithms driving human and animal behavior.

### The Flipped Relationship: AI as a Tool for Neuroscience
* **From Inspiration to Discovery:** Back in 2019, AI was in its infancy and researchers studied the brain's mechanisms—like memory and navigation—to build better AI. Today, Maria Eckstein notes that AI is advanced enough that the goal is no longer making AI human-like, but using AI as a tool to understand the brain's ~86 billion neurons.
* **Moving Beyond Prediction to "Why":** Kim Stachenfeld contrasts typical AI scientific breakthroughs (like protein folding, where predicting the final shape is enough) with neuroscience, where the goal is finding novel, underlying mechanisms that explain *why* data behaves the way it does.
* **The Interpretability vs. Accuracy Trade-off:** Researchers currently face a spectrum between complex "black-box" models (high predictive power, low human understanding) and simple models (easy to understand, poor predictive power). Stachenfeld's team aims to confer flexibility along this spectrum—or eventually organize complexity to achieve both.

### Rethinking Reward Learning: Theory-Driven AI (Maria Eckstein)
* **The Traditional Baseline:** Classic reinforcement learning (specifically Q-learning) assumes humans make choices by assigning values to options, experiencing a reward, and updating those values based on the discrepancy between expectation and reality (reward prediction error).
* **The Bandit Task Experiment:** Participants chose between 4 shapes on a screen to maximize points (e.g., 32 points for a green circle), with point values changing randomly over time.
  * **The Predictive Gap:** Random guessing yields 25% accuracy. The traditional Q-learning model scored in the 30s–40s% range, whereas a flexible, black-box neural network predicted **68%** of participant choices.
* **Automating "Graduate Student Descent":** Instead of humans manually tweaking Q-learning equations to account for forgetting or punishment, Eckstein's team built a hybrid model that kept the Q-learning structure but replaced hand-written equations with neural networks.
* **Key Findings:** 
  * **Refuting Q-learning:** Testing the entire class of Q-learning algorithms yielded no improvement over the baseline.
  * **Context Specificity & Complex Memory:** By relaxing Q-learning assumptions, the winning model matched the neural network's **68%** predictive ceiling while remaining interpretable. It revealed that humans evaluate rewards contextually (e.g., 50 points feels good against 10–12 points, but bad against 90 points) and use complex memory structures to track whether options are getting better or worse over time, even when outcomes are random.

### Automating Model Discovery: Data-Driven AI (Kim Stachenfeld)
* **Data Diver & Alpha Evolve:** Co-led with Kevin Miller, Stachenfeld's team built a pipeline called **Data Diver** using **Alpha Evolve** (an LLM-based system for finding problem-solving programs). Instead of starting from an existing theory like Q-learning, Data Diver generates thousands of code programs from scratch to imitate animal/human choices, then simplifies the code to prevent unreadable "spaghetti code."
* **Converging on the Same Answer:** Tested on the exact same bandit dataset, Data Diver's purely data-driven approach broadly agreed with Eckstein's theory-driven hybrid model. Both found complex memory tracking for unchosen options, alongside a working-memory strategy where the most recent reward for a chosen option simply overwrites the previous value rather than using incremental trial-and-error.
* **Cross-Species Insights:** Stachenfeld's team applied Data Diver across **5 datasets** and **4 species** (humans, rats, flies, and monkeys), finding that the discovered algorithms differed significantly by species and task environment.
* **Novelty in Monkeys:** Analyzing a monkey dataset from Vinnie Costa that introduced new choice options, Data Diver discovered a program that tracked **novelty** as a completely separate variable rather than just adding a "bonus" to an option's value. This suggests the brain may have two distinct neural substrates—one for reward and one for novelty—which could inform future neurological therapies and interventions.

### Future Directions & The Frontier of Science
* **Bridging Behavior and Neural Data:** Both researchers emphasized that the next step is connecting these behavioral algorithms with physical brain data (such as fMRI scanners or electrodes in monkeys) to validate which brain regions are actually executing these calculations.
* **Solving the Data Bottleneck:** Because data-to-model discovery requires massive amounts of data, Stachenfeld's team is exploring how to automate experimental design to collect meaningful real-world data more efficiently.
* **Removing Human Bias:** Professor Hannah Fry highlighted that traditional neuroscience models were built by humans and limited by human preconceptions; AI now allows researchers to explore vast hypothesis spaces at once and let the data speak for itself.
