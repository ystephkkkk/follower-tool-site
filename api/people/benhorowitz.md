### TL;DR
As public AI benchmarks saturate and self-reported model scores become increasingly unreliable, independent and continuously evolving evaluations are becoming critical for both enterprises measuring ROI and governments assessing safety risks.

### The Problem with Current AI Benchmarks
* **Saturation and Goodhart’s Law:** Public benchmarks are easily hacked and saturated as models optimize directly for the tests.
* **Flawed Self-Reporting:** Rayan Krishnan noted that when Meta released Llama 4, public open-source benchmarks showed incredible capabilities, but VALS's held-out private benchmarks revealed the model was actually underperforming.
* **The Need for Neutrality:** Rayan Krishnan explained that third-party evaluators must avoid conflicts of interest, drawing parallels to the Enron auditing scandal where mixing consulting with auditing corrupted incentives. VALS explicitly decided never to sell training data to labs to maintain independence.

### Enterprise ROI and the Shift to Token Spend
* **Escalating Costs:** Ben Horowitz noted the challenge of measuring fluid frontier intelligence compared to traditional Gartner quadrants. Rayan Krishnan shared an example from a Fortune 10 company where an engineer's budget increased from $100 to $300 per day, causing peak productivity to shift to 4:00 PM–6:00 PM when rate limits reset.
* **Token Spend vs. Salaries:** Rayan Krishnan recounted a VALS internal experiment where a token-maxing test resulted in spending $1.5 million in a month on free tokens—which was 10x higher than employee salaries for that month.
* **Val Smith Enterprise Product:** Rayan Krishnan introduced Val Smith, a tool allowing companies to take their GitHub codebase and build internal coding benchmarks to determine the most performant and Pareto-optimal models for their specific repositories.

### Pre-Release Infrastructure and Evolving Testing
* **The 6-Hour Pre-Release Window:** Rayan Krishnan described how VALS went from co-founders pulling all-nighters to running distributed evaluations at maximum rate limits using an internal system called "Steve, the economic VALS employee."
* **Handling Complex Workflows:** Rayan Krishnan noted that as evaluations become more complex (testing agentic work over hours, days, or weeks), sample sizes shrink while rubrics and criteria grow significantly larger (e.g., testing 50 full-stack web applications rather than millions of static images).
* **Always a Higher Peak:** Rayan Krishnan outlined VALS's unofficial motto and practice of deprecating saturated benchmarks and perpetually constructing new mountains for foundation model labs to summit.

### Specialized Indexes and Frontier Metrics
* **Economic Applications:** Rayan Krishnan highlighted VALS's finance agent benchmark (used by major financial institutions) and VI codebench for full-stack web application generation.
* **Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI):** Rayan Krishnan discussed the newly released RSI Index, which creates an apples-to-apples way to benchmark pre-training, post-training, and harness-level engineering proxies across frontier labs.

### Policy, Government Coordination, and Sovereign AI
* **Division of Labor:** Ben Horowitz compared the AI policy challenge to the MPAA rating system ("I know it when I see it"), arguing that governments are well-suited to set and enforce rules, while competent private companies must evaluate whether those rules are broken.
* **Information Sharing:** Rayan Krishnan stated that VALS regularly briefs executive and legislative branches on model capabilities and risks, helping policymakers ground abstract conversations in empirical data.
* **Global Verification:** Rayan Krishnan discussed the paradox of sovereign AI investments and suggested that international coordination on catastrophic risks (like cyber, bio, and RSI) will eventually require a shared language of evaluations, comparing it to Reagan's "trust but verify" doctrine for nuclear stockpiles.

* **Managing Growth and Governance:** Reflecting on his 2017 departure from Uber, Kalanick notes that while he stands by every decision, he pushed too close to the line and failed to realize that big enterprises must transition from an upstart "pirate" mentality to a disciplined "navy" framework.
* **Aggressive Tactics:** Kalanick shares that internal programs like "shoplifting" (aggressively recruiting drivers away from Lyft) had to be rebranded to harmless names like the North American Championship Series (NACS) after pushback from board members like David Drummond and legal training on antitrust rules.
* **Post-Uber Hell:** Kalanick spent 7-8 months post-Uber fighting lawsuits and investigations before finding his next venture.

### Building in Stealth: The 8-Year Journey of Atoms
* **Origins as Cloud Kitchens:** Following his exit from Uber, Kalanick partnered with a real estate savant who had started Cloud Kitchens (originally incorporated as City Storage Systems), a multi-tenant delivery-only commercial kitchen concept. Kalanick acquired the company and scaled it into a global operation.
* **Operating in Hard Mode:** To escape the 150 negative media articles a day from his final days at Uber, Kalanick operated in strict stealth for nearly 8 years, growing to thousands of employees across 30 countries (operating under various local names like Kitchen Valley in Korea, Flash Kitchen in China, and Food Stars in London) without the New York Times knowing.
* **The Cultural Advantage of Stealth:** Operating in stealth starved the company of external validation and fame-seeking, forcing internal correctness, emotional intelligence, and genuine fulfillment from the daily work rather than press clippings.

### Digitizing the Physical World: The Atoms-Based Computer
* **The Atoms Framework:** Kalanick explains his thesis that physical world industries can be modeled using computer science architecture: 
  * **Manufacturing** = CPU (manipulates atoms)
  * **Real Estate** = Storage (stores atoms)
  * **Transport & Logistics** = Network (moves atoms)
* **The Food Computer Vision:** The ultimate goal for Atoms Food is to make the preparation and delivery of a quality meal so efficient that it approaches the grocery store cost, effectively doing to the kitchen what Uber did to the car. This requires automated manufacturing (food robotics) and robotic couriers to drop distribution costs from $12 per meal down to $0.50–$1.00.
* **Expanding into Mining (Pronto):** Atoms acquired Pronto, an off-road autonomy and mining automation company where Kalanick was an early investor. The business targets a 20% increase in productivity and critical safety improvements for gold and mineral mines globally.

### Industrial AI and the Future of Physical Automation
* **Industrial AI Stack:** Kalanick defines the emerging category of Industrial AI as a practical stack of software, sensors, robotics, and machinery designed to automate heavy, multi-trillion-dollar physical industries one at a time—starting with food, mining, and transport.
* **Parallels to the Second Industrial Revolution:** Horowitz and Kalanick draw strong parallels between today's regulatory and public resistance to physical AI, data centers, and industrial automation and the struggles faced by historical industrialists like Carnegie, Rockefeller, and Ford.

### The Strategic Shift to Global Tech Leadership and Western Values

* **The Imperative of US Tech Leadership:** Ben Horowitz emphasizes that preserving America’s status as a land of opportunity requires dominant leadership across economic, cultural, military, and technological spheres.

### Technology as the Arena of National Power and Security

* **Deterrence Shift to Software Innovation:** Anne Neuberger notes that national deterrence is no longer defined strictly by the physical size of a military, but by the pace of software innovation. 
    * **Private Sector Dominance:** Critical defense capabilities—including autonomous systems, AI integration, and cyber defense—are now built in the private sector rather than inside government laboratories.
* **Geopolitical Chokepoints:** Technology has shifted from a tool of diplomacy to the actual arena of national power. Semiconductor supply chains, active pharmaceutical ingredients, and critical software vulnerabilities are now leveraged as foundational economic and national security tools.
* **The Evolution of Cybersecurity Threats:** Cybersecurity has transitioned through three distinct historical phases:
    1. *Vandalism:* Early desktop computer disruption.
    2. *Theft:* Financial crimes and ransomware enabled by the early commercial internet.
    3. *Warfare:* State-level attacks targeting critical physical infrastructure (e.g., rural healthcare networks, power grids, and water systems).

### A New Playbook for Startup Internationalization

* **Accelerated Global Footprints:** Raghu Raghuram explains that because digital products travel instantly via APIs, startups can no longer wait until they have hundreds of millions in revenue to go international; they must scale globally much earlier.
* **Targeting Top-Heavy Economies:** Rather than spending $5M to $10M upfront to establish localized legal entities and hire large regional teams, startups should leverage relationships with the small handful of top corporations and government entities that drive the majority of GDP in target countries.

### The Myth of a "Distributed" Silicon Valley

* **Why Physical Ecosystems Still Matter:** Ben Horowitz disputes the notion that the internet has democratized innovation to the point of distributing Silicon Valley everywhere. He argues that local policies, cultures, and laws remain highly divergent and determinative of success.

## The Media Game Has Changed

- Channel: a16z
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XROaLetSxg0
- Published: 2026-06-19
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20260619.html#video-XROaLetSxg0
- Speakers: Marc Andreessen (Co-founder, Andreessen Horowitz); Ben Horowitz (Co-founder, Andreessen Horowitz); Erik Torenberg (Co-founder, Turpentine)

---

**TL;DR**  
The media landscape has shifted from a defensive, centralized era of corporate branding to a decentralized "go-direct" era where individual authenticity is the primary competitive advantage. To build a modern audience, founders must abandon sterile media-trained personas, embrace being interesting (even at the cost of drawing critics), and frame their companies through "outside-in" global narratives rather than self-focused product updates.

---

### ## The Transition from Legacy Media to a "Go Direct" Strategy

* **The Fall of Legacy Media Relevance:** Since 2017, traditional media has shifted from objective, explanatory journalism to an adversarial, activist posture. 
    * **No Winning Strategy:** Marc Andreessen emphasizes that it is virtually impossible for a tech startup to secure a consistently fair or positive narrative through traditional press anymore. Individual hits are possible, but a sustainable legacy PR strategy is dead.
    * **The Prestige Shift:** Prestige has migrated to decentralized channels. Even in Washington, D.C., key decision-makers and senators now prioritize independent newsletters like Mark Halperin’s over legacy institutions like *The Washington Post*.
* **The "Go Direct" Mandate:** Companies must bypass traditional gatekeepers and tell their stories through owned channels and allied networks.
* **Fights as Brand Accelerators:** Ben Horowitz notes that responding strategically to major public attacks can dramatically elevate a brand. 
    * **The Instagram Kerfuffle:** A public pushback by a16z against *The New York Times* regarding Instagram became their highest-performing content piece of that era because "everyone loves a fight."
    * **The Engagement Rule:** Ignore minor trolls (e.g., low-follower accounts on X), but aggressively seize high-profile adversarial moments to clarify your worldview.

### ## Authenticity and the "Brand as Person" Era

* **The Decline of "Plastic" Media Training:** In the 1990s, corporate executives were trained to be as boring and innocuous as possible to minimize risk. Modern communication requires the exact opposite: speaking in public with the same unfiltered visceral energy you would use when having lunch with a friend.
* **The Person is the Brand:** 
    * **Historical Context:** In the 1930s and 40s, the rise of highly centralized media (three TV networks, limited print space) forced companies to distill their identities into abstract, corporate brands (e.g., General Motors, IBM, Tide). 
    * **The Decentralized Return:** In the internet era, distribution channels are unlimited, and human connection is paramount. Audiences associate companies with distinct personalities rather than abstract logos: Elon Musk represents SpaceX, Alex Karp represents Palantir, and Palmer Luckey represents Anduril.
* **The "Joe Rogan" Bar:** Modern leaders must be capable of sitting through three-hour, unscripted podcast discussions. If a founder cannot hold an audience's attention in this format, they face a severe marketing deficit.
* **The Danger of Neutrality:** To be interesting, you must accept polarization. If nobody hates you, nobody loves you either. Attempting to please everyone results in uninteresting, sterile corporate messaging.

### ## Mastering "Outside-In" Narrative Strategy

* **The Inside-Out Trap:** Most startups rely on self-absorbed narratives ("We raise money, we went on a walk, we launched a product"). These narratives are boring, indistinguishable, and fail to engage anyone outside the company's immediate bubble.
* **The Outside-In Hack:** Position your startup within a massive, highly compelling global trend that is already captured in the public consciousness.
    * **The Alex Karp Playbook:** The Palantir CEO rarely explains what Palantir does in technical terms. Instead, he addresses global conflict, AI integration in the military, and geopolitics. Because he is the loudest voice on these critical topics, Palantir becomes the default solution in the minds of the public and policymakers.
    * **The Ryan Petersen Playbook:** Rather than pitching Flexport as a freight-forwarding company, Petersen went on *60 Minutes* during the COVID-19 pandemic to discuss global supply chain collapse and food security. The connection to Flexport's business remained implicit but incredibly powerful.
* **Enterprise Credibility:** Enterprise customers and government officials do not want to meet with a generic vendor; they want to meet with leaders who understand the macroeconomic and geopolitical forces shaping the world.

### ## Tactics and Pitfalls for New Media Teams

* **Message Over Distribution:** Gaby Goldberg points out that founders frequently over-index on distribution tactics (e.g., "How do we go viral?" or "How do we get on Joe Rogan?") before defining a clear message. Strong distribution on a weak message only amplifies irrelevance.
* **Work Backward From Outcomes:** Start with your business objectives—such as recruiting top-tier engineers or closing enterprise sales—and design the message specifically for that target audience, rather than vomiting every corporate milestone into the public domain.
* **Hiring for the New Paradigm:** 
    * **Avoid Legacy PR Mindsets:** Traditional PR professionals who have spent decades vetting reporters and managing defensive Q&As find it incredibly difficult to operate in a direct-to-audience, offensive framework. 
    * **Hire Native Storytellers:** Build marketing and communication teams around individuals who have a proven track record of creating compelling content, building organic audiences, and understanding the digital "discourse."
