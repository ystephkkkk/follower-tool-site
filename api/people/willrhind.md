## ETF Edge on how the Nvidia saga impacts leveraged funds

### TL;DR
Nvidia’s board unanimously reinstated CEO Jensen Huang, sparking a 30% stock surge and record-setting trading volumes across leveraged single-stock ETFs like GraniteShares' 2x long Nvidia fund (NVDL). While retail traders are driving explosive growth in leveraged AI and tech products, ETF Action’s Mike Akins warns that compounding decay and poor market timing have cost investors roughly $15 billion since 2022.

### The Nvidia Whiplash & Record ETF Volumes
- **Nvidia’s Reversal:** Following a tumultuous period where Nvidia’s board stated founder and CEO Jensen Huang would not return, the board made a complete U-turn and unanimously reinstated him, triggering a 30% surge—price action not seen in Nvidia since the early 2000s.
- **Exploding Trading Volume:** 
  - Mike Akins noted that both the semiconductor levered ETF (SOXL) and NVDL were already trading well over 100% of their typical full-day volume mid-session.
  - Will Rhind reported that NVDL—the world's largest leveraged Nvidia ETF—was on pace to trade more than $2 billion on the day, potentially setting its highest-ever daily volume record.
- **Anthropic ETFs Overshadowed:** GraniteShares launched the first 2x long (AIL) and 2x short (ANS) Anthropic ETFs the same day, which Rhind described as a "sideshow" compared to the Nvidia frenzy.
- **Market Health Concerns:** Both Akins and Rhind cautioned that extreme whipsaw moves—large drawdowns followed by massive spikes—are historically unhealthy and typically signal recessionary or bear-market conditions, which Akins believes currently characterize the AI trade.

### Mechanics, Retail Dominance, and Investor Timing
- **Daily Reset vs. Long-Term Compounding:** Akins emphasized that leveraged single-stock ETFs are strictly single-day trading vehicles, not buy-and-hold investments.
  - Over the three years ending the prior Friday's close, Nvidia was up 150%, while 2x long NVDL was up only 100%—trailing the 300% return an investor might naively expect due to compounding effects.
- **Retail-Driven Ownership:** According to 13F institutional reporting as of September 30, 97% of NVDL’s ownership is "unknown," indicating it is overwhelmingly a retail trading vehicle.
- **The $15 Billion Timing Gap:** Since 2022, roughly $33 billion has flowed into single-stock leveraged ETFs, yet total assets sat at just $17 billion as of Friday's close—a ~$15 billion delta that Akins attributed to poor market timing by investors.

### Fee Structures vs. Traditional Leverage
- **Competitive Packaging:** Akins noted that while leveraged ETFs carry higher expense ratios than standard index funds, they offer retail investors at brokerages like Schwab or Fidelity a simpler, more liquid alternative to managing options or margin accounts, with no risk of margin calls.
- **Apples-to-Apples Cost Comparison:** Rhind pushed back on fee criticism, arguing that leveraged ETFs should be compared to margin loans, options, or structured products rather than a Vanguard S&P 500 ETF.
  - Because the funds target 2x daily movement, the fee equates to less than 1 basis point per day (a fraction of 0.01%), making it "practically free" for daily traders compared to traditional leverage.

### Industry Proliferation & Product Lifecycle
- **Rapid Sector Expansion:** Akins highlighted massive growth in single-stock leveraged ETFs:
  - **End of 2022:** 22 funds totaling $900 million in AUM.
  - **Prior to recent tech selloff:** Eclipsed $45 billion in AUM.
  - **As of Friday's close:** 388 funds totaling $18 billion in AUM.
- **What Drives New Launches:** Rhind explained that GraniteShares targets names with high investor enthusiasm, volume, and volatility—noting that low-volatility stocks like Berkshire Hathaway fail to gain traction in leveraged wrappers, while AI and crypto names thrive.
- **Reduced Taboo Around Closures:** Rhind noted that leveraged ETFs only survive as long as underlying trader interest persists, and the industry is seeing more fund closures alongside back-to-back record launch years as issuers become more willing to shut down low-demand strategies.
