### TL;DR
AI agents are transitioning from spectators to active market participants by securely accessing payment credentials, sparking a "wallet wars" race to connect agents with existing payment methods. Infrastructure providers like VGS are facilitating this shift by issuing secure agentic tokens that enable AI transactions while preserving consumer protections, fraud checks, and merchant relationships.

### The Evolution of Payment Infrastructure & Vaulting
- **The Core Role of VGS:** Explained by Terry Angelos as securing credentials for roughly one-third of all e-commerce by Gross Merchandise Value (GMV), serving major online merchants, neo-banks, and frontier AI labs by taking PCI DSS compliance completely out of scope.
- **The Shift from PANs to Network Tokens:** Explained by Terry Angelos as moving away from vaulting raw 16-digit primary account numbers (PANs) toward domain-bound network tokens issued by Visa, Mastercard, AMEX, and Discover to enhance security on specific merchant domains.
- **Multi-Processor Sharding:** Described by Alex Rampell and Terry Angelos as a requirement for merchants with over $1 billion in GMV, who typically use 3 to 5 payment processors to optimize costs, approval rates, and regional routing—multiplying the complexity of vaulting and card updates.
- **Card Turnover and Updates:** Highlighted by Terry Angelos that about 25% of cards expire annually due to a standard four-year lifecycle, with an additional 5% turning over due to fraud, portfolio acquisitions (e.g., Capital One buying Discover), or network switches—complications that large, neutral vaults resolve automatically.

### The Rise of Agentic Commerce & The Wallet Wars
- **Agents with Arms and Legs:** Described by Terry Angelos as a major leap over the last 3 months where AI assistants (like Muse, Instinct, and Town) have evolved from passive recommendation tools to actors capable of completing end-to-end purchasing tasks (such as booking piano teachers or finding chandeliers) once granted safe payment access.
- **The Wallet Wars:** Highlighted by Terry Angelos as consumer and merchant wallets rushing to integrate with AI agents based on consumer reach and merchant footprint:
  - **Stripe Link:** Reaches ~300 million consumers and ~1 million merchants.
  - **Shopify Shop Pay:** Connects with ~1.7 million merchants.
  - **PayPal:** Encompasses over 400 million accounts and ~30 million merchants.
- **Stripe as the "AOL" of Agentic Commerce:** Compared by Terry Angelos to early internet walled gardens; Stripe handles transactions by issuing virtual one-time-use Visa cards when direct links to merchants (like Walmart) are absent, though this workaround creates friction with chargebacks and merchant re-engagement.

### Open Standards, Agentic Tokens, and Merchant Friction
- **The New Agentic Token Standard:** Detailed by Terry Angelos as a 16-digit tokenized credential issued via networks that allows wallets to hand safe parameters (such as spending caps and agent verification) to AI agents, preserving traditional chargeback mechanisms and post-purchase engagement.
- **VGS's Growth in Tokens:** Noted by Terry Angelos that VGS went from zero agentic tokens 3 months ago to processing a few million tokens per week across agents, wallets, and merchants.
- **Merchant Pushback (Amazon vs. Walmart):** Discussed by Terry Angelos and Alex Rampell as a strategic divide: Amazon blocks third-party agents like Muse to protect its high-margin advertising business and direct consumer eyeballs, whereas Walmart and SMBs under Shopify are more receptive or view agents as vital sales channels.

### Transactional Security: The Three Checks of Commerce
- **Step 1 (Card Validity):** Outlined by Alex Rampell as verifying whether a credit card number is structurally valid via checksums (mod 10 check) and checking with the issuing bank.
- **Step 2 (Identity & Ownership):** Described by Alex Rampell as verifying the human or agent owner via cryptographic identity, similar to legacy practices like checking photo IDs and signatures for large purchases.
- **Step 3 (Sound Mind/Delegated Authority):** Explored by Alex Rampell and Terry Angelos as verifying buyer intent and preventing errors or hallucinations (e.g., preventing an agent from buying the wrong hotel dates or unauthorized high-value items), with delegated authority and liability remaining largely unresolved.

### Over-Hyped vs. Under-Hyped Trends
- **Over-Hyped (Financial Volatility):** Dismissed by Terry Angelos as unlikely to cause bank runs or extreme volatility in savings deposits through automated yield-maximizing agents due to existing banking friction and bill-pay integrations.
- **Under-Hyped (Unmonetized Demand & Agent Savings):** Claimed by Terry Angelos as the hidden expansion of GDP through AI agents continuously identifying consumer needs in group chats, alongside agents saving consumers money on forgotten subscriptions that can be redirected toward new, desired purchases.

### TL;DR
Keith Peiris shares the story of how his team abandoned Tome (an AI presentation tool with 25 million users) after realizing it lacked the context to serve as an indispensable business tool, ultimately pivoting to found Lightfield—an AI-native CRM and "business world model" that recently raised a $47 million Series A led by a16z.

### The Tome Journey & The Hard Pivot
* **The Tome Success & Limitations:** 
  * Started Tome with three former Facebook founders out of five founding members, targeting idea storytelling and presentations.
  * Reached 2 million users a month around the launch of GPT-3 and ChatGPT.
  * Ultimately decided to walk away because the technology was constrained to individuals and students; the model lacked context regarding the presenter, the audience, and their relationship.
* **Transitioning to B2B:** 
  * Followed B2B sales and marketing users from Tome, running free pilots with twelve 500-to-1000-person companies.
  * Realized that while customers wanted pitch decks, they also needed help with research, lead qualification, and company expansion.
  * Discovered that the hardest underlying problem was making sense of incomplete, conflicting data scattered across disparate systems (CRMs, call recorders, data warehouses).

### Building Lightfield & Reimagining the CRM
* **Negative Pricing Strategy:** 
  * After initial go-to-market assistant software failed to gain pricing power, the team built a CRM in the dark for 4 months.
  * Offered free office space in exchange for using their early CRM, attracting 10 startups that provided hourly feedback via Slack.
* **The "Business World Model" Architecture:** 
  * Modeled the relationship chronology first—building an activity log (emails, calls, meetings, documents, product usage, payments) rather than forcing manual data entry into rigid schemas.
  * Employs a semi-structured approach: storing vast amounts of unstructured data in the activity log, allowing AI to infer causality and power CRM updates, field filling, and workflows.
* **Real-World Impact:** 
  * Customer *Power* uses Lightfield to aggregate patients with illnesses (like Alzheimer's) and match them with frontier clinical trials and pharmaceutical companies by scraping the FDA and clinicaltrials.gov.

### Greenfield vs. Brownfield & Enterprise Strategy
* **Navigating the Brownfield:** 
  * Initially targeted new companies via LinkedIn prospecting to YC companies, growing with startups from 0 reps to 100 reps.
  * Avoided superficial features like basic email sending (which incumbents like Salesforce easily replicate), focusing instead on high-fidelity business modeling and company reality.
* **Overcoming the "Seasoned VP of Sales" Objection:** 
  * Offered the product for free across entire greenfield companies (engineering, customer support, finance, sales).
  * Created internal network effects where departments outside of sales rely on Lightfield, making it harder to rip and replace when a traditional, Salesforce-trained VP of Sales is eventually hired.

### Pragmatic Product Design & Flexible Pricing
* **Pragmatic UI Overdogmatism:** 
  * Blends natural language agents with traditional spreadsheet, table, and dashboard views because users still rely on familiar, deterministic workflows while transitioning to agentic workflows (e.g., chat-based sequences replacing complex trigger-and-arrow rule builders).
* **The Pricing Model:** 
  * Moved away from pure seat pricing (where power users consumed 10,000x more than others) and pure consumption pricing (where users hesitated to touch anything).
  * Landed on a **platform fee plus seat pricing** for core CRM workflows, combined with **consumption pricing** for pipeline generation, enrichment, workflow automations, and intelligence/forecasting.

### Company Culture, Speed, and Pivoting
* **Generalist-First Culture:** 
  * Rebuilt company operations to eliminate siloed "swim lanes" and playing house; with ~40 employees, everyone attends daily standups to stack-rank and tackle the most important problems (delivery, engineering, or customer success).
* **Continuous Planning:** 
  * Maintains a low bar for starting projects but a high bar for shipping (using company-wide bug bashes inspired by Instagram).
* **Advice on Pivoting:** 
  * Ignore outside noise, focus maniacally on customer pain, and be willing to completely abandon past work to follow the heat.

- **TL;DR:** Fintech pioneers Max Levchin and Alex Rampell discuss the 25-year evolution of digital payments, the origins of Affirm, and why agentic AI is poised to finally disrupt the ultimate user interface: the credit card.

### 25 Years of Fintech Evolution
- **The Best User Interface:** Alex Rampell and Max Levchin highlight that the credit card is arguably the singular best user interface ever created, serving the world's largest market where "there are no niches in payments smaller than $100 billion."
- **The Apple Pay / Google Pay Shift:** 
  - Alex notes that changing consumer behavior is notoriously difficult, but a convergence of three factors made mobile wallets ubiquitous: merchant liability shifts for chip readers, a mandatory hardware upgrade cycle (EMV switch), and ubiquitous mobile smartphones.
  - Max adds that Visa and Mastercard enforce a hard 2.5-second interaction limit at physical points of sale, leaving little room for offline innovation. Apple and Google Pay successfully time-shifted this by using secure enclaves inside chips to handle processing offline before talking to the networks.
- **The Crypto Reality:** Max recounts attending the bankruptcy party for DigiCash (*David Chaum's* project) and later being booed off stage for pitching a non-anonymous digital payments idea. While Bitcoin succeeded as a currency, store of value, and commodity, neither Max nor Alex are convinced it ever became a practical payment method for everyday purchases like a cup of coffee.

### The Origin Story of Affirm
- **From "Bill Me Later" to "Expedite":** 
  - In April 2011, Max and Alex began emailing about alternatives to Bill Me Later, initially exploring a B2B "pay me sooner" accounts receivable/payable financing idea before realizing consumer convenience offered a larger revenue opportunity.
  - The company was originally incorporated as **Expedite Software Inc.** (briefly known as "Expedite") before officially becoming **Affirm**.
- **The "Pajama Problem" Hypothesis:** Early on, the founders thought they were solving the "pajama problem" (wanting to buy something online while your wallet is downstairs) by enabling users to "pay with your identity" using Facebook data and social graphs.
- **The Breakthrough at Beautylish:** 
  - Initial tests with merchants like 1800 Flowers stalled because merchants felt Affirm was cannibalizing credit card volume while charging higher merchant discount rates (MDR).
  - The breakthrough occurred when online cosmetics retailer **Beautylish** placed installment options **up-funnel** (during product selection rather than at final checkout), instantly increasing conversion rates by **30%**.
- **The Mattress Boom & Direct-to-Consumer (DTC):** Mattresses (companies like Casper and Purple) became a massive driver because their 80% gross margins and high price points made merchants willing to subsidize the MDR, enabling true 0% interest loans.

### Business Model Innovations: Negative CAC and True 0%
- **Negative Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC):** Max and Alex emphasize that Affirm possesses a rare competitive advantage: negative CAC. Because Affirm acts as the financial intermediary and handles customer communications and collections, merchants are happy to offload those operational burdens.
- **The War on Fake 0% Financing:** Max expresses strong opposition to "fake 0% financing" (deferred interest credit cards that retroactively charge high interest if a balance is missed by a penny or a day), cementing Affirm's core brand promise: no late fees, no deferred interest, and zero negative surprises.
- **Long-Term Underwriting:** Affirm stands out from competitors by issuing longer-term loans (up to **3.5 years** / **39 installments**), requiring sophisticated machine learning models to manage default and delinquency rates while creating multiple touchpoints to upsell consumers.

### The PayPal Mafia Legacy
- **By Design:** Max explains that PayPal intentionally attracted and selected for future entrepreneurs, often asking departing employees if this was their last company (hoping the answer was no, serving as a "graduate degree in entrepreneurship").
- **De-deifying Tech Icons:** Working in sweaty, high-stress rooms exposed the human side of figures like Elon Musk, Peter Teal, and David Sacks—proving they were normal people filled with doubt, which inspired peers to pursue ambitious ventures themselves.

### Predictions & Forward Calls
- **Adoption of Agentic Commerce:** Max Rampell predicts that agentic shopping adoption will be relatively slow ("slowly at first, then all at once"), noting that people still want personal control over discretionary items like bike parts rather than outsourcing taste to a robot.
- **Disruption of Payment Interfaces:** Max Rampell predicts that AI agents will finally force a renegotiation of the credit card payment interface because agents are smarter than plastic cards.

## Ben Horowitz on AI Anxiety, Big Tech Transitions & The Future of Startups | a16z

- Channel: a16z

---

**TL;DR:** AI has fundamentally altered the "laws of physics" for tech competition by making capital a viable substitute for time and eroding traditional software moats, necessitating a massive rebuild of physical infrastructure and a shift toward cryptographic trust systems.

### The New "Laws of Physics" for Software
The historical rules of building and defending a software company have been upended by AI.

*   **Capital as a Substitute for Time:** It was previously a truism that "nine women can't have a baby in a month" (the mythical man-month). In the AI era, this is no longer true; if a company is behind, they can now throw massive amounts of money at GPUs and data to solve almost any software problem and catch up to a competitor.
*   **The Erosion of Moats:** Traditional software moats—including migration pain, data lock-in, and user interface familiarity—are disappearing.
    *   **Portability:** It is now easy to replicate code and move data.
    *   **Agentic Interaction:** Because AI agents, rather than humans, will increasingly interact with software, they are indifferent to UI/UX "stickiness."
*   **Compressed Timelines:** The window to establish a product has shrunk from years to weeks. Financial markets are currently devaluing SaaS companies because of significant doubts regarding their "terminal value."

### Survival Strategies for Legacy SaaS
Not every pre-AI company is "roadkill," but survival depends on the nature of the service provided.

*   **Physical and Relationship Moats:** Companies like **Navan** (travel) remain resilient because their value is tied to complex, real-world infrastructure. 
    *   **Offline Complexity:** Providing global travel requires explicit relationships with thousands of airlines, hotels, and rail systems that an AI cannot simply "generate."
    *   **Channel Access:** Legacy companies often hold the relationship with the "travel manager" or internal gatekeeper—a channel that AI labs like OpenAI have no immediate interest in building.
*   **The "Hostage" Model:** Alex Rampell noted that the best companies have "hostages, not customers," where the cost of extraction remains high due to deep integration into core business logic.

### The Looming Infrastructure Bottleneck
The primary constraint on AI advancement has shifted from software and chips to physical capacity and power.

*   **The Power Crisis:** The U.S. is currently facing an immediate shortage of electricity. While Nvidia will eventually produce enough chips, the grid cannot support the vertical demand for tokens.
*   **Rebuilding America:** Horowitz argues the U.S. must rebuild its entire industrial base, specifically focusing on:
    *   **Rare Earth Minerals:** Securing the raw materials for high-tech manufacturing.
    *   **Transformers:** Investing in hardware like power transformers, which haven't seen significant innovation since the invention of electricity.
*   **The Supply Chain Gap:** There are massive latencies in the supply chain; building a DRAM factory or a new power plant takes years, whereas AI demand scales instantly.

### The Essential Intersection of AI and Crypto
As AI makes communication and digital content "unusable" due to deepfakes and mass personalization, crypto becomes the necessary layer for trust.

*   **Proof of Personhood:** With CAPTCHAs becoming anachronistic, cryptographic keys will be required to distinguish humans from bots in social media, dating apps, and Zoom calls.
*   **Proof of Truth:** To combat deepfakes, content will need to be "signed" using mathematical, game-theoretic properties of the blockchain rather than relying on a centralized authority (e.g., Google or the Government).
*   **AI as Economic Actors:** AIs cannot open traditional bank accounts or become credit card merchants easily. They require "internet money"—bearer instruments like crypto—to act as independent economic actors.
*   **Fraud Prevention:** Horowitz cites the ~$450 billion stolen during U.S. stimulus programs as proof that the government needs better digital address systems (crypto wallets) for distributing funds.

### The Future of Work and Venture Capital
Despite "AI anxiety," historical precedents suggest technological shifts lead to greater abundance and higher employment in unforeseen sectors.

*   **The Keynesian Fallacy:** John Maynard Keynes famously predicted a 15-hour work week due to efficiency. He was wrong because he didn't account for the fact that human "wants" become "needs" instantly (e.g., from needing one car to needing a car for every person).
*   **New Needs:** As AI commoditizes current white-collar tasks, humans will invent new, labor-intensive needs—such as high-end travel or elaborate "foodie" experiences—that don't exist yet.
*   **Entrepreneurship for All:** AI lowers the barrier to entry so that 8 billion people can manifest their ideas without needing a "capital gate." This could lead to a future where everyone is an entrepreneur, creating a significantly larger market for venture capital.

## Is This the Biggest Software Shift of the Decade? | a16z Interview - Atlassian CEO

- Channel: a16z
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0lzo2tFBFy8

- Speakers: Mike Cannon-Brookes (Cofounder and CEO, Atlassian); Alex Rampell (General Partner, a16z); Erik Torenberg (Host)

---

**TL;DR**
The software industry is transitioning from a 60-year era of "digital filing cabinets" (databases) to an era where software actively executes work; success in this shift depends on moving from static systems of record to dynamic "systems of process" while solving the massive design challenge of human-AI trust.

### The "SaaS-pocalypse" and Valuation Frameworks
The public markets are currently struggling to value software businesses during a period of high disruption, leading to a "wait and see" investor sentiment. Alex Rampell (a16z) categorizes the risk into three buckets of SaaS companies:

*   **Bucket 1: Seats Tied to Outcomes (High Risk):** Platforms where pricing is based on human seats performing a task (e.g., Zendesk). If AI can perform the task, the need for seats—and thus the revenue model—collapses unless they pivot to outcome-based pricing.
*   **Bucket 2: Seats as a Pricing Proxy (Low Risk):** Platforms where seats are used for "fairness" but don't produce the direct outcome (e.g., Workday). These are safer because the software remains the essential system of record, and AI simply makes the record-keeping more efficient.
*   **Bucket 3: The Creative Middle Ground:** Platforms like Adobe, where AI might reduce some seats but increase the productivity and value of others.

### From Filing Cabinets to Systems of Process
Mike Cannon-Brookes (Atlassian) argues that the definition of software is fundamentally changing from a storage medium to a processing agent.

*   **The Historical Context:** From 1960 to 2022, software was essentially taking a physical filing cabinet and turning it into a database (e.g., Sabre for flight records, Salesforce for CRMs). The cabinet "couldn't think."
*   **The AI Shift:** The "cool thing" now is that the filing cabinet can do the work itself. Instead of a human retrieving a file from QuickBooks, QuickBooks can actively collect accounts receivable.
*   **Input vs. Output Constraints:** 
    *   **Input-Constrained:** Processes like customer service or legal where the goal is efficiency (you don't want more legal questions, just faster answers).
    *   **Output-Constrained:** Processes like software development or marketing where AI can unlock unlimited creative value and output.
*   **The "Vibe Coding" Reality:** While Alex Rampell views building entire enterprise systems via "vibe coding" (natural language prompting) as preposterous due to hidden edge cases (e.g., complex state laws), Mike Cannon-Brookes sees it as a revolutionary tool for **extensibility**—allowing non-technical users to build custom "mini-apps" on top of stable platforms.

### The Paradox of Software Pricing
A significant portion of the briefing examines why moving away from "per-seat" pricing is difficult despite the rise of AI agents.

*   **Predictability and Fairness:** Per-seat pricing persists because it feels "fair" and is highly predictable for budget planning. 
*   **The "Casino Chip" Problem:** Consumption-based "AI credits" are unpopular with enterprises because they feel like opaque casino chips. Customers lose control over costs when a vendor adds a new AI feature that suddenly spikes credit usage without warning.
*   **The Outcome-Based Trap:** Outcome-based pricing (e.g., "we save you $10 per ticket") is a great sales pitch in Year 1, but creates friction in Year 2 when the customer no longer has a "pre-AI" baseline to compare against.

### The Next Frontier: Design, Trust, and Agents
Mike Cannon-Brookes emphasizes that the biggest bottleneck for AI in the enterprise isn't model capability, but design and user trust.

*   **The Skeuomorphic Phase:** Much like early mobile apps just mimicked desktop websites, current AI is stuck in the "chat box" phase. True AI design requires moving beyond the "Harry Potter spell" (complex prompting) toward seamless agentic workflows.
*   **The "50 Interns" Problem:** Managing 50 AI agents is like managing 50 interns—if they ask 50 questions a minute to verify their work, the manager becomes the bottleneck. Design must solve how agents "show their work" without being annoying.
*   **Atlassian’s Strategy:**
    *   **Teamwork Graph:** Using organizational data to give AI context so it understands not just *what* is being done, but *who* is doing it and *why*.
    *   **Rovo:** Atlassian’s new tool that moves from a blank page to a collaborative "chat-and-edit" paradigm, where the AI acts as a researcher and editor rather than just a generator.

## Ramp founder Eric Glyman on the many ways AI is changing corporate spending

- Channel: Stripe
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TRPCRIhrYac
- Published: 2026-02-17
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20260217.html#video-TRPCRIhrYac
- Speakers: Eric Glyman (CEO, Ramp); John Collison (Cofounder, Stripe); Alex Rampell (General Partner, Andreessen Horowitz)

---

**TL;DR**
Ramp has scaled to over $1 billion in revenue in just seven years by evolving from a corporate card provider into a comprehensive finance automation platform that leverages AI agents to "sell time," automating complex tasks like expense auditing and procurement with 99% accuracy.

### Business Scale and "Multi-Front Door" Growth
*   **Rapid Scaling:** Ramp surpassed $1 billion in annual revenue in roughly seven years. While card interchange remains the largest single portion, the business has diversified rapidly into Bill Pay, Treasury, and Procurement.
*   **Revenue Diversification:** 
    *   **Bill Pay:** A two-year-old business line already generating over $100 million in revenue.
    *   **Treasury:** A one-year-old product with several billion dollars in deposits.
    *   **Shift in Profit Mix:** By the end of this year, non-card business lines are expected to comprise the majority of Ramp’s total business.
*   **Customer Impact:** The average Ramp customer cuts expenses by 5% annually and grows revenue at 16%—significantly higher than the 5% U.S. national average.
*   **New Acquisition Channels:** Companies are increasingly joining Ramp through "front doors" other than the card, such as using the platform specifically for bill payments or procurement automation.

### The Agentic Revolution in Expense Management
*   **AI Audit Scale:** Ramp currently processes over 100,000 expenses per day using agentic AI. 
*   **Accuracy over Humans:** These agents review transactions against plain-English company policies with over 99% accuracy, outperforming human managers who often view expense review as a low-priority task.
*   **Contextual Reasoning:** Eric Glyman notes that AI can handle "moral code" nuances—such as allowing a luxury hotel stay if it directly correlates to closing a multi-million dollar deal—which traditional "if-then" software could never manage.
*   **Regulatory Compliance:** The system allows for the legally required "separation of duties" by having an agent perform the review and a human provide the final sign-off, removing the manual labor from the process.

### AI Engineering and the "SaaS-pocalypse"
*   **Role Blurring:** AI is collapsing traditional departmental silos. At Ramp, marketing and customer support staff are now "shipping code" to production to solve immediate problems without waiting for dedicated engineering cycles.
*   **Code as a Liability:** In the AI era, lines of code are viewed as liabilities rather than assets. Glyman predicts "self-healing" codebases where models rewrite underlying code annually to optimize for a desired outcome, effectively eliminating technical debt.
*   **The "Dark Matter" Moat:** Alex Rampell argues that while AI can easily clone a user interface, it cannot easily replicate the "dark matter" of a successful product—the thousands of edge cases and institutional knowledge gained through years of operation.
*   **Feature vs. Company:** AI makes it easier to build "feature-companies" (e.g., PagerDuty or simple expense apps), which may lead to a "SaaS-pocalypse" for businesses that don't provide deep, proprietary data value.

### The Future of Corporate Banking and Treasury
*   **The Yield Gap:** Traditional banks often enjoy high profit margins by sharing almost no yield with customers (national average for business checking is roughly 0.07%).
*   **Automated Capital Allocation:** Modern treasury products aim to eliminate "slack" in the system by automatically moving "walking around money" into high-yield accounts until the exact moment payroll or bills are due.
*   **The Capital One Blueprint:** Glyman highlights Capital One as the "Czerny of Fintech," citing their "Information-Based Strategy" (IBS) and culture of hiring for "slope" (intelligence and growth potential) over specific banking experience as the foundation for modern fintech.
*   **"Hostages" vs. Customers:** Rampell notes that the most durable enterprise software companies (like Workday or NetSuite) often have "hostages" rather than customers because their back-end complexity makes them nearly impossible to rip and replace.

> "The goal of Ramp is to be a digital brain for organizations to allocate resources and make sure spend is not wasted." — **Eric Glyman**

> "The best companies have hostages, not customers... they are much, much harder to go rip that out." — **Alex Rampell**

> "I think that the half-life of seeing a problem to how long it takes for you to go fix it is shrinking immensely." — **Eric Glyman**
