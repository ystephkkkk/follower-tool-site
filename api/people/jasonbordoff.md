## The Hormuz shock, AI and China: the state of global energy, with Jason Bordoff

- Channel: World Economic Forum
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_G9kPgEy3aQ
- Published: 2026-07-09
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20260709.html#video-_G9kPgEy3aQ
- Speakers: Jason Bordoff (Director, Center on Global Energy Policy); Roberto Booca (Head of the Centre for Energy and Materials, World Economic Forum)

---

**TL;DR**
Despite the Strait of Hormuz closure being the largest oil supply disruption in history, global energy markets showed unexpected resilience due to shifting Chinese demand and high inventories; however, the transition to clean energy now faces critical "headwinds" caused by geopolitical fragmentation and the difficulty of de-risking dependence on Chinese supply chains.

### The Hormuz Shock and Market Resilience
* **Historical Disruption:** Jason Bordoff characterized the recent closure of the Strait of Hormuz as the "largest oil supply disruption in history," involving the loss of 20% of the world’s oil and LNG supply.
    * **Price Impact:** Contrary to "nightmare scenarios" of $150–$200 per barrel, prices peaked at $120 and eventually settled back toward $70 as flows resumed.
    * **Market Factors:** Resilience was attributed to an oversupplied market, aggressive inventory draws, and a significant 4-million-barrel-per-day drop in Chinese imports.
* **The "Swing Consumer" Concept:** China may have emerged as a "swing consumer," capable of dramatically shifting consumption and import levels in response to shocks, though Bordoff notes this may have been a unique result of China pausing its strategic stock-filling.
* **Fragile Recovery:** Despite current price stability, the 60-day nuclear deal involving Iran and the U.S. remains tenuous. Risks persist regarding physical infrastructure damage and the confidence of shippers to return to the Strait.

### The "China Dilemma" in Clean Energy
* **Strategic Resilience:** China’s long-term strategy of electrification, domestic renewables, and maintaining large strategic buffers validated its resilience during the Hormuz crisis.
* **The Dependency Risk:** China currently maintains a 70% to 90% dominant position in clean energy kit manufacturing (solar panels, batteries, and critical minerals).
* **"Sand in the Gears":** Bordoff warned that if Western economies label all Chinese-made components as "risks" and attempt to decouple entirely, it will throw a "very large amount of sand into the gears of the clean energy transition."
    * **De-risking vs. Decoupling:** Experts emphasized the need to "de-risk" (diversify) rather than "decouple" (completely separate), as the latter is extraordinarily difficult and expensive.
    * **Technology Transfer:** Roberto Bocca noted that future transitions may require accepting Chinese investment in Europe or North America to facilitate technology transfer and job creation.

### The Role of AI and Electrification
* **The Age of Electrification:** Electricity use is growing three to four times faster than total energy use.
    * **Primary Drivers:** While data centers are a significant factor, Roberto Bocca highlighted that **cooling and air conditioning** are actually the biggest sources of growth for electrification globally.
* **AI as a "Friend" to Transition:** Bordoff argued that AI is an asset rather than a foe for the energy transition.
    * **Grid Optimization:** AI can optimize existing grid capacity by 30% or more through predictive maintenance and weather modeling, reducing the immediate need for massive new infrastructure.
    * **Supply/Demand Management:** AI enables better "demand response" and efficiency, addressing the "soft path" of energy—getting more service from the energy we already use.

### Global Investment Disparities
* **The Capital Gap:** Roberto Bocca highlighted a major imbalance in the Energy Transition Index; 75% of clean energy capital is concentrated in China and developed markets.
* **Developing Market Stagnation:** Only 25% of capital reaches large developing markets in Africa, Southeast Asia, and Latin America, despite these being the areas where future energy demand will be highest.
* **Energy Security Trade-offs:** In some regions, like China, "coal-to-chemicals" remains a strategy for energy security, illustrating that security goals and decarbonization goals are not always perfectly aligned.

## The Iran oil shock: will it force the world to re-think the future of energy?

- Channel: World Economic Forum
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GsUJZY93qSI
- Published: 2026-04-28
- Briefing: https://ystephkkkk.github.io/follower-tool-site/20260428.html#video-GsUJZY93qSI
- Speakers: Jason Bordoff (Center on Global Energy Policy)

---

**TL;DR**
The 2026 oil shock, triggered by the Iran war and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, represents the largest energy supply disruption in history, forcing a global shift away from energy interdependence toward domestic "autarky" and accelerated electrification.

### The Magnitude of the 2026 Oil Shock
*   **Historic Supply Disruption:** Somewhere between 13 to 15 million barrels of oil per day—approximately 10% to 15% of global supply—are currently offline due to the closure of the Strait of Hormuz.
    *   This disruption is significantly larger than the 1973 Arab oil embargo (7%) or the Gulf War (6%).
*   **Infrastructure Vulnerability:** While physical infrastructure damage has been limited so far (mostly affecting Qatari natural gas), a resolution is not immediate. 
    *   **Recovery Timeline:** Even if the Strait opened today, it would take weeks to months to restore supply due to production "shut-ins" and maritime insurance/safety concerns.
    *   **The Insurance Premium:** Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, which bypasses the Strait, serves as a primary example of "energy insurance" that countries will now be more willing to pay for.
*   **Regional Disparity:** The shock is hitting emerging markets in Southeast Asia and lower-income nations like Bangladesh and Pakistan hardest, causing physical fuel shortages and school closures. The U.S. remains somewhat more insulated due to its high domestic production levels.

### The Allure of "Energy Autarky"
*   **Strategic Liability:** Historically, energy security was built on interconnection and global trade. In the current fragmented geopolitical landscape, countries now view integration as a risk rather than a source of resilience.
*   **The Impulse for Self-Sufficiency:** Nations are trending toward "autarky"—the desire to produce all energy within their own borders to avoid Middle Eastern geopolitical turmoil.
    *   **Economic Cost:** Trade generally lowers costs; moving toward a "fortress approach" is significantly more expensive and requires massive investment in redundant infrastructure.
*   **Swapping Dependencies:** Transitioning to domestic clean energy reduces oil dependence but creates new vulnerabilities.
    *   **China’s Dominance:** Approximately 70% to 90% of the supply chain for clean energy (batteries, solar panels, and critical minerals) is controlled by China.

### National Security as a Catalyst for Energy Transition
*   **The Situation Room Effect:** Jason Bordoff notes that national security concerns often motivate government action more effectively than climate change warnings. The current crisis may drive a faster transition than environmental policy alone.
*   **Electrification vs. Addition:** Bordoff clarifies that the world has historically engaged in "energy addition" (adding renewables while still increasing fossil fuel use) rather than a true "transition."
    *   **Displacement:** For a real transition to occur, clean energy must grow fast enough to displace the 80% of the energy mix currently held by fossil fuels—a figure that has remained stagnant for decades.
*   **Emerging Market Shifts:** High oil prices are driving a surge in electric vehicle (EV) adoption in developing nations; for example, Chinese automaker BYD is seeing record sales in Brazil and the Philippines as consumers flee high pump prices.

### Long-Term Structural Challenges
*   **Short-Termism in Policy:** Politicians often react to oil shocks with temporary fixes (releasing strategic reserves or waiving taxes) rather than long-term structural changes. 
    *   **The Urgency Gap:** Once the immediate crisis fades, the political will to fund expensive energy insurance typically evaporates.
*   **AI and Data Centers:** The "age of electrification" is being strained by the energy requirements of AI and data centers. 
    *   **Investment Risk:** Regions like the UAE and Saudi Arabia were positioning themselves as hubs for data centers, but the current conflict may alter the "risk profile" for tech firms looking to place physical infrastructure in the Gulf.
*   **The Hard-to-Abate Sectors:** While cars are easily electrified, Bordoff warns that steel and cement production—which account for double the emissions of all the world’s cars combined—require "molecules" rather than just "electrons," making them harder to secure during an oil and gas shock.
