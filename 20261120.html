TL;DR: Altimeter AI Partner Apoorv Agrawal breaks down the historic scale of the AI supercycle—where Big Tech CapEx has hit $860 billion—and explains why Anthropic’s S-1 reveals one of the most capital-efficient, fastest-scaling businesses in history, underpinned by a 1.5T–2T IPO valuation (11–14x forward revenue) and a "Four Seasons of AI" framework led by Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceX, and Google.

The AI Supercycle & Early-Inning Adoption

  • Historic Capital Expenditure: Big Tech AI CapEx has reached ~$860 billion this year, having more than tripled over the last two years to rival U.S. defense spending. As a percentage of GDP, this spend already exceeds every major infrastructure boom of the past 100 years (railroads, oil & gas, telco, shale).
  • The AI Stack vs. Cloud Economy: While the traditional cloud economy generates ~$600B in application/software revenue supported by ~$300B in infra and ~$80B in semis, the AI economy currently generates ~$140B–$150B at the model/app layer on top of a much larger semiconductor base. However, over the last 2–3 years, the AI economy has grown 6x overall while the apps and models layer has grown ~100x.
  • Shift to Agentic AI: The market has transitioned from "Phase 1" (chatbots/knowledge retrieval, launched with ChatGPT in Nov 2022, making up ~80% of early use cases) to "Phase 2" (agentic AI, long-running agents, and "swarms of agents" for coding, sales, marketing, support, and investment teams, now ~80% of agentic use cases).
  • Widening Adoption Gaps ("A Tale of Two Cities"):
    • Enterprise: OpenAI data shows its top 10% of customers grew usage 17x over the past 1–1.5 years, compared to 2x for the median customer.
    • Individual: Cursor data shows the top 10% of users consume two-thirds of all tokens.
    • Headroom: Fortune 500 customers spending over $100,000 annually on Anthropic report that it represents less than 0.1% of their total expenses, signaling massive runway as adoption moves from 0.1% toward 1%.

Anthropic’s S-1 & IPO Valuation

  • Unprecedented Scale & Growth: Anthropic has grown 10x every year for the last three years, hitting ~$17 billion in Q3 revenue—the fastest scale-up in history.
  • Diversified Enterprise Base: The company has ~12,000 customers paying over $100,000/year and ~1,900 customers paying over $1 million/year, with 900% net revenue retention and minimal customer concentration.
  • Capital Efficiency & Margins: Anthropic burned less than $10 billion in cash to reach its ~$17B revenue run rate. The cost to serve $1 of revenue has dropped from $0.88 of compute to $0.15, yielding ~85% margins.
  • Compute Monetization: Through heterogeneous compute orchestration (TPUs, Trainium, Nvidia GPUs), Anthropic’s compute monetization has risen from ~$16/watt to ~$60/watt.
  • Valuation Thesis: At the rumored $1.5T–$2T IPO range, Anthropic trades at 11x–14x forward GAAP revenue—a reasonable multiple compared to current market comps.

The "Four Seasons of AI" & Evergreen Open Source

  • OpenAI: Now at over 1.2 billion ChatGPT users, OpenAI ended September at a ~$50B revenue run rate (beating year-end guidance). Following a "code red" earlier in the year, it shipped frontier models (Astra) and products (Codex, ChatGPT Work) while upgrading its commercial leadership (Dolly Rojik, Brian McCarthy, Chad Peets).
  • SpaceX (AI Infrastructure): SpaceX’s ~$60B revenue run rate is now ~50% AI infrastructure (SI unit), ~33% Starlink, and the remainder launch. Its SI unit added over 20% relative market share in Q3.
    • Spot Compute Model: Unlike neoclouds (CoreWeave, Nebius) that lease long-term (1–3 years) at $10–$20/watt, SpaceX rents compute at spot prices ($25–$50/watt, currently at the $50/watt high end), allowing it to recoup CapEx in under a year (vs. 2–3 years for neoclouds).
    • Scale: Estimated to end this year at ~3 GW of compute, guiding to ~13 GW by the end of next year.
  • Google ("A Tale of Two Cities"): While Google Search (~$250B run rate) is growing at 12%, Google Cloud—which houses its TPU business—is at a $120B revenue run rate growing 103% YoY (8x faster than Search), serving customers like Anthropic and Oracle.
  • Open Source (The Evergreen Fifth Season): Expected to capture 20%–30% of all AI spend, driven by AI-native startups (e.g., Cursor, Instinct, Decagon, Harvey) and enterprises seeking control, lower inference costs, and data sovereignty.
    • Open-source frontier models consistently lag closed frontier models by ~6 months.
    • Value accrues at the inference/cloud layer via platforms like Baseten, Fireworks, and Modal (e.g., OpenAI recently partnered with Baseten so enterprise customers can burn down OpenAI credits on open-source models).

Data Center Bottlenecks & Market Outlook

  • Power & Permitting Constraints: The U.S. is expected to end this year at 35–40 GW of compute capacity online. While 40–50 GW of additions were forecasted for next year, permitting and regulation will likely cause a 10–15 GW shortfall.
  • Winners vs. Losers: Hyperscalers holding large capacity pools benefit as bottlenecks, though their expansion ambitions face risk. Conversely, smaller neoclouds and startups "17th in line" for capacity face existential risk.
  • Who Wins the Stack: While "everyone is going to win" (chips, data centers, clouds, models), it has so far been a "game of emperors" favoring the largest balance sheets. Over time, improving compute efficiency will allow more value to accrue to the application and startup layers.

TL;DR

The resignation of Nvidia’s Jensen Huang and a Democratic sweep in the midterm elections have triggered a market correction, effectively dissolving Nvidia’s circularly financed "virtual holding company." With Nvidia no longer backstopping the AI stack, AI is no longer a single monolithic trade, bringing stock picking and fundamental valuation back to the forefront.

Market Correction & The "Jensen Unwind"

  • Market Action: From Friday through Thursday (Nov 19), the NASDAQ fell ~4%. The market declined Monday and Tuesday, staged a small rally Wednesday, and saw only a brief, fading pop on Thursday following the Anthropic IPO.
  • Political & Leadership Catalysts: A Democratic sweep of both houses in the midterms introduced the risk of an AI moratorium or data center construction slowdown. Combined with Jensen Huang’s resignation, these events dwarf all other market news.
  • S&P 500 AI Concentration:
    • Infotech represents 40% of the S&P 500; adding tech-adjacent giants like Google and Amazon brings it to 50%.
    • Including utilities, large banks, asset managers/alternative asset managers funding data centers, and industrials providing construction or power (e.g., Eaton, Quanta, GE Vernova) pushes AI-exposed companies to 65% of the S&P 500.
  • Circular Financing & Dissolution: Under Huang, Nvidia operated as a "virtual vertical holding company" by taking equity stakes or issuing loans to customers who used that capital to buy Nvidia chips—including Anthropic ($20B), OpenAI ($30B), SpaceX ($20B), CoreWeave ($2.5B), and Nebius ($2B).
    • Huang's resignation dissolves this structure. While tech leaders want him back, Treasury Secretary Bessent has appointed himself watchdog, making a front-door return to Nvidia unlikely.
    • Banks, private credit, private equity, and shareholders must now evaluate these AI companies on their own standalone merits (cash flow, balance sheets, leadership, and financing).

Nvidia’s Earnings & Forward Uncertainty

  • Q3 Results (Backward-Looking): Nvidia reported EPS of $2.68 (+111% YoY) and revenue of $116B (+103% YoY).
  • Guidance & Stock Reaction: Management guided next quarter’s revenue to $130B, explicitly excluding "compute revenue from China." The stock rose ~3% after hours, opened up Wednesday, and closed slightly down.
  • Opacity & Red Flags:
    • Roughly 20% of Nvidia's revenue reportedly came from shipments to "dicey Asian entities" that were likely illegal and could drop to zero.
    • It remains unclear whether Nvidia’s guidance excludes only direct sales to China or also sales to Asian intermediaries funneling chips into China.
    • Eisman argues management made a mistake by issuing guidance at all before unwinding circular financing and quantifying lost revenue.

How to Think About Quarterly Earnings

  • Earnings Cadence: Begins ~15 days after quarter-end, opening with the banks and closing ~6 weeks later with Nvidia.
  • Shifting Sector Metrics:
    • *Banks:* Loan growth, net interest margin (NIM) changes, operating efficiency, and credit quality.
    • *Tech/AI:* Shifted from revenue, earnings, and backlog to CapEx, cash flow, and whether CapEx is funded internally or via capital markets.
    • *Software (SaaS):* Shifted from seat growth and pricing trends to any signs that AI is slowing growth rates.
  • Thesis Checking & Guidance: Earnings serve as a data-point check (e.g., 16% vs. 14% growth against a 15% thesis). Because companies "under-promise and over-deliver" and can massage accounting within limits, missing guidance signals a potential fundamental issue.
  • The "Steve Eisman Drama Scale" (1–10, Nvidia at 11): For low-drama stocks like Coca-Cola, backward-looking numbers suffice. When sector narratives shift rapidly (like AI today, or Cisco’s recent strong report), backward-looking earnings tell investors very little about future quarters.

Retail & Consumer Earnings: Housing Drag vs. Turnarounds

  • Home Depot: Facing the worst existing home sales market in decades ("a bad macro trumps a good company"). Reported EPS of $3.86 (+3% YoY) and same-store sales comp of +2.2% (50 bps beat), but guided Q4 comps to just 1% (below consensus), sending the stock down Tuesday (roughly flat YTD).
  • Lowe’s: Reported EPS of $3.10 (+1% YoY) and same-store sales comp of +1.8% (160 bps beat, but still poor).
  • Target: Turnaround efforts are succeeding, with EPS of $2.65 (+49% YoY) and same-store sales growth of +5.6% (well above expectations).
  • Walmart: Reported EPS of $0.65 (+5% YoY, a $0.01 beat) and net sales growth of +4.4%. Trading as a "high-class safety stock" amid the post-September 30 AI reversal, shares have climbed from $105 to $125.

The Anthropic IPO & Mailbag

  • Anthropic IPO: Went public Thursday (Nov 19) to an anti-climactic reception. Eisman finds the stock uncompelling and is uninvolved due to:
    • *Slowing Growth:* Sequential revenue growth decelerated from 145% in Q2 to 45% in Q3, attributed to the end of "token maxing."
    • *Lack of Moats:* Easy for customers to switch models, forcing Anthropic to cut prices by 50% in October to defend market share against cheaper open-weight models.
    • *Balance Sheet & Cost Risks:* Anthropic has $900B in commitments, meaning it will have to raise expensive debt (a junk credit rating is possible) while spending heavily to keep its frontier models competitive.
  • Mailbag 1 — Correction vs. Bear Market Positioning: A correction lasts 1–2 years; a true bear market lasts years (none since the 1970s). The US economy is too dynamic and banks are too well-capitalized for a long-term bear market.
    • *Long-term investors:* Dollar-cost average into the S&P 500 index and ignore the noise.
    • *Traders:* Rotate into a healthcare index, a staples index, and short-term Treasuries.
  • Mailbag 2 — Identifying "Liar Loans" in 2008: Moody’s securitization database revealed that starting in 2006, ~50% of all securitized subprime loans were "low doc" or "no doc" loans. Underwriters actively cajoled borrowers into overstating income (e.g., telling a $40,000 earner they needed to state $50,000 to qualify), though the exact percentage of liar loans within low-doc pools remains unknown.

TL;DR

Charisma is a learnable skill—not an innate trait—rooted in balancing warmth and competence (which together drive 82% of first impressions) to trigger oxytocin and dopamine in others. By using purposeful body language, vocal confidence, playful conversational hooks, and strategic mirroring, leaders and professionals can build immediate trust and influence without faking authenticity.

The Science of Charisma: Warmth, Competence, and Brain Chemistry

  • Warmth as a Lubricant for Competence: Vanessa Van Edwards notes that warmth and competence make up 82% of our impressions of people. Smart professionals often over-index on competence, which backfires because competence without warmth breeds suspicion.
    • Warmth is not "bubbly extroversion" or fake smiling; it shows up as vulnerability, humor, sarcasm, or even stumbling with words (e.g., Elon Musk’s unpolished vulnerability or Mark Zuckerberg’s everyday-guy demeanor).
  • Rejecting "Just Be Yourself": Both Sam Parr and Vanessa agree that "authenticity" and "just be yourself" are unhelpful advice for people trying to improve. Instead, charisma is about honing your own distinct flavor of communication.
  • The First-Minute Rule: First impressions are nearly irreversible; your first minute matters more than your first 30 minutes because the primitive brain is assessing friend vs. foe.
    • Oxytocin (Connection): Triggered in the first 3–5 seconds of eye contact, visible open hands (signaling no hidden weapons), and physical touch. A handshake produces roughly 3 hours' worth of eye-contact oxytocin.
    • Dopamine (Motivation & Energy): Triggered verbally by breaking social scripts and creating "information gaps" (e.g., Sam opening the show with a magic trick his daughter taught him, or Howard Stern asking, "Want to hear the weirdest thing that ever happened to me?"). Vanessa advises using information hooks every 1–2 minutes when speaking.

Mastering Conversation: Hooks, Small Talk, and Playfulness

  • Entertainers vs. Appreciators: Vanessa challenges Dale Carnegie’s advice to just ask lots of follow-up questions. While *Entertainers* love being the center of attention and answering questions, most VIPs are *Appreciators* who spend all day talking and want to be entertained or hear your stories.
    • Sam shares a story of dining with a ~$30 billion AI pioneer who spent the entire dinner asking Sam about his basic AI use cases because he was tired of being interviewed himself.
  • Dr. Dan McAdams’s Three Levels of Connection:
    • Level 1 (General Traits): Basic facts ("What do you do?"). Vanessa recommends swapping this for, *"Working on anything exciting these days?"*—a question she honed over 20 years.
    • Level 2 (Personal Concerns): Motivations, values, and goals. Transition here quickly by asking about goals tied to time boxes (quarters, New Year's, birthdays) or what someone is learning (e.g., books).
    • Level 3 (Self-Narrative): The story someone tells themselves about themselves.
  • Playfulness and Trigger Words: Keep a mental list of 20–50 "trigger words" (topics you enjoy) to steer conversations. Halting boring small talk with playful guessing games (e.g., guessing where someone is going on vacation or joking about baby names) triggers dopamine for both parties without requiring loud extroversion.
  • Being "Chalant": Sam and Vanessa emphasize that enthusiasm—the physical display of passion—is contagious. Citing a dating study by Monica Moore, Vanessa notes that availability signals make people more attractive than physical looks alone, both romantically and professionally.

Vocal Confidence, Language, and Leadership

  • Contagious Emotions & "Calm Assertive" Leadership: Vanessa cites an fMRI study where participants smelled sweat from skydivers and "caught" their fear in their amygdala. Sam connects this to his experience at age 21 training an 80-pound pit bull ("Jake's dog"), where he learned that leaders must be *calm assertive* rather than high-energy reactive.
  • Eliminating Uptalk: Using an accidental question inflection at the end of statements (e.g., stating a $10,000 price like a question) signals nervousness and deception, switching the listener's brain from listening to scrutinizing.
    • Men often do this in the first 10 seconds of a call/meeting while trying to sound warm; women often do it during the most nerve-wracking parts of a pitch.
  • Maximum Resonance Point: Every voice has a 10-to-12-note range. Speaking on the out-breath at the lower, relaxed end of your range projects confidence—without overcorrecting to an unnatural extreme.
  • Purposeful Language (Behavior Cues): In a prisoner’s dilemma study, groups told they were playing the "Wall Street game" shared an average of 1/3 of their profits, while groups told they were playing the "Community game" shared 2/3. Leaders should audit their sent emails and intentionally use achievement words (*win, succeed, master*) for performance days and collaborative words for brainstorming days.

Reading People, Hiring, and Mirroring VIPs

  • Hiring "Ambitious Heroes": Vanessa looks for candidates whose self-narrative is overcoming hurdles through grit.
    • Behavioral & Hypothetical Questions: Ask about the *most recent* hurdle they overcame (so it’s unrehearsed) or give them a real-time logistical hypothetical.
    • The Luck Test: Sam asks candidates, *"Do you feel lucky?"* Vanessa confirms research shows people who feel unlucky feel out of control and literally spot fewer opportunities.
    • The Character Question: From 10 years of research (Question #7 of 9 in her book), Vanessa asks: *"What book, movie, or TV character is most like you?"*—and warns never to argue with their answer, as it reveals how they predictably operate.
  • Finding Your Flavor: Rather than mimicking one celebrity, watch the top 10–20 TED Talks to see which delivery style resonates with you.
  • Mirroring VIPs: To charm high-status individuals without seeming like a clown (and with good, non-manipulative intent), mirror them on three planes:
    • Non-verbally: Match their animation, gestures, and pause lengths (e.g., matching a "2-second pauser").
    • Verbally: Adopt their exact lexicon (e.g., saying "clients" instead of "customers" if they do).
    • Tonally: Match their volume and speaking speed.

The Fall of Jensen Huang and Nvidia's "Exoskeleton"

  • The Resignation: Jensen Huang resigned as CEO of Nvidia on Thursday, November 12, 2026, ending a 33-year tenure that began when he co-founded the company on April 5, 1993.
  • Nominal vs. True Cause:
    • Nominally, Huang stepped down due to affairs with at least two Nvidia employees and Alice Huang, an executive at Singaporean data center firm MegaSpeed (who is alleged to be a Chinese spy).
    • Fundamentally, Huang’s exit was caused by his refusal to accept that Nvidia was now viewed by Washington as a defense contractor.
  • The "Exoskeleton" Structure: Nvidia was uniquely organized around one man, operating like a startup with very little middle management, 60 direct reports to Huang, and no succession plan. While this enabled rapid, aggressive bets, it left no internal checks on Huang's behavior or compliance.
  • The Asian Supply Chain: Huang acted as a "one-person coordination function" for the Asian supply chain, famously eating and drinking across Asia to convince reluctant suppliers to commit capacity to Nvidia’s yearly release cycle.

The Erosion of Nvidia's Four Moats

  • Historical Dominance: Nvidia’s rise was built on the 2001 GeForce 3 (programmable pixel shaders), the 2006 launch of CUDA (an API that almost killed the company before becoming its core platform), and the 2019 acquisition of Mellanox (enabling scale-out systems). Following ChatGPT’s 2022 launch, Nvidia commanded margins exceeding 70%.
  • Moat 1 — CUDA (Eroding):
    • Developer energy has moved up the stack to LLM APIs, making CUDA less relevant for most companies.
    • Anthropic proved leading LLMs can eliminate Nvidia dependency by learning to run on Amazon’s Trainium chips and Google’s TPUs.
    • LLMs themselves are so effective at writing software that any software-based moat will inevitably dry up.
  • Moat 2 — Networking & Systems (Challenged): While more durable than CUDA, Nvidia's systems approach is increasingly challenged by Google and Anthropic, and relies heavily on neoclouds that struggle with power access and political permission.
  • Moat 3 — Supply Chain Mastery (Tenuous): Huang bragged that he never signed a contract with TSMC over their three-decade partnership, relying instead on a handshake and mutual trust. Without Huang, that commitment is in question.
  • Moat 4 — Organizational Speed (Collapsed): With Huang gone, the single-founder exoskeleton that allowed Nvidia to outmaneuver rivals is poised to collapse.

The AI Bubble (2025–2027) and Nvidia's "Risky Business"

  • Shifting Funding Mechanisms: The AI infrastructure build-out progressed from hyperscaler free cash flow, to debt (second half of 2025), and now to equity (starting with Google in summer 2026).
  • The Neocloud Hedge: To avoid complete dependence on hyperscalers (who are building their own chips), Nvidia aggressively sold to "neoclouds" (independent GPU renters).
  • Circular Financing: Because neoclouds face expensive debt and increasingly worthless equity, Nvidia used its own cash flow and credit rating to backstop and fund its own customers.
  • The $1 Trillion Threshold: Industry-wide AI revenue of roughly $1 trillion needs to materialize sooner rather than later. If it falls short, hyperscalers will prioritize their own compute, and Nvidia’s neocloud hedge will turn into a millstone, flooding the market with distressed assets.

AI as "Normal Technology" vs. China Hawks and Doomers

  • The Policy Battle: Huang argued AI was "normal technology" with engineering-solvable safety issues, putting him at odds with both AI "doomers" (e.g., Anthropic) and "China hawks," who viewed him as public enemy number one.
  • The China Dilemma: China traditionally accounted for roughly 25% of Nvidia’s sales. When the Biden administration imposed chip controls in 2022, Nvidia immediately released the A800 (an A100 with a third of its interconnects disabled), establishing a pattern of abiding by the letter—never the spirit—of the law.
  • The "Great Firewall" Playbook: China is currently banning imports of Nvidia’s two-generations-behind H20 chips (after the Trump administration banned and then reversed the ban). By cutting off access to Nvidia, China is forcing domestic demand to use inferior local alternatives—the exact strategy used with the Great Firewall to build Chinese internet giants—which will eventually enable a competing Chinese semiconductor ecosystem.

The End of Tech Company Independence (1968–2026)

  • The Intel Precedent: Since Robert Noyce founded Intel in 1968 (backed by Arthur Rock), tech companies operated on the premise that the world was their customer, volume drove progress, and executives—not the government—defined their products.
  • The Banking Standard: In a *Financial Times* interview, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent demanded answers from Nvidia’s board and outside counsel within 10 days and called for a "shake-up" with an external CEO.
  • Compliance Over Ignorance: Bessent criticized Howard Lutnick’s Commerce Department for delaying adding MegaSpeed to the U.S. blacklist, but argued Nvidia should be held to the same standard as a bank: ignorance is not a defense, and companies must put "boots on the ground" to enforce the spirit of U.S. sanctions.

Asia Trip, Trilateral Summit & Global Security

  • First-Ever US-Russia-China Trilateral Summit: President Trump met with President Xi Jinping and President Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen, China, focusing on nuclear de-escalation among the three nations, which hold 92% of the world's nuclear weapons (Trump noted the U.S. has ~4,000, Russia is second, and China has 600).

    • The three nations also agreed to increase oil, gasoline, and diesel production to lower global prices.

Trade Deals & International Partnerships

  • ASEAN & Philippines ($17B+ in Deals): Addressing 14 ASEAN nations in Manila, Trump secured over $17 billion in commercial deals, including Philippine Airlines purchasing 20 Boeing 787 Dreamliners with General Electric engines (supporting 47,000 U.S. jobs).

    • Starting January 1, the Philippines will buy 100% of its agricultural products and 100% of its energy from the U.S., alongside over $2 billion in U.S. military equipment, under a framework deal slashing Philippine tariffs.

TL;DR

David Heinemeier Hansson (DHH) argues that the agent era has reopened the operating system market for the first time in 25 years, making Linux—whose terminal, config files, and detailed error messages are ideal for AI—the natural successor to macOS and Windows. Through his agent-first Linux distribution, Omarchy, DHH demonstrates how tiny teams can now rival tech giants, while taste, craft, and the obsession over the "final 2%" remain the true differentiators.

The Agent-First OS Opportunity: Why Linux Wins Now

  • The End of Mouse-and-Click OSs: DHH, who spent 30 years building software (Basecamp v5, Ruby on Rails v8, Hey.com) and used Mac since OS X Beta 4 in 2001, switched to Ubuntu 2.5 years ago over Apple’s 30% App Store cut and developer hostility. He argues macOS and Windows are fundamentally unfit for agents, whereas Linux’s historic weaknesses—heavy terminal use, text config files, and verbose error messages—are the exact interfaces agents love.
  • Omarchy’s Rapid Rise: Built part-time in ~2.5 months by DHH and Ryan Hughes, Omarchy is designed to be modified live by prompting. DHH has not written a single line of code by hand in 5–6 months, instead generating hundreds of thousands of lines via agents. The Omarchy Foundation has raised ~$23M in tokens and donations.
  • Breaking Platform Lock-In:
    • Desktop & Mobile: Apple risks becoming the "AOL of the agent era" with walled gardens and features like Genmoji. DHH’s motto is *"first we take Manhattan (desktop), then we take Berlin (mobile)."*
    • Eradicating App Moats: An Omarchy community member replicated Adobe Lightroom in two weeks—a feat that took Adobe ~15 years and tens of millions of dollars. DHH no longer uses Word or Photoshop, relying on agents to draft contracts, generate PDFs, sign documents, and edit photos.
    • Anish Acharya’s Take: Five years ago, building a third mobile ecosystem was certain to fail due to app availability; today, a modest token budget can replicate the top 10,000 apps, or agents can simply perform the work natively without the app.

The Economics of Software, Speed, and the Final 2%

  • Token Velocity & Model Leaps: Current models run at ~50 tokens/sec (120 on ultra-fast), but Fireworks recently demonstrated 1,500 tokens/sec—generating a playable Tetris game in 4 seconds and mutating a side-scroller shooter live. During Omarchy’s development, models jumped from Opus 48 to Saul to Opus 55.
  • Negligible Cost: Despite having the means for unlimited compute, DHH only uses three Claude subscriptions—and mostly relies on a single $200 20x subscription, maxing it out only two weeks out of the last three months.
  • Craftsmanship & Taste Still Matter: Martin Casado noted that great software—like *StarCraft* receiving a Ghost balance patch after 20 years—is defined by long-tail design choices rather than raw lines of code. DHH agreed, explaining he is working 16-hour days because agents eliminate the friction of fixing the "final 2%," where he gets his dopamine.
  • Hyperdrive Without Headcount: Basecamp/37signals has ~60 employees; DHH never wanted 6,000 or 60,000. Agents provide the productive capacity of 60,000 people with zero HR or coordination overhead—echoing Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke’s description of returning to a 7,000-person office as "coming out of hyperdrive."

Superintelligence, Frontier Labs vs. Open Weights

  • Software vs. Hardware Timelines: DHH believes we already have superintelligence for software development. However, Martin Casado’s personal AGI test—giving models a $20,000 budget to order parts and build an autonomous, walkable robot—shows physical embodiment is far behind (his setup, Groqbot, mostly orders home automation gear). Visiting Unitree in China two weeks prior, DHH found robotics engineers projecting 4–10 years for embodied breakthroughs, contrasting with Silicon Valley’s 1-year timelines.
  • Avoiding a Frontier Duopoly: Drawing parallels to the 1990s encryption wars and Google/Meta capturing 99% of online ad value, DHH warned against a commercial or government-enforced duopoly between top labs (Anthropic and OpenAI).
  • The Role of Open Weights: DHH cheers on US frontier labs (including xAI and Google) while stressing that open-weight models (e.g., GLM 53, DeepSeek Flash, which he estimates are ~6–7 months behind) keep pricing honest. Local models like Qwen 3 8 Flash/Next now run usefully on a single-digit-thousand-dollar DGX Spark on a desk.

The Return of Hacker, Demo, and Pseudo-Anonymous Culture

  • Industry Veterans & Empathy: Martin Casado and Anish Acharya highlighted how stalwarts like DHH, Eric Raymond, John Carmack, Tim Sweeney, and Mitchell Hashimoto are guiding the industry. While DHH expressed empathy for anxious programmers—comparing them to 1970s Detroit auto workers and English Luddites—he insisted there is no going back.
  • Pseudo-Anonymous Builders: Agents are redemocratizing software, bringing back 1990s hacker culture. The Omarchy core team and Foundation sponsor pseudo-anonymous contributors like Outfoxed (creator of QuickShell), Handcore, 0x0, and MiasLab, connected largely via X.
  • 90s Demo & BBS Nostalgia: Rejecting Jony Ive’s "aluminum-polished" aesthetic that stripped character from computers, Omarchy embraces 1995 demo-party vibes with a 40-effect ASCII screensaver and ASCII loading animations. DHH reminisced about running an illegal 3-line BBS out of his bedroom at age 14 on a US Robotics 33.6k modem, arguing computers should feel mysterious and cool again.

Predictions & Forward Calls
